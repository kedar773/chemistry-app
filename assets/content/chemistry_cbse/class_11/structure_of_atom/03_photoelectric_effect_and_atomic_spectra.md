## 1.1 The Photoelectric Effect

### Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board & Competitive Frequency)

### NCERT Definition & Historical Background
In 1887, Heinrich Hertz performed the pioneering experiment in which electrons (initially called photoelectrons) were ejected when certain metals (such as Potassium, Rubidium, Caesium) were exposed to a beam of light. Albert Einstein provided the complete quantum-mechanical explanation in 1905 using Max Planck's Quantum Hypothesis, for which he was awarded the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics.

```
                    Incident Radiation (hν)
                         \       /
                          \     /
                           \   /
                            v v
  ======================================================
  [ - - - Metal Surface (Binding Energy / Work Function: W₀) - - - ]
  ======================================================
                            |
                            v (if hν > W₀)
                     Ejected Electron (e⁻)
                     Kinetic Energy: K.E. = 1/2 m v²
```

### Key Experimental Observations
1. **Instantaneous Emission:** Ejection of photoelectrons begins the exact instant the light beam strikes the surface; there is no measurable time lag ($< 10^{-9}\text{ s}$).
2. **Threshold Frequency ($\nu_0$):** For each metal, there exists a characteristic minimum frequency of incident radiation below which no photoelectrons are ejected, regardless of the intensity or duration of illumination.
3. **Dependence of Kinetic Energy:** The maximum kinetic energy ($K.E._{\text{max}}$) of the emitted photoelectrons is directly proportional to the frequency ($\nu$) of the incident radiation and is independent of the light's intensity.
4. **Dependence of Photoelectric Current:** The number of photoelectrons emitted per unit time (photoelectric current) is directly proportional to the intensity of the incident radiation, provided $\nu > \nu_0$.

### Why Classical Wave Theory Failed
* **Classical Prediction for Kinetic Energy:** Classical electromagnetism treats light as a continuous wave where intensity is proportional to the square of wave amplitude. According to this view, increasing the intensity should deposit more energy into the electrons, thus increasing their kinetic energy.  
  **Experimental Reality:** Intensity only increases the number of emitted electrons, never their individual maximum kinetic energy.
* **Classical Prediction for Time Lag:** The continuous wavefront delivers energy spread across millions of surface atoms. Each electron would theoretically need hours or days to accumulate enough energy to overcome the surface binding potential.  
  **Experimental Reality:** Ejection is virtually instantaneous ($10^{-9}\text{ s}$).
* **Classical Prediction for Threshold Frequency:** Any frequency of light should eventually eject electrons if the intensity is high enough or the exposure long enough.  
  **Experimental Reality:** Below $\nu_0$, no emission occurs even with ultra-intense beams.

## 1.2 Dual Nature of Electromagnetic Radiation

### Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (High Conceptual Frequency)

Electromagnetic radiation exhibits wave-particle duality:

```
                      ELECTROMAGNETIC RADIATION
                                   |
         +-------------------------+-------------------------+
         |                                                   |
    Wave Nature                                        Particle Nature
  (Continuous Wavefront)                             (Quantized Photons)
         |                                                   |
  • Diffraction                                      • Black Body Radiation
  • Interference                                     • Photoelectric Effect
  • Maxwell's Field Equations                        • Compton Effect
  • Characteristic: λ, ν                             • Characteristic: E = hν, p = h/λ
```

### Microscopic Interpretation of a Photon
1. Radiation is propagated, absorbed, and emitted in discrete energy packets called **quanta** (or **photons** in the case of light).
2. Energy of a single photon:
   

$$
E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

3. All photons of light of a particular frequency have the same energy and momentum, irrespective of intensity.
4. An increase in the intensity of light of a given frequency increases only the photon flux (number of photons passing through unit area per second), not the individual photon energy.
5. Photons are electrically neutral and cannot be deflected by magnetic or electric fields.
6. In a photon-particle collision (e.g., photon-electron collision), total energy and total momentum are conserved, but the total number of photons may not be conserved (a photon may be completely absorbed).

## 1.3 Line Spectrum of Hydrogen & The Bohr Transition Model

### Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board & Competitive Frequency)

### Emission vs. Absorption Spectra
* **Continuous Spectrum:** When white light passes through a prism, it refracts into a continuous band of colors without sharp boundaries (e.g., rainbow).
* **Line Emission Spectrum:** Produced when gas or vapor atoms at low pressure are excited (by thermal heating or electric discharge). The emitted radiation consists of isolated bright lines against a dark background, corresponding to specific emitted wavelengths. Every element possesses a unique atomic fingerprint spectrum.
* **Absorption Spectrum:** Produced when continuous white light passes through an unexcited atomic gas. Dark lines appear at the exact wavelengths where the substance absorbs radiation. These dark lines match the emission lines of that specific substance.

## 2.1 Derivation of Einstein’s Photoelectric Equation

### Assumptions & Physical Basis
1. A collision between a photon and an electron is a localized, one-to-one interaction. One photon can eject at most one photoelectron.
2. Energy conservation holds strictly for each individual photon-electron interaction:
   

$$
\text{Total Energy of Incident Photon} = \text{Work Function} + \text{Maximum Kinetic Energy of Ejected Electron}
$$

### Step-by-Step Derivation

1. Let an incident photon carry energy:
   

$$
E = h\nu
$$

2. Let the minimum binding energy holding the electron inside the metal surface be the **Work Function** ($W_0$ or $\Phi$):
   

$$
W_0 = h\nu_0
$$

   where $\nu_0$ is the threshold frequency.

3. If $h\nu < h\nu_0$, the photon lacks the energy required to overcome the binding potential; no ejection occurs.

4. If $h\nu \ge h\nu_0$, excess energy is transferred to the electron as maximum kinetic energy ($K.E._{\text{max}}$):
   

$$
h\nu = W_0 + K.E._{\text{max}}
$$

5. Rearranging for maximum kinetic energy:
   

$$
K.E._{\text{max}} = h\nu - W_0
$$

   

$$
K.E._{\text{max}} = h\nu - h\nu_0 = h(\nu - \nu_0)
$$

6. Expressing $K.E._{\text{max}}$ in terms of the maximum velocity ($v_{\text{max}}$) of the electron (mass $m_e = 9.10938 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$):
   

$$
\frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\text{max}}^2 = h(\nu - \nu_0)
$$

7. Expressing in terms of wavelength ($\lambda$):
   

$$
\frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\text{max}}^2 = hc\left(\frac{1}{\lambda} - \frac{1}{\lambda_0}\right) = hc\left(\frac{\lambda_0 - \lambda}{\lambda \lambda_0}\right)
$$

   where $\lambda_0 = \frac{c}{\nu_0}$ is the threshold wavelength.

8. In terms of **Stopping Potential** ($V_0$ or $V_s$):
   The stopping potential is the minimum retarding potential required to reduce the photoelectric current to zero (stopping the fastest photoelectrons):
   

$$
K.E._{\text{max}} = e V_0
$$

   

$$
e V_0 = h\nu - W_0 \implies V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \frac{W_0}{e}
$$

$$
\boxed{K.E._{\text{max}} = \frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\text{max}}^2 = h\nu - h\nu_0 = e V_0}
$$

* **SI Units:**
  * $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$
  * $\nu, \nu_0\text{ in }\text{s}^{-1}\text{ or }\text{Hz}$
  * $W_0, K.E.\text{ in Joules (J)}$ (Note: $1\text{ eV} = 1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$)
  * $V_0\text{ in Volts (V)}$

## 2.3 Derivation of Rydberg Formula for Hydrogen Spectral Lines

### Assumptions in Bohr's Formulation
1. The electron revolves around the nucleus in specific non-radiating circular orbits (stationary states).
2. Quantization condition: Orbital angular momentum is an integral multiple of $\frac{h}{2\pi}$:
   

$$
m_e v r = \frac{nh}{2\pi} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)
$$

3. Electrostatic attraction provides the centripetal force:
   

$$
\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z e^2}{r^2} = \frac{m_e v^2}{r}
$$

4. Radiation emission occurs only when an electron jumps from a higher stationary state ($n_2$) to a lower stationary state ($n_1$).

### Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

1. **Radius of $n$-th orbit ($r_n$):**
   From Bohr's first two postulates:
   

$$
v = \frac{nh}{2\pi m_e r}
$$

   Substitute into centripetal balance equation:
   

$$
\frac{Z e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{m_e}{r}\left(\frac{n^2 h^2}{4\pi^2 m_e^2 r^2}\right) = \frac{n^2 h^2}{4\pi^2 m_e r^3}
$$

   Solving for $r$:
   

$$
r_n = \frac{\varepsilon_0 n^2 h^2}{\pi m_e Z e^2} = \frac{a_0 n^2}{Z} \quad \left(a_0 = 0.529\text{ Å} = 52.9\text{ pm}\right)
$$

2. **Total Energy of the Electron ($E_n$):**
   

$$
E_n = K.E. + P.E. = \frac{1}{2}m_e v^2 - \frac{Z e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n}
$$

   Since $\frac{1}{2}m_e v^2 = \frac{Z e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n}$:
   

$$
E_n = -\frac{Z e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n}
$$

   Substitute $r_n$:
   

$$
E_n = -\frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{8 \varepsilon_0^2 h^2}\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right)
$$

   For Hydrogen ($Z = 1$):
   

$$
E_n = -\frac{R_H'}{n^2} = -\frac{2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}}{n^2} = -\frac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2}
$$

3. **Energy Emitted in a Transition ($n_2 \to n_1$ where $n_2 > n_1$):**
   

$$
\Delta E = E_{n_2} - E_{n_1} = \left(-\frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{8 \varepsilon_0^2 h^2 n_2^2}\right) - \left(-\frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{8 \varepsilon_0^2 h^2 n_1^2}\right)
$$

   

$$
\Delta E = \frac{m_e Z^2 e^4}{8 \varepsilon_0^2 h^2}\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
$$

4. **Wavenumber ($\bar{\nu}$) and Wavelength ($\lambda$):**
   

$$
\Delta E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = hc\bar{\nu}
$$

   

$$
\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{\Delta E}{hc} = \frac{m_e e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2 h^3 c} Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
$$

5. **Defining the Rydberg Constant ($R$ or $R_H$):**
   

$$
R_H = \frac{m_e e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2 h^3 c} \approx 109,677\text{ cm}^{-1} = 1.09677 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)}
$$

### Problem 1 (Photoelectric Effect - Comprehensive)
**Problem:** A metal has a work function of $2.14\text{ eV}$. Light of wavelength $400\text{ nm}$ is incident on its surface. Calculate:
1. The threshold frequency ($\nu_0$) and threshold wavelength ($\lambda_0$).
2. The maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons in $\text{eV}$ and in Joules.
3. The maximum speed of the photoelectrons.
4. The stopping potential ($V_0$).

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

* **Part 1: Threshold parameters**
  

$$
W_0 = 2.14\text{ eV} = 2.14 \times 1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 3.4287 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

  

$$
\nu_0 = \frac{W_0}{h} = \frac{3.4287 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}} = 5.174 \times 10^{14}\text{ Hz}
$$

  

$$
\lambda_0 = \frac{c}{\nu_0} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}}{5.174 \times 10^{14}\text{ s}^{-1}} = 5.798 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} = 579.8\text{ nm}
$$

* **Part 2: Energy of incident photon and $K.E._{\text{max}}$**
  

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{1240\text{ eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{400\text{ nm}} = 3.10\text{ eV}
$$

  

$$
K.E._{\text{max}} = E - W_0 = 3.10\text{ eV} - 2.14\text{ eV} = 0.96\text{ eV}
$$

  

$$
K.E._{\text{max}} = 0.96 \times 1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 1.538 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

* **Part 3: Maximum speed ($v_{\text{max}}$)**
  

$$
K.E._{\text{max}} = \frac{1}{2} m_e v_{\text{max}}^2 \implies v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times K.E._{\text{max}}}{m_e}}
$$

  

$$
v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.538 \times 10^{-19}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}}} = \sqrt{0.3376 \times 10^{12}} = 5.81 \times 10^5\text{ m/s}
$$

* **Part 4: Stopping potential ($V_0$)**
  

$$
e V_0 = K.E._{\text{max}} = 0.96\text{ eV} \implies V_0 = 0.96\text{ V}
$$

### Problem 2 (Hydrogen Spectrum - Series Limits & Wavelength Ratios)
**Problem:** Calculate the ratio of the longest wavelength line to the shortest wavelength line in the Balmer series of the Hydrogen spectrum.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

For Balmer series: $n_1 = 2$.

1. **Longest wavelength ($\lambda_{\text{max}}$):**
   Occurs for the minimum energy transition: $n_2 = 3$.
   

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{max}}} = R_H \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = R_H \left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9}\right) = R_H \left(\frac{5}{36}\right) \implies \lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{36}{5 R_H}
$$

2. **Shortest wavelength / Series Limit ($\lambda_{\text{min}}$):**
   Occurs for the maximum energy transition: $n_2 = \infty$.
   

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{min}}} = R_H \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{\infty^2}\right) = R_H \left(\frac{1}{4} - 0\right) = \frac{R_H}{4} \implies \lambda_{\text{min}} = \frac{4}{R_H}
$$

3. **Ratio:**
   

$$
\frac{\lambda_{\text{max}}}{\lambda_{\text{min}}} = \frac{36 / (5 R_H)}{4 / R_H} = \frac{36}{5 \times 4} = \frac{9}{5} = 1.8
$$

### Problem 3 (Spectral Transitions in Sample vs. Single Isolated Atom)
**Problem:** An electric discharge excites electrons in a sample of atomic hydrogen gas to the $n = 5$ state.
1. What is the maximum number of spectral lines observed in the emission spectrum from this gas sample?
2. If only one isolated hydrogen atom has an electron excited to $n = 5$, what is the maximum possible number of spectral lines emitted during de-excitation to the ground state?

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

* **Case 1: Large statistical ensemble of atoms (gas sample):**
  Different atoms can de-excite via different routes. The total number of unique spectral lines is given by the combinations formula:
  

$$
N = \frac{\Delta n (\Delta n + 1)}{2} = \frac{(n_2 - n_1)(n_2 - n_1 + 1)}{2}
$$

  Here $n_2 = 5, n_1 = 1 \implies \Delta n = 4$:
  

$$
N = \frac{4(4 + 1)}{2} = \frac{20}{2} = 10\text{ spectral lines}
$$

* **Case 2: A single isolated hydrogen atom:**
  An individual electron can follow only **one** cascade path down to $n = 1$.  
  To maximize the number of emission events, it must jump down one step at a time:
  

$$
5 \to 4 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1
$$

  This yields a maximum of $5 - 1 = 4\text{ photons}$ (4 spectral lines).

# SECTION 4: EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON STUDENT PITFALLS

***

### 1. The Intensity vs. Energy Trap
* **Trap:** A question states: *"Monochromatic light with frequency $\nu < \nu_0$ is shone on a metal. The intensity is increased 100-fold. What happens to the kinetic energy and rate of emission?"*
* **Student Error:** Writing that increasing intensity will cause emission because total energy has increased.
* **Correction:** If $\nu < \nu_0$, **ZERO** electrons are emitted, regardless of intensity. Intensity increases the rate of photon arrivals, but each individual photon still lacks the work function energy.

### 2. Units in Work Function Calculations
* **Trap:** Adding or subtracting $h\nu$ in Joules with $W_0$ given in $\text{eV}$.
* **Student Error:** Forgetting to convert $W_0$ from $\text{eV}$ to $\text{J}$ ($1\text{ eV} = 1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$) or using $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ directly with electron-volts.
* **Correction:** Ensure strict unit consistency: either convert everything to Joules or use $h = 4.1357 \times 10^{-15}\text{ eV}\cdot\text{s}$.

### 3. Balmer Series Region Confusion
* **Trap:** *"Which spectral series of Hydrogen lies in the visible region?"*
* **Student Error:** Writing that all Balmer lines are in the visible region.
* **Correction:** Only the first four transitions ($n = 3, 4, 5, 6 \to 2$) fall in the visible region ($400\text{ nm} - 700\text{ nm}$). Higher transitions ($n \ge 7 \to 2$) and the series limit lie in the **near-ultraviolet** region.

### 4. Hydrogen-like Species Multipliers
* **Trap:** Calculating the wavelength for $\text{He}^+$ or $\text{Li}^{2+}$.
* **Student Error:** Forgetting the $Z^2$ factor in Rydberg's formula:
  

$$
\bar{\nu} = R_H Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
$$

  For $\text{He}^+$, $Z = 2 \implies Z^2 = 4$. Forgetting $Z^2$ causes an immediate factor-of-4 or factor-of-9 error.

# SECTION 5: SPEED HACKS & GOLDEN FORMULAE

***

### 1. Fast Photon Energy Conversions

$$
E (\text{in eV}) = \frac{1240}{\lambda (\text{in nm})} = \frac{12400}{\lambda (\text{in Å})}
$$

* Example: For $\lambda = 310\text{ nm}$:
  

$$
E = \frac{1240}{310} = 4.0\text{ eV}
$$

### 2. Fast Rydberg Constant Arithmetic
Do not evaluate $\frac{1}{1.097 \times 10^7}$ manually. Use:

$$
\frac{1}{R_H} \approx 912\text{ Å} = 91.2\text{ nm}
$$

* For the Lyman series limit ($n = 1, n_2 = \infty$):
  

$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{R_H} = 912\text{ Å}
$$

* For the Balmer series limit ($n = 2, n_2 = \infty$):
  

$$
\lambda = \frac{4}{R_H} = 4 \times 912\text{ Å} = 3648\text{ Å} = 364.8\text{ nm}
$$

### 3. Total Spectral Lines Shortcut
* For transition from $n_2$ to $n_1$ in a collection of atoms:
  

$$
N_{\text{total}} = \frac{(n_2 - n_1)(n_2 - n_1 + 1)}{2}
$$

* Number of lines belonging specifically to a series with lower level $n_k$ (where $n_k \ge n_1$):
  

$$
N_{\text{series}} = n_2 - n_k
$$

  * *Example:* For transition from $n = 6 \to 1$:
    * Total lines = $\frac{(6-1)(6-1+1)}{2} = \frac{5 \times 6}{2} = 15$
    * Lyman lines ($n_k = 1$): $6 - 1 = 5$
    * Balmer lines ($n_k = 2$): $6 - 2 = 4$
    * Paschen lines ($n_k = 3$): $6 - 3 = 3$
    * Brackett lines ($n_k = 4$): $6 - 4 = 2$
    * Pfund lines ($n_k = 5$): $6 - 5 = 1$
    * Total = $5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15$
