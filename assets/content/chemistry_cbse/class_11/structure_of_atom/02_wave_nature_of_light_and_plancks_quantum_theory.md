### 1.1 Classical Wave Theory of Light (James Clerk Maxwell, 1870)
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Historical Context & Premise
Prior to Maxwell, light was viewed either as corpuscles (Newton) or as mechanical longitudinal/transverse disturbances requiring a material medium (Huygens, Fresnel). Maxwell demonstrated theoretically and experimentally that when an electrically charged particle moves under acceleration, alternating electrical and magnetic fields are produced and transmitted in the form of coupled waves called **Electromagnetic Waves (EMWs)** or **Electromagnetic Radiation**.

```
                ▲ Electric Field Vector (E)
                │       _.._
                │     .'    '.
                │    /        \
                │   /          \
  ──────────────┼──*────────────*────────────*──► Direction of Propagation (z)
               /    \          / '.        .'
              /      '.      .'    '-.__.-'
             /         `''''`
            ▼ Magnetic Field Vector (B)
```

#### Core Postulates
1. **Source of Radiation:** Accelerated or oscillating electric charges radiate energy continuously in the form of waves.
2. **Coupled Fields:** The oscillating electric field ($\vec{E}$) and magnetic field ($\vec{B}$) are perpendicular to each other, and both are mutually perpendicular to the direction of propagation of the wave.
3. **Medium-Independent Propagation:** Unlike sound waves or seismic waves, electromagnetic waves require **no material medium** for propagation and can travel through vacuum.
4. **Universal Velocity in Vacuum:** In a vacuum, all forms of electromagnetic radiation travel at an identical speed, designated by $c$:
   

$$
c = 2.99792458 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1} \approx 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

### 1.2 Fundamental Wave Parameters
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

1. **Wavelength ($\lambda$):**
   - *Definition:* The linear distance between two consecutive crests or two consecutive troughs of a wave.
   - *SI Unit:* Meter ($\text{m}$).
   - *Common Metric Conversions:*
     - $1\ \text{Å (Angstrom)} = 10^{-10}\ \text{m} = 10^{-8}\ \text{cm} = 0.1\ \text{nm} = 100\ \text{pm}$
     - $1\ \text{nm (nanometer)} = 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 10\ \text{Å} = 1000\ \text{pm}$
     - $1\ \text{pm (picometer)} = 10^{-12}\ \text{m} = 10^{-3}\ \text{nm} = 10^{-2}\ \text{Å}$

2. **Frequency ($\nu$, Greek letter 'nu'):**
   - *Definition:* The number of complete wave cycles that pass a given reference point per second.
   - *SI Unit:* Hertz ($\text{Hz}$), where $1\ \text{Hz} = 1\ \text{cycle s}^{-1} = 1\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

3. **Velocity ($c$ or $v$):**
   - *Definition:* The linear distance traveled by the wave per unit time in a given medium.
   - *Equation:* 
     

$$
c = \nu \lambda
$$

4. **Wave Number ($\bar{\nu}$, 'nu bar'):**
   - *Definition:* The number of wavelengths per unit length; fundamentally, the spatial frequency and the mathematical reciprocal of wavelength.
   - *Equation:*
     

$$
\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{\nu}{c}
$$

   - *SI Unit:* $\text{m}^{-1}$.
   - *Common Laboratory Unit:* $\text{cm}^{-1}$ (widely used in vibrational/rotational spectroscopy).
   - *Conversion:* $1\ \text{cm}^{-1} = 100\ \text{m}^{-1}$; $1\ \text{m}^{-1} = 10^{-2}\ \text{cm}^{-1}$.

5. **Amplitude ($A$):**
   - *Definition:* The maximum displacement or height of a crest (or depth of a trough) from the undisturbed equilibrium line of propagation.
   - *Physical Significance:* Determines the **intensity** or brightness of the radiation:
     

$$
I \propto A^2
$$

6. **Time Period ($T$):**
   - *Definition:* The time taken by a single complete wave cycle to pass a designated point.
   - *Equation:*
     

$$
T = \frac{1}{\nu}
$$

   - *SI Unit:* Second ($\text{s}$).

### 1.3 The Electromagnetic Spectrum
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

The complete, continuous array formed by arranging all types of electromagnetic radiations in order of **increasing wavelength** (or **decreasing frequency / wave number**).

```
Decreasing Frequency (ν), Decreasing Energy (E), Increasing Wavelength (λ) ─────────►
┌──────────────┬─────────────┬─────────────┬─────────────┬──────────────┬─────────────┬─────────────┐
│  Gamma Rays  │   X-Rays    │     UV      │   Visible   │   Infrared   │ Microwaves  │ Radio Waves │
│ (γ-rays)     │             │             │             │    (IR)      │             │             │
└──────────────┴─────────────┴─────────────┴─────────────┴──────────────┴─────────────┴─────────────┘
λ: ~10⁻¹⁶ m    ~10⁻¹⁰ m      ~10⁻⁸ m       400-750 nm    ~10⁻⁵ m        ~10⁻² m       ~10²-10⁴ m
ν: ~10²⁴ Hz    ~10¹⁸ Hz      ~10¹⁶ Hz      ~10¹⁴ Hz      ~10¹³ Hz       ~10¹⁰ Hz      ~10⁶ Hz
```

#### Spectral Bands & Analytical Signatures
* **Gamma ($\gamma$) rays:** $\lambda \sim 10^{-16}\ \text{m}$ to $10^{-12}\ \text{m}$; nuclear transitions.
* **X-rays:** $\lambda \sim 10^{-12}\ \text{m}$ to $10^{-9}\ \text{m}$; inner-shell electronic transitions.
* **Ultraviolet (UV):** $\lambda \sim 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$ to $4 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$ ($10\ \text{nm} - 400\ \text{nm}$); outer valence electronic transitions.
* **Visible Spectrum:** $\lambda \approx 380\ \text{nm} - 750\ \text{nm}$ ($4000\ \text{Å} - 7500\ \text{Å}$).
  - Sequence: **V - I - B - G - Y - O - R**
  - Violet: $\lambda \approx 380 - 450\ \text{nm}$ (highest frequency, highest energy)
  - Red: $\lambda \approx 650 - 750\ \text{nm}$ (longest wavelength, lowest energy)
* **Infrared (IR):** $\lambda \sim 750\ \text{nm}$ to $1\ \text{mm}$; molecular vibrations.
* **Microwaves:** $\lambda \sim 1\ \text{mm}$ to $0.1\ \text{m}$; molecular rotations, radar, telecommunications.
* **Radio waves:** $\lambda \sim 0.1\ \text{m}$ to $10^5\ \text{m}$; nuclear magnetic spin flips, radio/television broadcast.

### 1.4 Failure of Classical Electromagnetic Wave Theory
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Maxwell’s wave model successfully explained bulk wave phenomena governed by phase coherence:
* Reflection & Refraction
* Interference (constructive and destructive wave superposition)
* Diffraction (bending of waves around obstacles)

However, it **fundamentally failed** to rationalize phenomena involving the interaction of radiation with matter at atomic/submicroscopic dimensions:
1. **Black Body Radiation:** Classical electrodynamics could not explain the spectral distribution curve and predicted an infinite energy emission at short wavelengths (the "Ultraviolet Catastrophe").
2. **Photoelectric Effect:** Classical theory predicted that electron emission should depend on the *intensity* (amplitude) of light and exhibit a measurable time lag, contradicting experimental reality.
3. **Variation of Heat Capacity of Solids ($C_v$) as a Function of Temperature:** Classical Dulong-Petit law ($C_v = 3R$) failed at low temperatures, where heat capacity drops sharply toward zero.
4. **Line / Atomic Spectra of Atoms:** Classical theory dictated that orbiting, accelerating electrons must radiate energy continuously, producing a continuous spectrum and collapsing into the nucleus within $\sim 10^{-8}\ \text{s}$. Instead, isolated atoms emit discrete, discontinuous spectral lines.

### 1.5 Black Body Radiation
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Rigorous Definitions
* **Ideal Black Body:** An idealized physical body that acts as a perfect absorber and perfect emitter of all electromagnetic radiation, irrespective of frequency or angle of incidence.
* **Absorption Coefficient ($\alpha$):** For an ideal black body, $\alpha = 1.0$ across all wavelengths ($\lambda \in [0, \infty)$). It neither reflects nor transmits any incident radiation.
* **Physical Realization:** A hollow enclosure (cavity) constructed of a high-melting conductor (e.g., carbon/platinum) coated internally with lampblack or platinum black, having a minute pinhole aperture. Any radiation entering through the aperture undergoes infinite internal reflections with near-complete absorption at each bounce, rendering the aperture a near-ideal black body.

#### Experimental Observations & Spectral Energy Distribution Curves
When a black body is maintained at a constant uniform temperature $T$, it emits thermal radiation consisting of an unbroken, continuous range of wavelengths.

```
Emissive Power
  E_λ (dE/dλ)
     ▲
     │              .---.  T₃ (Highest Temp, e.g., 2000 K)
     │             /     \
     │            /       \
     │           /  .---.  \  T₂ (e.g., 1500 K)
     │          /  /     \  \
     │         /  /   _   \  \   T₁ (Lowest Temp, e.g., 1000 K)
     │        /  /  .' '.  \  \
     │       /  /  /     \  \  \
     │      /  /  /       \  \  \
     │     /  /  /         \  \  \
     └────┴──┴──┴───────────┴──┴──┴────────► Wavelength (λ)
          │  │  │
          λ₃ λ₂ λ₁  (λ_max shifts to lower values as T increases)
```

1. **Continuity:** The emitted radiation is continuous across the entire spectrum.
2. **Dependence Solely on Temperature:** The intensity and spectral distribution of emitted radiation depend **only on absolute temperature ($T$)**, completely independent of the material composition, shape, or size of the black body.
3. **Non-monotonic Distribution:** At a given constant temperature, as wavelength ($\lambda$) increases:
   - Emissive power ($E_\lambda$) starts near zero at very short wavelengths.
   - Rises to a distinct **maximum intensity** at a characteristic wavelength, designated as $\lambda_{\text{max}}$.
   - Progressively decreases toward zero as $\lambda \to \infty$.
4. **Effect of Increasing Temperature:**
   - **Total Emitted Energy Increases:** The total radiant energy emitted per unit surface area per second (represented by the total integrated area under the curve) increases sharply with temperature ($\propto T^4$).
   - **Hypsochromic (Blue) Shift:** The peak of the curve ($\lambda_{\text{max}}$) systematically shifts toward **shorter wavelengths** (higher frequencies/energies).
   - *Everyday Qualitative Observation:* Heating an iron rod in a furnace:
     

$$
\text{Dull Red} \xrightarrow{\Delta T} \text{Bright Red} \xrightarrow{\Delta T} \text{Orange/Yellow} \xrightarrow{\Delta T} \text{White} \xrightarrow{\Delta T} \text{Blue-White}
$$

     (Red has the longest visible wavelength; as temperature climbs, emission shifts to shorter, higher-energy wavelengths).

### 1.6 Planck's Quantum Theory of Radiation (Max Planck, 1900)
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

To resolve the complete failure of classical physics to model black body radiation, Max Planck introduced a revolutionary postulate that marked the birth of quantum mechanics.

#### Core Postulates
1. **Discontinuous Emission and Absorption:** Radiant energy is **not** emitted or absorbed continuously like an unbroken stream of fluid. Instead, it is emitted or absorbed discontinuously in discrete, localized packets or bundles of energy.
2. **Definition of Quantum:** The smallest indivisible packet of radiant energy that can be emitted or absorbed is termed a **quantum** (plural: *quanta*). In the specific context of electromagnetic radiation and light, this packet is termed a **photon** (a term coined later by Gilbert N. Lewis in 1926).
3. **Energy Proportionality:** The energy ($E$) of a single quantum is directly proportional to the frequency ($\nu$) of the associated radiation:
   

$$
E \propto \nu \implies E = h\nu
$$

   Where:
   - $E$ is the energy of one quantum/photon in Joules ($\text{J}$).
   - $\nu$ is the frequency of radiation in Hertz ($\text{s}^{-1}$).
   - $h$ is **Planck's Constant**, a fundamental universal constant of nature:
     

$$
h = 6.62607015 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s} \quad (\text{or kg m}^2\ \text{s}^{-1})
$$

     

$$
h \approx 6.626 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{erg s}
$$

4. **Quantization of Energy:** An oscillating atom, molecule, or material system can emit or absorb energy only in integral multiples of a single quantum. Fractional quanta (e.g., $1.5\,h\nu$, $0.7\,h\nu$) are strictly forbidden by physical law:
   

$$
E_{\text{total}} = n h \nu = n \frac{h c}{\lambda} = n h c \bar{\nu} \quad (n \in \mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, \dots\})
$$

   Here, $n$ represents the **number of photons** or the **quantum number**.

### 2.1 Classical Radiation Laws: Rayleigh-Jeans Law & The Ultraviolet Catastrophe
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐

#### Assumptions (Classical Physics)
1. The interior cavity walls of a black body consist of classical harmonic oscillators vibrating with continuous energy states.
2. By the **Classical Equipartition of Energy Theorem**, each standing electromagnetic wave mode inside the cavity possesses an average thermal kinetic + potential energy of:
   

$$
\langle \varepsilon \rangle = k_B T
$$

   Where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant ($k_B = R / N_A = 1.3806 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1}$).

#### Derivation Summary
The number of spatial standing wave modes per unit volume in the frequency interval between $\nu$ and $\nu + d\nu$ inside a three-dimensional enclosure is:

$$
N(\nu)\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi \nu^2}{c^3}\,d\nu
$$

Multiplying the density of modes by the classical average energy per mode ($\langle \varepsilon \rangle = k_B T$):

$$
u_\nu\,d\nu = N(\nu) \langle \varepsilon \rangle\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi \nu^2}{c^3} k_B T\,d\nu
$$

Converting to wavelength domain using $\nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} \implies |d\nu| = \frac{c}{\lambda^2}\,d\lambda$:

$$
u_\lambda\,d\lambda = u_\nu \left|\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}\right| d\lambda = \left(\frac{8\pi (c/\lambda)^2}{c^3} k_B T\right) \left(\frac{c}{\lambda^2}\right) d\lambda
$$

$$
\boxed{u_\lambda\,d\lambda = \frac{8\pi k_B T}{\lambda^4}\,d\lambda} \quad \text{[Rayleigh-Jeans Law]}
$$

#### Physical Consequence: The Ultraviolet Catastrophe
As $\lambda \to 0$ (i.e., in the ultraviolet, X-ray, and $\gamma$-ray regions):

$$
\lim_{\lambda \to 0} u_\lambda = \lim_{\lambda \to 0} \frac{8\pi k_B T}{\lambda^4} = +\infty
$$

Classical physics predicted that any warm body should radiate infinite power at short wavelengths. This catastrophic divergence is termed the **Ultraviolet Catastrophe**.

### 2.2 Planck’s Radiation Law
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Planck's Postulate
Cavity resonators cannot absorb or emit continuous energies; instead, their energy states are strictly discrete:

$$
\varepsilon_n = n h \nu \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots)
$$

#### Derivation of Average Energy of an Oscillator ($\langle \varepsilon \rangle$)
According to Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics, the probability of an oscillator occupying energy level $\varepsilon_n$ at temperature $T$ is proportional to the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\varepsilon_n / k_B T}$:

$$
\langle \varepsilon \rangle = \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \varepsilon_n e^{-\varepsilon_n / k_B T}}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\varepsilon_n / k_B T}} = \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (n h \nu) e^{-n h \nu / k_B T}}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-n h \nu / k_B T}}
$$

Let $x = \frac{h\nu}{k_B T}$, then:

$$
\text{Denominator: } S = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-n x} = 1 + e^{-x} + e^{-2x} + \dots = \frac{1}{1 - e^{-x}}
$$

$$
\text{Numerator: } -\frac{d S}{d(1/k_B T)} = h\nu \sum_{n=0}^\infty n e^{-nx} = -h\nu \frac{d S}{dx} = -h\nu \left[ -\frac{e^{-x}}{(1 - e^{-x})^2} \right] = \frac{h\nu e^{-x}}{(1 - e^{-x})^2}
$$

Therefore, the average energy per oscillator is:

$$
\langle \varepsilon \rangle = \frac{\frac{h\nu e^{-x}}{(1 - e^{-x})^2}}{\frac{1}{1 - e^{-x}}} = \frac{h\nu e^{-x}}{1 - e^{-x}} = \frac{h\nu}{e^x - 1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\langle \varepsilon \rangle = \frac{h\nu}{e^{\frac{h\nu}{k_B T}} - 1}}
$$

#### Planck's Spectral Energy Density Formula
Multiplying the spatial mode density $N(\nu)\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi \nu^2}{c^3}\,d\nu$ by the quantum average energy $\langle \varepsilon \rangle$:

$$
u_\nu\,d\nu = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3}{c^3 \left(e^{\frac{h\nu}{k_B T}} - 1\right)}\,d\nu
$$

In terms of wavelength ($\lambda$):

$$
\boxed{u_\lambda\,d\lambda = \frac{8\pi h c}{\lambda^5 \left(e^{\frac{h c}{\lambda k_B T}} - 1\right)}\,d\lambda}
$$

#### Limiting Cases
* **Low Frequencies / Long Wavelengths ($h\nu \ll k_B T$ or $\lambda \to \infty$):**
  Expand the exponential using Taylor series: $e^{\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}} \approx 1 + \frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T} + \dots$
  

$$
u_\lambda \approx \frac{8\pi h c}{\lambda^5 \left(1 + \frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T} - 1\right)} = \frac{8\pi k_B T}{\lambda^4} \quad \text{(Reduces identically to Rayleigh-Jeans Law)}
$$

* **High Frequencies / Short Wavelengths ($h\nu \gg k_B T$ or $\lambda \to 0$):**
  Since $e^{\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}} \gg 1$:
  

$$
u_\lambda \approx \frac{8\pi h c}{\lambda^5} e^{-\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}} \quad \text{(Reduces identically to Wien's Distribution Law)}
$$

  As $\lambda \to 0$, $e^{\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}} \to \infty$, causing $u_\lambda \to 0$. The Ultraviolet Catastrophe is completely resolved.

### 2.3 Wien's Displacement Law
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Statement
The wavelength corresponding to the maximum spectral emissive power ($\lambda_{\text{max}}$) of an ideal black body is inversely proportional to its absolute thermodynamic temperature ($T$).

#### Mathematical Formulation

$$
\lambda_{\text{max}} \propto \frac{1}{T} \implies \boxed{\lambda_{\text{max}} \cdot T = b}
$$

Where $b$ is **Wien's Displacement Constant**:

$$
b = 2.89777 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K} \approx 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K} = 0.2898\ \text{cm}\cdot\text{K}
$$

#### Mathematical Derivation from Planck's Distribution
To find the peak wavelength $\lambda_{\text{max}}$, differentiate Planck’s distribution with respect to $\lambda$ and set the derivative to zero:

$$
\frac{d u_\lambda}{d\lambda} = 0
$$

Using $u_\lambda = C_1 \lambda^{-5} \left(e^{\frac{C_2}{\lambda T}} - 1\right)^{-1}$ where $C_1 = 8\pi hc$ and $C_2 = \frac{hc}{k_B}$:

$$
\frac{d u_\lambda}{d\lambda} = C_1 \left[ -5\lambda^{-6}\left(e^{\frac{C_2}{\lambda T}} - 1\right)^{-1} - \lambda^{-5}\left(e^{\frac{C_2}{\lambda T}} - 1\right)^{-2} e^{\frac{C_2}{\lambda T}}\left(-\frac{C_2}{\lambda^2 T}\right) \right] = 0
$$

Dividing by $C_1 \lambda^{-6} \left(e^{\frac{C_2}{\lambda T}} - 1\right)^{-1}$:

$$
-5 + \frac{C_2}{\lambda T} \frac{e^{\frac{C_2}{\lambda T}}}{e^{\frac{C_2}{\lambda T}} - 1} = 0
$$

Let $x = \frac{C_2}{\lambda T} = \frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}$:

$$
\frac{x e^x}{e^x - 1} = 5 \implies x = 5(1 - e^{-x})
$$

Solving this transcendental equation numerically:

$$
x \approx 4.965114
$$

Substituting back:

$$
\frac{hc}{\lambda_{\text{max}} k_B T} = 4.965114 \implies \lambda_{\text{max}} T = \frac{hc}{4.965114\,k_B}
$$

Evaluating using standard fundamental physical constants:

$$
\lambda_{\text{max}} T = \frac{(6.62607 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(2.99792 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1})}{(4.965114)(1.38065 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1})} = \boxed{2.8978 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}}
$$

### 2.4 Stefan-Boltzmann Law
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Statement
The total radiant energy ($E_{\text{total}}$) emitted per unit surface area of a black body per unit time (integrated over all frequencies) is directly proportional to the **fourth power** of its absolute thermodynamic temperature ($T$).

#### Mathematical Formulation

$$
\boxed{E_{\text{total}} = \sigma T^4}
$$

For a non-black, real physical surface with emissivity $e$ ($0 < e < 1$):

$$
E_{\text{total}} = e \sigma T^4
$$

Where:
- $\sigma$ is the **Stefan-Boltzmann Constant**:
  

$$
\sigma = \frac{2\pi^5 k_B^4}{15 c^2 h^3} \approx \boxed{5.670374 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\ \text{K}^{-4} \quad (\text{or J s}^{-1}\ \text{m}^{-2}\ \text{K}^{-4})}
$$

- Emissivity of an ideal black body: $e = 1$.

#### Net Radiative Heat Transfer Rate
If a black body at temperature $T$ is placed inside an enclosure maintained at ambient temperature $T_0$ (where $T > T_0$):

$$
\boxed{\frac{dQ_{\text{net}}}{dt} = \sigma A (T^4 - T_0^4)}
$$

### 2.5 Master Quantum Energy Formulations
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Energy of a Single Photon

$$
\boxed{E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = hc\bar{\nu}}
$$

#### 2. Total Energy of $n$ Photons

$$
\boxed{E_{\text{total}} = n h \nu = n \frac{hc}{\lambda} = n h c \bar{\nu}}
$$

$$
\boxed{n = \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{E_{\text{total}}\cdot \lambda}{hc}}
$$

#### 3. Energy of 1 Mole of Photons (The Einstein of Radiation)

$$
E_{\text{mole}} = N_A h \nu = \frac{N_A hc}{\lambda}
$$

Where $N_A = 6.02214 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.

#### 4. Source Power and Photon Emission Rate
Power ($P$) is defined as energy emitted per unit time:

$$
P = \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{n_{\text{total}} E_{\text{photon}}}{t} \quad [\text{Watts} = \text{J s}^{-1}]
$$

Let $N_{\text{rate}} = \frac{n}{t}$ be the **photon flux** (number of photons emitted per second):

$$
\boxed{P = N_{\text{rate}} \cdot \left(\frac{hc}{\lambda}\right) \implies N_{\text{rate}} = \frac{P \cdot \lambda}{hc}}
$$

If source operating efficiency is $\eta\%$ (e.g., a $100\ \text{W}$ bulb converting only $5\%$ input electric energy to light):

$$
P_{\text{useful}} = P_{\text{rated}} \times \left(\frac{\eta}{100}\right)
$$

$$
\boxed{N_{\text{rate}} = \frac{P_{\text{rated}} \times \eta \times \lambda}{100 \times hc}}
$$

### Problem 3.1: Fundamental Wave Parameters (NCERT Core Archetype)
**Question:** The vivid yellow emission line from a low-pressure sodium vapor lamp has a measured wavelength ($\lambda$) of $589\ \text{nm}$. Calculate:
1. The frequency ($\nu$) of this sodium line in vacuum.
2. The wave number ($\bar{\nu}$) in both $\text{m}^{-1}$ and $\text{cm}^{-1}$.
3. The time period ($T$) of the oscillation.

#### Solution:
**Step 1: Convert units to standard SI format**

$$
\lambda = 589\ \text{nm} = 589 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 5.89 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}
$$

Speed of light in vacuum, $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

**Step 2: Frequency ($\nu$)**

$$
\nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1}}{5.89 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 0.509337 \times 10^{15}\ \text{s}^{-1}
$$

$$
\mathbf{\nu = 5.09 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}}
$$

**Step 3: Wave Number ($\bar{\nu}$)**

$$
\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{5.89 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 1.69779 \times 10^6\ \text{m}^{-1}
$$

$$
\mathbf{\bar{\nu} = 1.70 \times 10^6\ \text{m}^{-1}}
$$

Converting to $\text{cm}^{-1}$:

$$
\bar{\nu}_{(\text{cm}^{-1})} = \frac{\bar{\nu}_{(\text{m}^{-1})}}{100} = \frac{1.6978 \times 10^6}{100} = \mathbf{1.70 \times 10^4\ \text{cm}^{-1}}
$$

**Step 4: Time Period ($T$)**

$$
T = \frac{1}{\nu} = \frac{1}{5.0934 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}} = \mathbf{1.96 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{s}}
$$

### Problem 3.2: Photon Energy Comparison & Conversions
**Question:** Calculate and compare the energies of:
1. A photon of ultraviolet radiation having $\lambda = 250\ \text{nm}$.
2. A photon of infrared radiation having $\lambda = 10\ \mu\text{m}$.
Express both energies in Joules ($\text{J}$) and in electron-volts ($\text{eV}$) ($1\ \text{eV} = 1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$).

#### Solution:
**Case 1: Ultraviolet Photon ($\lambda_1 = 250 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$)**

$$
E_1 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_1} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1})}{250 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}}
$$

$$
E_1 = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{J m}}{2.50 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = \mathbf{7.95 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}}
$$

In electron-volts:

$$
E_{1(\text{eV})} = \frac{7.951 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}}{1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J eV}^{-1}} = \mathbf{4.96\ \text{eV}}
$$

**Case 2: Infrared Photon ($\lambda_2 = 10\ \mu\text{m} = 10 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}$)**

$$
E_2 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_2} = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{J m}}{1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}} = \mathbf{1.988 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}}
$$

In electron-volts:

$$
E_{2(\text{eV})} = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}}{1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J eV}^{-1}} = \mathbf{0.124\ \text{eV}}
$$

**Energy Ratio:**

$$
\frac{E_{\text{UV}}}{E_{\text{IR}}} = \frac{\lambda_{\text{IR}}}{\lambda_{\text{UV}}} = \frac{10 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}}{250 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}} = \mathbf{40}
$$

The UV photon carries 40 times more energy than the IR photon.

### Problem 3.3: Photon Flux from a Rated Lamp (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
**Question:** A $100\ \text{W}$ sodium light bulb radiates monochromatic yellow light uniformly in all directions. The wavelength of the emitted radiation is $\lambda = 589.3\ \text{nm}$. Assuming the electrical-to-radiant energy conversion efficiency of the bulb is strictly $3.0\%$, calculate the number of photons emitted by the bulb per second.

#### Solution:
**Step 1: Determine useful radiant optical power ($P_{\text{radiant}}$)**

$$
\text{Total input electrical power } P_{\text{in}} = 100\ \text{W} = 100\ \text{J s}^{-1}
$$

$$
\text{Efficiency } \eta = 3.0\%
$$

$$
P_{\text{radiant}} = P_{\text{in}} \times \frac{\eta}{100} = 100 \times \frac{3.0}{100} = 3.0\ \text{J s}^{-1}
$$

Thus, radiant energy emitted in $1\ \text{second}$ is $E_{\text{total}} = 3.0\ \text{J}$.

**Step 2: Calculate energy of a single photon ($E_{\text{photon}}$)**

$$
\lambda = 589.3 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}
$$

$$
E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1})}{589.3 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}}
$$

$$
E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-25}}{5.893 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.37315 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

**Step 3: Calculate photon emission flux ($N_{\text{rate}}$)**

$$
N_{\text{rate}} = \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{3.0\ \text{J s}^{-1}}{3.37315 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J photon}^{-1}}
$$

$$
\mathbf{N_{\text{rate}} = 8.89 \times 10^{18}\ \text{photons s}^{-1}}
$$

### Problem 3.4: Quantum Energy of 1 Mole of Photons (Einstein of Radiation)
**Question:** A pulse of laser radiation has a wavelength of $400\ \text{nm}$. Calculate:
1. The energy carried by one mole of these photons in kilojoules per mole ($\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$).
2. How many moles of photons are required to provide $1000\ \text{kJ}$ of energy?

#### Solution:
**Step 1: Energy per mole ($E_{\text{mole}}$)**

$$
E_{\text{mole}} = \frac{N_A hc}{\lambda}
$$

Where:
- $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$
- $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$
- $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
- $\lambda = 400 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 4.00 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$

$$
E_{\text{mole}} = \frac{(6.022 \times 10^{23})(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^8)}{4.00 \times 10^{-7}}
$$

$$
N_A hc = (6.022 \times 10^{23})(1.9878 \times 10^{-25}) = 0.119705\ \text{J m mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
E_{\text{mole}} = \frac{0.119705}{4.00 \times 10^{-7}} = 299263\ \text{J mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\mathbf{E_{\text{mole}} = 299.3\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}}
$$

**Step 2: Number of moles to produce $1000\ \text{kJ}$**

$$
n_{\text{mole}} = \frac{E_{\text{given}}}{E_{\text{mole}}} = \frac{1000\ \text{kJ}}{299.26\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}} = \mathbf{3.34\ \text{mol}}
$$

### Problem 3.5: Wien's Displacement Law Applied to Stellar Black Bodies
**Question:** The surface of a distant luminous star behaves as a near-perfect black body radiator. An astronomical spectrophotometer records that the peak spectral radiant intensity occurs at a wavelength of $\lambda_{\text{max}} = 362.2\ \text{nm}$. 
1. Compute the effective surface temperature of this star.
2. If the temperature of the star were to double, at what wavelength would the peak emission occur, and in which region of the EM spectrum would that fall? (Given: $b = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$).

#### Solution:
**Step 1: Surface Temperature ($T$)**
By Wien's Displacement Law:

$$
\lambda_{\text{max}} \cdot T = b \implies T = \frac{b}{\lambda_{\text{max}}}
$$

$$
\lambda_{\text{max}} = 362.2\ \text{nm} = 3.622 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}
$$

$$
T = \frac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}}{3.622 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = \mathbf{8001\ \text{K}} \approx \mathbf{8.00 \times 10^3\ \text{K}}
$$

**Step 2: New Peak Wavelength upon Doubling Temperature**

$$
T_2 = 2 \times T_1
$$

$$
\lambda_{\text{max}, 2} \cdot T_2 = \lambda_{\text{max}, 1} \cdot T_1 \implies \lambda_{\text{max}, 2} = \lambda_{\text{max}, 1} \times \frac{T_1}{T_2} = \frac{362.2\ \text{nm}}{2} = \mathbf{181.1\ \text{nm}}
$$

Since $\lambda = 181.1\ \text{nm} < 380\ \text{nm}$, this peak lies deep within the **Ultraviolet (UV)** region of the spectrum.

### Problem 3.6: Stefan-Boltzmann Radiative Energy Calculation
**Question:** A spherical carbon black body of radius $R = 5.0\ \text{cm}$ is maintained inside a vacuum furnace at a temperature of $1273\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The furnace walls are kept at an ambient temperature of $27\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Taking the emissivity $e = 1.0$ and $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\ \text{K}^{-4}$:
1. Find the total radiant power emitted per unit area by the sphere.
2. Calculate the net rate of radiant energy loss from the sphere to the furnace walls.

#### Solution:
**Step 1: Temperature Conversion to Thermodynamic Kelvin Scale**

$$
T = 1273 + 273.15 = 1546.15\ \text{K} \approx 1546\ \text{K}
$$

$$
T_0 = 27 + 273.15 = 300.15\ \text{K} \approx 300\ \text{K}
$$

**Step 2: Emissive Power per Unit Area ($E$)**

$$
E = \sigma T^4 = (5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\ \text{K}^{-4}) \times (1546\ \text{K})^4
$$

$$
(1546)^4 \approx 5.7126 \times 10^{12}\ \text{K}^4
$$

$$
E = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 5.7126 \times 10^{12} = \mathbf{3.24 \times 10^5\ \text{W m}^{-2}}
$$

**Step 3: Surface Area of the Sphere ($A$)**

$$
R = 5.0\ \text{cm} = 0.05\ \text{m}
$$

$$
A = 4\pi R^2 = 4 \times 3.1416 \times (0.05\ \text{m})^2 = 4 \times 3.1416 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-3} = 3.1416 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{m}^2
$$

**Step 4: Net Rate of Heat Loss ($\frac{dQ_{\text{net}}}{dt}$)**

$$
\frac{dQ_{\text{net}}}{dt} = \sigma A (T^4 - T_0^4)
$$

$$
T^4 = (1546)^4 \approx 5.7126 \times 10^{12}\ \text{K}^4
$$

$$
T_0^4 = (300)^4 = 8.1 \times 10^9\ \text{K}^4 = 0.0081 \times 10^{12}\ \text{K}^4
$$

$$
(T^4 - T_0^4) = (5.7126 - 0.0081) \times 10^{12} \approx 5.7045 \times 10^{12}\ \text{K}^4
$$

$$
\frac{dQ_{\text{net}}}{dt} = (5.67 \times 10^{-8}) \times (3.1416 \times 10^{-2}) \times (5.7045 \times 10^{12})
$$

$$
\frac{dQ_{\text{net}}}{dt} = 1.7813 \times 10^{-9} \times 5.7045 \times 10^{12} = \mathbf{10161\ \text{W}} \approx \mathbf{10.16\ \text{kW}}
$$

# SECTION 4: EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

***

### Trap 4.1: Metric Unit Mismatch in Planck's Equation
* **The Error:** A student calculates photon energy $E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$ by substituting $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$ and $c = 3 \times 10^8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, but enters $\lambda$ in Angstroms (e.g., $4000$) or nanometers (e.g., $400$) directly without converting to meters.
* **Resulting Calamity:** The calculated energy is incorrect by $10^{10}$ or $10^9$ orders of magnitude.
* **Examiner Rule:** Always confirm:
  

$$
\lambda\ \text{must be in meters (m)} \iff h\ \text{is in }\text{J s} \iff c\ \text{is in }\text{m s}^{-1}
$$

***

### Trap 4.2: Celsius vs. Absolute Thermodynamic Kelvin ($T$)
* **The Error:** In Wien's Law ($\lambda_{\text{max}} T = b$) or the Stefan-Boltzmann Law ($E = \sigma T^4$), the student directly inserts temperatures given in Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$).
* **Why it is fatal:** 
  

$$
\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^4 \neq \left(\frac{\theta_2\ (^\circ\text{C})}{\theta_1\ (^\circ\text{C})}\right)^4
$$

  For example, going from $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $200\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is **not** a doubling of thermodynamic temperature ($373\ \text{K} \to 473\ \text{K}$, a ratio of $1.27$, not $2$).
* **Examiner Rule:** Absolute zero ($0\ \text{K}$) is the baseline. Always add $273.15$ to Celsius temperatures before evaluating powers.

***

### Trap 4.3: Confusion Between Intensity and Frequency/Energy
* **The Error:** Assuming that "more intense light means more energetic photons."
* **The Scientific Fact:**
  - **Intensity** corresponds to the **number of photons** hitting a unit area per second (photon flux / photon density).
  - **Frequency ($\nu$) / Wavelength ($\lambda$)** determines the **energy per individual photon** ($E = h\nu$).
  - *Illustration:* A low-intensity, dim ultraviolet laser beam emits far fewer photons than a 1000 W red heat lamp, but each UV photon carries significantly higher energy than each red photon.

***

### Trap 4.4: Wave Number ($\bar{\nu}$) vs. Frequency ($\nu$)
* **The Error:** Confusing the symbols $\nu$ and $\bar{\nu}$, or using the value of $\bar{\nu}$ directly in $E = h\nu$.
* **The Correction:**
  

$$
E \neq h\bar{\nu} \quad \left[\text{Dimensionally incorrect: }\text{J s} \times \text{m}^{-1} \neq \text{J}\right]
$$

  

$$
\text{Correct: } E = hc\bar{\nu} \quad \left[\text{Dimensionally correct: }\text{J s} \times \text{m s}^{-1} \times \text{m}^{-1} = \text{J}\right]
$$

***

### Trap 4.5: Black Body Emission Curve Asymmetry
* **The Error:** Sketching the black body radiation intensity curve as a symmetrical Gaussian/bell curve.
* **Examiner Trap:** Board and competitive exam evaluators look for the characteristic **skewed asymmetry**: the curve rises steeply at short wavelengths to a peak ($\lambda_{\text{max}}$), then decays gradually with an extended tail toward longer wavelengths. Drawing a symmetric curve loses marks.

### 5.1 Pre-Computed Numerical Constants (Competitive Speed Hacks)

In competitive environments (NEET / JEE Main), computing $(6.626 \times 10^{-34}) \times (3 \times 10^8)$ repeatedly burns valuable minutes. Memorize these composite products:

#### 1. The $hc$ Product:

$$
hc \approx 1.9878 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{m} \approx \mathbf{2 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{m}}\quad (\text{for quick estimation})
$$

#### 2. The $hc$ Product in Electron-Volts:
When $\lambda$ is provided in nanometers ($\text{nm}$) or Angstroms ($\text{Å}$):

$$
\boxed{E\ (\text{in eV}) \approx \frac{1240}{\lambda\ (\text{in nm})}} \quad \text{or} \quad \boxed{E\ (\text{in eV}) \approx \frac{12400}{\lambda\ (\text{in Å})}}
$$

*(Accurate to within $0.2\%$, ideal for rapid MCQs).*

#### 3. The Molar Einstein Constant ($N_A \times hc$):

$$
N_A hc \approx (6.022 \times 10^{23}) \times (1.9878 \times 10^{-25}) = \mathbf{0.1197\ \text{J}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} \approx \mathbf{1.2 \times 10^{-1}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

$$
\boxed{E_{\text{mole}}\ (\text{kJ mol}^{-1}) \approx \frac{1.2 \times 10^5}{\lambda\ (\text{in nm})}}
$$
