#### Question A1: Industrial Spectroscopy and Fireworks Pyrotechnics
**Tag:** [CBSE Competency Pattern, Case-Based, 4 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Passage:**  
When pyrotechnic aerial shells explode, metal salts vaporize and dissociate into gaseous atoms or ions. Thermal energy promotes bound electrons into transient excited quantum levels. Upon radiative relaxation to lower electronic manifolds, monochromatic photons are emitted according to the Planck–Einstein relation:

$$
\Delta E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

In a pyrotechnic manufacturing lab, an analytical chemist analyzes two unknown display formulation batches ($\text{Batch X}$ and $\text{Batch Y}$) using emission flame atomic spectroscopy.
- $\text{Batch X}$ produces a bright crimson red emission with a predominant spectral line at $\lambda_1 = 670.8\text{ nm}$.
- $\text{Batch Y}$ generates an intense yellow-amber emission with a doublet centroid at $\lambda_2 = 589.2\text{ nm}$.
- Meanwhile, an automated UV curing process at the assembly facility utilizes a solid-state laser generating a beam of radiation at $\lambda_3 = 254\text{ nm}$ with an operational power output of $45\text{ W}$.

```
Physical Constants:
h = 6.626 × 10^-34 J·s
c = 3.0 × 10^8 m·s^-1
N_A = 6.022 × 10^23 mol^-1
```

**Sub-Questions:**
1. Identify the alkali metal cation responsible for the characteristic emission in $\text{Batch Y}$ and determine the transition energy per emitted photon in electron volts ($\text{eV}$) ($1\text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$).
2. Calculate the total energy emitted per mole of excited atoms relaxing in $\text{Batch X}$ at $\lambda_1 = 670.8\text{ nm}$ in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
3. Calculate the number of photons emitted per second by the $45\text{ W}$ solid-state laser operating at $254\text{ nm}$.  
   *OR*  
   Explain why continuous heating of an incandescent tungsten filament produces a continuous blackbody spectrum, whereas vaporized atomic sodium in pyrotechnics yields discrete spectral lines.

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Part 1:**
- **Identification:** The characteristic yellow-amber emission doublet at $589.2\text{ nm}$ corresponds to the $3p \to 3s$ electronic relaxation of **Sodium ($\text{Na}^+$ or atomic $\text{Na}$)**. **[½ Mark]**
- **Calculation of Energy per photon:**
  

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda_2} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s} \times 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{589.2 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}}
$$

  

$$
E = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-25}}{589.2 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.374 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

 **[½ Mark]**
- **Conversion to $\text{eV}$:**
  

$$
E(\text{eV}) = \frac{3.374 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}\cdot\text{eV}^{-1}} = 2.106\text{ eV}
$$

 **[½ Mark]**

**Part 2:**
- **Energy per photon ($\text{Batch X}$):**
  

$$
E_1 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_1} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s} \times 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{670.8 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}} = 2.963 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

- **Molar Energy ($E_{\text{molar}}$):**
  

$$
E_{\text{molar}} = E_1 \times N_A = 2.963 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1} = 1.784 \times 10^5\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

  

$$
E_{\text{molar}} = 178.4\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark for formula & substitution, ½ mark for correct answer with $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$]**

**Part 3:**
- **Laser Energy Calculation:**
  Power $P = 45\text{ W} = 45\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$.
  Energy of a single photon at $254\text{ nm}$:
  

$$
E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda_3} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{254 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}} = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-25}}{254 \times 10^{-9}} = 7.826 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

 **[½ Mark]**
  Number of photons emitted per second ($n$):
  

$$
n = \frac{P}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{45\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{7.826 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}} = 5.75 \times 10^{19}\text{ photons}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}
$$

 **[½ Mark]**

  *OR (Alternative Part 3):*
- In an incandescent solid tungsten filament, atoms are strongly packed and thermally coupled; molecular and lattice vibrational-rotational modes broaden the emission over a wide continuum (blackbody radiation). **[½ Mark]**
- In vaporized atomic gas, isolated atoms have unperturbed, discrete, quantized electronic energy eigenstates ($E_n \propto -1/n^2$). Transitions between these quantized levels emit photons of strictly discrete, definite frequencies ($\nu = \Delta E/h$), yielding line spectra. **[½ Mark]**

#### Question A2: Photoelectric Threshold in Spacecraft Photovoltaic Sensors
**Tag:** [CBSE Competency Pattern, Case-Based, 4 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

**Passage:**  
Outer-space orbital probes utilize specialized photoelectric sensor targets for electrostatic charge dissipation and attitude control. When radiant electromagnetic flux strikes a clean metal surface inside an ultra-high vacuum environment, photoelectrons are ejected only when the incident radiant frequency $\nu$ satisfies $\nu \ge \nu_0$, where $\nu_0$ is the metal's threshold frequency. The photoelectric process follows Einstein’s photoelectric equation:

$$
h\nu = W_0 + K_{\text{max}} = h\nu_0 + \frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\text{max}}^2 = h\nu_0 + eV_0
$$

where $W_0$ is the work function, $K_{\text{max}}$ is the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electron, and $V_0$ is the stopping potential.

A sensor payload test bench evaluates three metallic surfaces:
- **Cesium ($\text{Cs}$):** $W_0 = 2.14\text{ eV}$
- **Potassium ($\text{K}$):** $W_0 = 2.30\text{ eV}$
- **Platinum ($\text{Pt}$):** $W_0 = 5.65\text{ eV}$

The surface is irradiated with monochromatic radiation of wavelength $\lambda = 300\text{ nm}$.

**Sub-Questions:**
1. Which of the metals will exhibit photoelectric emission under this incident radiation? Justify by calculating the energy of the incident photon in $\text{eV}$.
2. Determine the maximum de Broglie wavelength ($\lambda_{\text{dB}}$) of photoelectrons ejected from the Cesium plate. ($m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$)
3. What will happen to (i) the stopping potential ($V_0$) and (ii) the photocurrent if the intensity of the incident radiation is tripled while keeping the wavelength constant?

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Part 1:**
- **Incident Photon Energy ($E$):**
  

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s} \times 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{300 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}} = 6.626 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

  

$$
E(\text{eV}) = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}\cdot\text{eV}^{-1}} \approx 4.136\text{ eV}
$$

 **[½ Mark]**
- **Criterion:** Photoelectric emission occurs if $E_{\text{photon}} \ge W_0$.
  - For $\text{Cs}$: $4.136\text{ eV} > 2.14\text{ eV}$ (Photoelectric emission occurs).
  - For $\text{K}$: $4.136\text{ eV} > 2.30\text{ eV}$ (Photoelectric emission occurs).
  - For $\text{Pt}$: $4.136\text{ eV} < 5.65\text{ eV}$ (No photoelectric emission).
  **Conclusion:** Only **Cesium ($\text{Cs}$)** and **Potassium ($\text{K}$)** will emit photoelectrons. **[½ Mark]**

**Part 2:**
- **Maximum Kinetic Energy for $\text{Cs}$:**
  

$$
K_{\text{max}} = E - W_0 = 4.136\text{ eV} - 2.14\text{ eV} = 1.996\text{ eV}
$$

  

$$
K_{\text{max}} = 1.996 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 3.198 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

 **[½ Mark]**
- **de Broglie wavelength ($\lambda_{\text{dB}}$):**
  

$$
\lambda_{\text{dB}} = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_e K_{\text{max}}}}
$$

  

$$
p = \sqrt{2 \times (9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}) \times (3.198 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J})} = \sqrt{5.826 \times 10^{-49}} = 7.633 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}
$$

  

$$
\lambda_{\text{dB}} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}}{7.633 \times 10^{-25}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}} = 8.68 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.868\text{ nm}\text{ (or } 8.68\text{ Å)}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark formula, ½ mark correct calculation]**

**Part 3:**
- (i) **Stopping Potential ($V_0$):** Remains **unchanged**. $V_0$ depends strictly on photon frequency/energy ($\nu$) and work function ($W_0$), which are constant: $eV_0 = h\nu - W_0$. **[½ Mark]**
- (ii) **Photocurrent:** Increases **three-fold (linearly proportional)**. Tripling the radiant intensity triples the number of incident photons per unit area per second, proportionally tripling the rate of photoelectron ejection. **[½ Mark]**

#### Question A3
**Tag:** [CBSE New Pattern A-R, 1 Mark]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

- **Assertion (A):** The ground-state electronic configuration of Chromium ($Z = 24$) is $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^5\,4s^1$ rather than $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^4\,4s^2$.
- **Reason (R):** Half-filled ($d^5$) and fully filled ($d^{10}$) subshells possess symmetrical spatial distribution of charge and maximize exchange energy, conferring extra thermodynamic stability.

**Model Solution:**
- **Answer:** **(a)** Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A). **[1 Mark]**
- **Explanation:** In the $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^5\,4s^1$ configuration, five electrons with parallel spins occupy five degenerate $3d$ orbitals. The number of possible stabilizing quantum-mechanical exchange pairs is:
  

$$
{}^5C_2 = \frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10
$$

  For $3d^4$, exchange pairs would be only ${}^4C_2 = 6$. The large exchange energy and symmetric charge distribution overcome the small $4s \to 3d$ promotion energy.

#### Question A4
**Tag:** [CBSE New Pattern A-R, 1 Mark]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

- **Assertion (A):** It is fundamentally impossible to define a fixed, circular, deterministic classical trajectory (orbit) for an electron in an atom.
- **Reason (R):** According to Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle, the simultaneous precise determination of both the exact position and momentum of a subatomic particle is impossible ($\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$).

**Model Solution:**
- **Answer:** **(a)** Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A). **[1 Mark]**
- **Explanation:** A classical trajectory requires precise simultaneous knowledge of coordinates ($x, y, z$) and momentum components ($p_x, p_y, p_z$) at every point in time. Because $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$, defining trajectory for an electron is impossible. Thus, Bohr's planar circular orbits are physically invalid and replaced by three-dimensional probability distributions (orbitals).

#### Question A5
**Tag:** [CBSE New Pattern A-R, 1 Mark]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

- **Assertion (A):** In a hydrogen atom, the $3s$, $3p$, and $3d$ orbitals all possess identical energy.
- **Reason (R):** The energy of an electron in any multielectron atom or ion depends solely on the principal quantum number $n$.

**Model Solution:**
- **Answer:** **(c)** (A) is true but (R) is false. **[1 Mark]**
- **Explanation:**
  - For single-electron systems (e.g., $\text{H}$, $\text{He}^+$, $\text{Li}^{2+}$), the potential energy is governed strictly by the Coulombic attraction between the single electron and the nucleus. There are no interelectronic repulsive interactions or screening/shielding effects. Hence, energy depends exclusively on $n$: $E_n = -R_H \frac{Z^2}{n^2}$. Thus, $E(3s) = E(3p) = E(3d)$ (degenerate).
  - For multielectron atoms, electron-electron repulsions break this degeneracy; the orbital energy depends on both $n$ and $l$ (governed by the $(n+l)$ rule). Hence, (R) is completely false.

#### Question A6
**Tag:** [CBSE New Pattern A-R, 1 Mark]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐ [Core]

- **Assertion (A):** The total number of angular nodes exhibited by a $4d_{xy}$ orbital is 2, while the number of radial nodes is 1.
- **Reason (R):** For any atomic orbital, the number of angular nodes equals the azimuthal quantum number $l$, and the number of radial nodes is given by the expression $(n - l - 1)$.

**Model Solution:**
- **Answer:** **(a)** Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A). **[1 Mark]**
- **Explanation:**
  - For a $4d_{xy}$ orbital: $n = 4$, $l = 2$.
  - Angular nodes $= l = 2$ (the nodal planes are $xz$ and $yz$).
  - Radial nodes $= n - l - 1 = 4 - 2 - 1 = 1$.
  - Total nodes $= (n - 1) = 4 - 1 = 3$. Both statements are correct and (R) directly defines the analytical formulas used in (A).

#### Question A7: Multi-Concept Photo-Ionization & Wave-Particle Duality
**Tag:** [CBSE Competency HOTS, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Problem:**  
A stationary hydrogen atom in its electronic ground state ($n = 1$) absorbs an extreme ultraviolet (EUV) photon having a wavelength of $\lambda = 65.63\text{ nm}$. The photon ejects the ground-state electron via photo-ionization.
1. Calculate the threshold energy (ionization enthalpy) of atomic hydrogen in Joules.
2. Determine the kinetic energy of the ejected photoelectron in Joules.
3. Determine the de Broglie wavelength ($\lambda_{\text{dB}}$) of the ejected photoelectron.

```
Given:
R_H = 2.18 × 10^-18 J
h = 6.626 × 10^-34 J·s
c = 3.0 × 10^8 m·s^-1
m_e = 9.109 × 10^-31 kg
```

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Step 1: Calculate Ionization Energy ($E_{\text{threshold}}$):**
- The ionization energy represents the energy required to transition the electron from $n=1$ to $n=\infty$:
  

$$
E_1 = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}\cdot\frac{1^2}{1^2} = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}
$$

  

$$
E_{\text{threshold}} = \Delta E = E_\infty - E_1 = 0 - (-2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}) = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark formula, ½ mark value]**

**Step 2: Incident Photon Energy & Photoelectron Kinetic Energy:**
- Energy of the incident photon ($E_{\text{photon}}$):
  

$$
E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s} \times 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{65.63 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}} = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-25}}{65.63 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.0287 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}
$$

- According to Einstein’s photoelectric equation:
  

$$
K_e = E_{\text{photon}} - E_{\text{threshold}}
$$

  

$$
K_e = 3.0287 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J} - 2.1800 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J} = 8.487 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark for subtraction, ½ mark for correct kinetic energy]**

**Step 3: de Broglie Wavelength of the Ejected Electron:**
- Momentum of the photoelectron:
  

$$
p = \sqrt{2 m_e K_e} = \sqrt{2 \times (9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}) \times (8.487 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J})}
$$

  

$$
p = \sqrt{1.546 \times 10^{-48}} = 1.243 \times 10^{-24}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}
$$

- de Broglie wavelength:
  

$$
\lambda_{\text{dB}} = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}}{1.243 \times 10^{-24}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}} = 5.33 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.533\text{ nm}\text{ (or } 5.33\text{ Å)}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark for formula, ½ mark for final answer with units]**

#### Question A8: The Uncertainty Principle at Macroscopic vs. Subatomic Scales
**Tag:** [CBSE Competency HOTS, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

**Problem:**  
To establish why quantum mechanics is required for atomic systems but classical mechanics suffices for macro-objects, solve the following comparative problem:
1. A cricket ball of mass $m_1 = 0.15\text{ kg}$ is pitched with a velocity of $40\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$. If its velocity can be measured with an accuracy of $2\%$, calculate the minimum uncertainty in its position ($\Delta x_1$).
2. An electron ($m_2 = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$) is accelerated in an electron microscope to a speed of $6.0 \times 10^6\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$, measured with the same accuracy of $2\%$. Calculate the uncertainty in its position ($\Delta x_2$).
3. Compare $\Delta x_2$ to the typical diameter of an atom ($\approx 10^{-10}\text{ m}$) and state the physical implication.

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Part 1: Macroscopic Ball:**
- Velocity uncertainty ($\Delta v_1$):
  

$$
\Delta v_1 = 40 \times \frac{2}{100} = 0.8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}
$$

- Applying Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle:
  

$$
\Delta x_1 \ge \frac{h}{4\pi m_1 \Delta v_1} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}}{4 \times 3.1416 \times 0.15\text{ kg} \times 0.8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{1.508} = 4.39 \times 10^{-34}\text{ m}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark formula, ½ mark calculation]**

**Part 2: Subatomic Electron:**
- Velocity uncertainty ($\Delta v_2$):
  

$$
\Delta v_2 = 6.0 \times 10^6 \times \frac{2}{100} = 1.2 \times 10^5\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}
$$

- Position uncertainty:
  

$$
\Delta x_2 \ge \frac{h}{4\pi m_2 \Delta v_2} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}}{4 \times 3.1416 \times (9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}) \times (1.2 \times 10^5\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1})}
$$

  

$$
\Delta x_2 = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{1.3736 \times 10^{-24}} = 4.82 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.482\text{ nm}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark substitution, ½ mark final answer]**

**Part 3: Comparative Analysis & Physical Consequence:**
- $\Delta x_1 = 4.39 \times 10^{-34}\text{ m}$ is negligible compared to the size of a cricket ball ($\sim 10^{-1}\text{ m}$); thus, wave nature and quantum uncertainties have no physical relevance at the macroscopic scale. **[½ Mark]**
- $\Delta x_2 = 4.82 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$ is approximately five times larger than the atomic diameter ($\approx 10^{-10}\text{ m}$). Consequently, the position of an electron is spread over the atomic dimensions, confirming that precise classical trajectory calculations are invalid and the quantum-probabilistic orbital model is required. **[½ Mark]**

#### Question B1: Transition Equivalence in Hydrogen-like Species
**Tag:** [NCERT Exercise Q2.16, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Problem:**  
What is the wavelength of light emitted when the electron in a hydrogen atom undergoes transition from an energy level with $n = 4$ to an energy level with $n = 2$? What is the wavenumber corresponding to this transition?

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

- **Governing Equation (Rydberg Formula):**
  

$$
\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right)
$$

  where $R_H = 109,677\text{ cm}^{-1} = 1.097 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$, $n_1 = 2$, $n_2 = 4$. **[½ Mark]**
- **Substitution and Wavenumber Calculation:**
  

$$
\bar{\nu} = 1.09677 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1} \times \left( \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{4^2} \right)
$$

  

$$
\bar{\nu} = 1.09677 \times 10^7 \times \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{16} \right) = 1.09677 \times 10^7 \times \left( \frac{3}{16} \right)
$$

  

$$
\bar{\nu} = 2.0564 \times 10^6\text{ m}^{-1}\text{ (or } 20564.4\text{ cm}^{-1}\text{)}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark substitution, ½ mark answer]**
- **Wavelength Calculation:**
  

$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{\bar{\nu}} = \frac{1}{2.0564 \times 10^6\text{ m}^{-1}} = 4.863 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} = 486.3\text{ nm}\text{ (or } 4863\text{ Å)}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark formula, ½ mark answer with units]**
- **Spectral Region Identification:** This emission corresponds to the blue-green line ($H_\beta$) in the **Balmer Series** (Visible spectrum). **[½ Mark]**

#### Question B2: Quantum Energy Level Equivalence Between $\text{He}^+$ and $\text{H}$
**Tag:** [NCERT Exercise Q2.17, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Problem:**  
Calculate the wavenumber for the longest wavelength transition in the Balmer series of atomic hydrogen. Also, determine which transition in the spectrum of singly ionized helium ($\text{He}^+$) has the same wavelength as this Balmer transition of hydrogen.

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Part 1: Longest wavelength transition in Balmer series of Hydrogen:**
- The Balmer series has $n_1 = 2$.
- Longest wavelength corresponds to the minimum energy difference ($\Delta E_{\text{min}}$), which occurs from the adjacent energy level: $n_2 = 3$. **[½ Mark]**
- Calculating wavenumber:
  

$$
\bar{\nu}_H = R_H \cdot Z_H^2 \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) = R_H (1)^2 \left( \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2} \right)
$$

  

$$
\bar{\nu}_H = R_H \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9} \right) = R_H \left( \frac{5}{36} \right) = \frac{5}{36} R_H
$$

  

$$
\bar{\nu}_H = \frac{5}{36} \times 109677\text{ cm}^{-1} = 15232.9\text{ cm}^{-1} = 1.5233 \times 10^6\text{ m}^{-1}
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark substitution, ½ mark final wavenumber]**

**Part 2: Finding the equivalent transition in $\text{He}^+$:**
- For $\text{He}^+$, $Z = 2$.
  

$$
\bar{\nu}_{\text{He}^+} = R_H \cdot Z_{\text{He}^+}^2 \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) = R_H \cdot (2)^2 \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) = 4 R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right)
$$

- Equating $\bar{\nu}_{\text{He}^+}$ to $\bar{\nu}_H$:
  

$$
4 R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) = R_H \left( \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2} \right)
$$

  

$$
\left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) = \frac{1}{4} \left( \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2} \right) = \left( \frac{1}{(2 \times 2)^2} - \frac{1}{(2 \times 3)^2} \right) = \left( \frac{1}{4^2} - \frac{1}{6^2} \right)
$$

 **[1 Mark: Algebraic deduction of quantum numbers]**
- Thus, comparing terms gives:

#### Question B5: Quantum Numbers and Orbital Filling Rules
**Tag:** [NCERT Exercise Q2.28 & Q2.30, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Problem:**  
1. An atom has $2K$, $8L$, and $11M$ electrons. Write down its complete ground-state electronic configuration and determine the number of unpaired electrons in the ground state.
2. How many subshells are associated with principal quantum number $n = 4$? How many electrons will have $m_s = -\frac{1}{2}$ for $n = 4$?
3. Arrange the following orbitals in order of increasing energy according to the Aufbau Principle:
   

$$
4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d
$$

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Part 1:**
- Total number of electrons $Z = 2 + 8 + 11 = 21$ (Scandium, $\text{Sc}$). **[½ Mark]**
- Electronic configuration:
  

$$
1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,3d^1\,4s^2 \quad \text{or} \quad [\text{Ar}]\,3d^1\,4s^2
$$

- Unpaired electrons: Orbitals up to $3p^6$ are fully paired; $4s^2$ is fully paired; $3d$ has $1$ electron.  
  Number of unpaired electrons = **1**. **[½ Mark]**

**Part 2:**
- For $n = 4$:
  - Number of subshells $= n = 4$ (specifically: $l = 0\,(s), 1\,(p), 2\,(d), 3\,(f)$). **[½ Mark]**
  - Total number of orbitals $= n^2 = 4^2 = 16$.
  - Total maximum electron capacity $= 2n^2 = 2(16) = 32$.
  - Since each orbital accommodates two electrons with opposite spins ($m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$ and $m_s = -\frac{1}{2}$), exactly half of the electrons will have $m_s = -\frac{1}{2}$.
  - Number of electrons with $m_s = -\frac{1}{2} = 16$. **[½ Mark]**

**Part 3: Increasing Order of Energy via $(n + l)$ Rule:**
- Calculate $(n + l)$:
  - For $4d$: $n=4, l=2 \implies (n+l) = 6$
  - For $5p$: $n=5, l=1 \implies (n+l) = 6$
  - For $6s$: $n=6, l=0 \implies (n+l) = 6$
  - For $4f$: $n=4, l=3 \implies (n+l) = 7$
  - For $5d$: $n=5, l=2 \implies (n+l) = 7$
- Rule: Orbitals with lower $(n+l)$ have lower energy. If two orbitals have equal $(n+l)$, the orbital with lower $n$ has lower energy.
  - For $(n+l)=6$: $4d < 5p < 6s$
  - For $(n+l)=7$: $4f < 5d$
- **Correct Order:**
  

$$
4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d

**Problem:**  
Which of the following sets of quantum numbers represents an impossible state for an electron in an isolated ground or excited state atom? Justify each determination.
- **(A)** $n = 3,\, l = 2,\, m_l = -2,\, m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$
- **(B)** $n = 4,\, l = 0,\, m_l = 1,\, m_s = -\frac{1}{2}$
- **(C)** $n = 2,\, l = 2,\, m_l = 0,\, m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$
- **(D)** $n = 5,\, l = 3,\, m_l = -3,\, m_s = -\frac{1}{2}$

What is the maximum number of degenerate orbitals in a subshell specified by azimuthal quantum number $l$?

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

- **Evaluation of Sets:**
  - **Set (A):** Valid. For $n=3$, $l$ can be $0, 1, 2$. Here $l=2$ ($3d$ orbital). $m_l$ can take values from $-2$ to $+2$. $m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$. Permissible.
  - **Set (B):** **Impossible / Invalid.** For $l = 0$ ($s$-subshell), the magnetic quantum number can only be $m_l = 0$. $m_l = 1$ violates the boundary condition $-l \le m_l \le +l$. **[½ Mark]**
  - **Set (C):** **Impossible / Invalid.** The azimuthal quantum number $l$ can only adopt values $0 \le l \le (n - 1)$. For $n = 2$, maximum permitted $l = 1$ ($2s, 2p$). A value of $l = 2$ ($2d$) is physically non-existent. **[½ Mark]**
  - **Set (D):** Valid. Represents a $5f$ orbital ($n=5, l=3, m_l=-3$). Permissible.
- **Degeneracy of a Subshell:**
  The number of degenerate orbitals within any subshell defined by $l$ equals:
  

$$
\text{Degenerate orbitals} = (2l + 1)
$$

 **[1 Mark: Explicit relation and justification]**

#### Question C2: Isoelectronic Radii and Electronic Mechanics
**Tag:** [NCERT Exemplar Analytical, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Problem:**  
Consider the following chemical species:

$$
\text{N}^{3-},\; \text{O}^{2-},\; \text{F}^{-},\; \text{Ne},\; \text{Na}^{+},\; \text{Mg}^{2+},\; \text{Al}^{3+}
$$

1. What do these species have in common? Explicitly show this by determining their total number of electrons.
2. Arrange these species in strictly increasing order of their ionic/atomic radii.
3. Provide a rigorous physical explanation for the observed radius trend in terms of effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) and the electron-to-proton ($e/p$) ratio.

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Part 1: Isoelectronic Character:**
- Counting total electrons ($e^-$):
  - $\text{N}^{3-}: 7 + 3 = 10$
  - $\text{O}^{2-}: 8 + 2 = 10$
  - $\text{F}^{-}: 9 + 1 = 10$
  - $\text{Ne}: 10$
  - $\text{Na}^{+}: 11 - 1 = 10$
  - $\text{Mg}^{2+}: 12 - 2 = 10$
  - $\text{Al}^{3+}: 13 - 3 = 10$
- **Identification:** All the species possess identical total numbers of electrons ($10\text{ }e^-$ with ground state $[\text{Ne}] = 1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6$) and are therefore **isoelectronic**. **[1 Mark: ½ mark count, ½ mark definition]**

**Part 2: Sequence of Increasing Radii:**

$$
\text{Al}^{3+} < \text{Mg}^{2+} < \text{Na}^{+} < \text{Ne} < \text{F}^{-} < \text{O}^{2-} < \text{N}^{3-}
$$

 **[1 Mark: Deduct ½ mark for any inverted pair]**

**Part 3: Physical Explanation:**
- In an isoelectronic series, the number of screening core electrons is identical ($10\text{ }e^-$), but the nuclear charge ($Z$, number of protons) increases progressively from $Z = 7$ ($\text{N}$) to $Z = 13$ ($\text{Al}$).
- The effective nuclear charge experienced by the valence $2p$ electrons increases steadily:
  

$$
Z_{\text{eff}} \propto Z
$$

  - For $\text{Al}^{3+}$: $13\text{ protons}$ attract $10\text{ electrons}$ ($e/p = 0.77$). The nucleus pulls the electronic cloud inward, resulting in the smallest ionic radius.
  - For $\text{N}^{3-}$: Only $7\text{ protons}$ hold $10\text{ electrons}$ ($e/p = 1.43$). The lower effective nuclear charge and greater interelectronic repulsions cause cloud expansion, resulting in the largest radius. **[1 Mark: Mentioning $Z_{\text{eff}}$, nuclear pull, and $e/p$ ratio]**

#### Question C3: Radial Probability Distribution Functions and Nodal Surfaces
**Tag:** [NCERT Exemplar Subjective Challenge, 5 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Problem:**  
The radial distribution function $4\pi r^2 R_{n,l}^2(r)$ describes the probability of finding an electron in a spherical shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ centered on the nucleus.
1. Plot the schematic curves of the radial probability distribution function $4\pi r^2 R^2(r)$ versus $r$ for:
   - (i) $1s$ orbital
   - (ii) $2s$ orbital
   - (iii) $2p$ orbital
2. For the $2s$ orbital, label the position of the radial node on the sketch and state its mathematical condition in terms of the radial wavefunction $R(r)$.
3. Explain why the radial probability density $4\pi r^2 R^2(r)$ is zero at $r = 0$ (the nucleus) for all orbitals, whereas the probability density $\psi^2(r)$ for an $s$-orbital is maximum at $r = 0$.
4. Calculate the total number of nodal surfaces (planes and spheres combined) present in a $5d_{z^2}$ orbital.

***

**Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:**

**Part 1: Schematic Sketches of Radial Probability Distribution Functions:**

```
(i) 1s Orbital (No radial node):
 4πr²R²
   ^
   |       /\
   |      /  \
   |     /    \
   |    /      \
   +---/--------\---------> r
   0   r_max = a₀ (52.9 pm)

(ii) 2s Orbital (One radial node):
 4πr²R²
   ^
   |        Radial node [R(r) = 0]
   |          |
   |     /\   |     /---\
   |    /  \  v    /     \
   +---/----\-----/-------\---> r
   0   Small peak  Main peak

(iii) 2p Orbital (No radial node):
 4πr²R²
   ^
   |         /\
   |        /  \
   |       /    \
   +------/------\--------> r
   0      r_max
```
**[1½ Marks: ½ mark for each accurate schematic highlighting relative peak heights and node existence]**

**Part 2: Condition for Radial Node:**
- A radial node is a spherical boundary surface where the probability of finding the electron is zero.
- **Mathematical condition:**
  

$$
R_{n,l}(r) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \psi(r) = 0 \quad (\text{for } 0 < r < \infty)
$$

- For a $2s$ orbital, the number of radial nodes is $n - l - 1 = 2 - 0 - 1 = 1$. **[1 Mark: Definition and mathematical condition]**

**Part 3: Resolution of the $r = 0$ Apparent Paradox:**
- The probability density per unit volume is given by $|\psi(r)|^2$. For $s$-orbitals ($l=0$), the wave function does not vanish at the nucleus; it has a finite non-zero value, making $|\psi(0)|^2$ a maximum at $r = 0$. **[½ Mark]**
- The radial probability represents the probability of locating the electron inside a thin spherical shell of volume $dV = 4\pi r^2 dr$:
  

$$
P(r)\,dr = 4\pi r^2 |\psi(r)|^2 dr
$$

- As $r \to 0$, the spherical shell volume factor $4\pi r^2 \to 0$. Therefore:
  

$$
\lim_{r \to 0} \left[ 4\pi r^2 |\psi(r)|^2 \right] = 0 \times |\psi(0)|^2 = 0
$$

- Thus, although the probability *density* is high at the nucleus, the physical volume of a point is zero, causing the integrated radial *probability* to be zero at $r = 0$. **[1 Mark: Clear mathematical differentiation between density $\psi^2$ and volume element $4\pi r^2 dr$]**

**Part 4: Calculation of Nodes for $5d_{z^2}$:**
- $n = 5$, $l = 2$.
- Number of Radial Nodes:
  

$$
\text{Radial nodes} = n - l - 1 = 5 - 2 - 1 = 2
$$

- Number of Angular Nodes:
  

$$
\text{Angular nodes} = l = 2
$$

  *(For $d_{z^2}$, these appear as two conical nodal surfaces).*
- Total number of nodes:
  

$$
\text{Total nodes} = \text{Radial} + \text{Angular} = (n - l - 1) + l = n - 1 = 5 - 1 = 4
$$

 **[1 Mark: ½ mark for component formulas, ½ mark for final count]**
