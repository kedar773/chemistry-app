# 1. Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$)

### Conceptual Definition & Physical Law
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

When an electron is added to a neutral, isolated, gaseous atom in its electronic ground state to convert it into a gaseous negative ion (uninegative anion), the enthalpy change accompanying the process is defined as the **Electron Gain Enthalpy** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$).

$$
\text{X}(g) + e^{-} \xrightarrow{T,\,P} \text{X}^{-}(g) \quad (\Delta H = \Delta_{\text{eg}}H)
$$

* **Thermodynamic Sign Convention:**
  * If energy is released when an electron is added, the process is **exothermic**, and $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ takes a **negative sign** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H < 0$).
  * If energy must be supplied to force an electron into an unstable or already negatively charged species, the process is **endothermic**, and $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ takes a **positive sign** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H > 0$).
* **SI Unit:** $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ or $\text{eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1}$ ($1\text{ eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1} \approx 96.485\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).

***

### Electron Affinity ($EA$) vs. Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

Historically, the ease of adding an electron was quantified as **Electron Affinity** ($EA$), defined as the negative of the internal energy change ($\Delta U$) at absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$):

$$
EA_0 = -\Delta U_0
$$

Using standard thermodynamic relations for an ideal gas reaction:

$$
\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta(PV) = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT
$$

For the addition of an electron:

$$
\text{X}(g) + e^{-}(g) \longrightarrow \text{X}^{-}(g)
$$

Here, $\Delta n_g = 1 - (1 + 1) = -1$ (treating the electron gas as an ideal monatomic gas):

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H = \Delta U - RT
$$

Since $EA = -\Delta U$:

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -EA - RT
$$

At room temperature ($T = 298.15\text{ K}$):

$$
\frac{5}{2}RT \approx 6.197\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Taking into account the translational kinetic energy of the free electron gas ($\frac{5}{2}RT$ term in statistical thermodynamics):

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -EA - \frac{5}{2}RT
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \approx -EA - 2.48\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \quad (\text{at } 298.15\text{ K})}
$$

* **Conclusion:** When an atom has a high positive electron affinity ($EA > 0$, strong attraction for electrons), its electron gain enthalpy is **large and negative** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \ll 0$).

***

### Successive Electron Gain Enthalpies
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **First Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_1$):**
   Usually **negative** (exothermic) for most neutral non-metals, because the incoming electron experiences a net electrostatic attraction from the nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}}$.
   

$$
\text{O}(g) + e^{-} \longrightarrow \text{O}^{-}(g) \quad \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_1 = -141\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

2. **Second Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2$):**
   **Always positive** (endothermic) for all elements without exception:
   

$$
\text{O}^{-}(g) + e^{-} \longrightarrow \text{O}^{2-}(g) \quad \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2 = +780\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Net Enthalpy for }\text{O}^{2-}(g): \Delta H_{\text{total}} = \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_1 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2 = -141 + 780 = +639\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

* **Physical Origin:** The second electron must be added against the strong electrostatic repulsion of the existing uninegative ion ($\text{O}^{-}$). Work must be done *on* the system to overcome inter-electronic repulsion, requiring external energy.
* **Classroom Application Question:** *Why does $\text{O}^{2-}$ exist abundantly in ionic lattices (e.g., $\text{MgO}$) if the formation of gaseous $\text{O}^{2-}(g)$ is highly endothermic?*  
  **Answer:** The high lattice enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^{\ominus}$) resulting from the interaction of the compact divalent cation ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) and divalent anion ($\text{O}^{2-}$) via Coulomb's Law:
  

$$
U \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_0}
$$

  releases enough energy to compensate for the endothermic second electron gain enthalpy.

***

### Factors Affecting Electron Gain Enthalpy
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$):**
   

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \propto -Z_{\text{eff}}
$$

   Higher positive charge pulls the incoming electron more tightly $\implies$ greater release of energy $\implies$ more negative $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$.
2. **Atomic Radius ($r$):**
   

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \propto \frac{1}{r}
$$

   As the radius increases, the distance between the nucleus and the incoming electron increases, diminishing electrostatic attraction $\implies$ less negative $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$.
3. **Electronic Configuration (Half-filled & Fully-filled Subshells):**
   Noble gases ($ns^2 np^6$), alkaline earth metals ($ns^2$), and Group 15 elements ($ns^2 np^3$) possess extra exchange energy stability. Forcing an electron into these configurations disrupts symmetry and incurs an energy penalty.

***

### Periodic Trends and Critical Anomalies
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

* **Across a Period (Left to Right):**
  $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases, atomic size decreases. The incoming electron experiences stronger electrostatic pull. Therefore, **$\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ becomes progressively more negative**.
* **Down a Group (Top to Bottom):**
  Principal quantum number $n$ increases, atomic size increases, shielding increases. The incoming electron enters a shell farther from the nucleus. Therefore, **$\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ becomes progressively less negative**.

```
[Noble Gases]       --> Completely filled valence shells (s²p⁶) --> ΔegH is LARGE POSITIVE
[Group 2: Be, Mg]   --> Stable s² configuration                 --> ΔegH is POSITIVE or NEAR ZERO
[Group 15: N, P]    --> Stable half-filled p³ configuration     --> N has positive/near zero ΔegH
[Period 2 vs. 3]    --> Small size of 2p causes high repulsion   --> Cl > F, S > O, P > N
```

#### Anomaly 1: The Group 17 Halogen Anomaly ($\text{Cl}$ vs. $\text{F}$)
Experimental data:

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Cl}) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{F}) = -328\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{|\Delta_{\text{eg}}H|: \text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I}}
$$

* **Mechanism:** Fluorine has valence electrons in the compact $2p$ subshell ($n=2$). The electron cloud density in this compact volume is exceptionally high. When an incoming electron enters the $2p$ orbital of $\text{F}$, it experiences strong inter-electronic repulsions from the existing 7 valence electrons.
* In Chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger $3p$ orbital ($n=3$). The electron density is lower and spread over a larger spatial region, so inter-electronic repulsions are significantly reduced. The net attractive force from the nucleus dominates.

#### Anomaly 2: Group 16 Chalcogen Anomaly ($\text{O}$ vs. $\text{S}$)

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{S}) = -200\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Se}) = -195\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Te}) = -190\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Po}) = -174\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{O}) = -141\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Negative value order: } \text{S} > \text{Se} > \text{Te} > \text{Po} > \text{O}}
$$

Oxygen has the **least negative** $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ in Group 16 because of the severe inter-electronic repulsion in its compact $2p^4$ shell.

#### Anomaly 3: Nitrogen vs. Carbon and Oxygen
* $\text{C}\ (2s^2 2p^2): \Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -122\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
* $\text{N}\ (2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1): \Delta_{\text{eg}}H = +31\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (or $\approx +2\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ depending on reference)
* $\text{O}\ (2s^2 2p^4): \Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -141\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
* Nitrogen has a symmetric, half-filled $2p^3$ configuration with maximum exchange energy ($K = 3$). Adding an electron forces pairing into one of the half-filled orbitals ($2p_x^2 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$), which introduces Coulombic pairing energy and reduces orbital symmetry.

# 2. Electronegativity ($\chi$)

### Definition and Conceptual Basis
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

**Electronegativity ($\chi$)** is a qualitative/relative property defined as:
> *The tendency of an atom in a chemical compound to attract a shared pair of electrons towards itself.*

* Unlike Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$) or Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$), which are measurable physical quantities of **isolated gaseous atoms**, electronegativity is **not an absolute measurable physical quantity**. It has no formal physical dimensions or SI units.

***

### Quantitative Scales of Electronegativity
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

#### 1. Pauling Scale (Based on Bond Energies)
Linus Pauling (1932) derived electronegativity values from thermochemical bond dissociation energies.

For a heteronuclear diatomic molecule $\text{A}-\text{B}$, if the bond were $100\%$ purely covalent, the bond energy $E_{\text{A}-\text{B}}$ would equal the geometric mean of the homonuclear bond energies $E_{\text{A}-\text{A}}$ and $E_{\text{B}-\text{B}}$:

$$
E_{\text{A}-\text{B, theoretical}} = \sqrt{E_{\text{A}-\text{A}} \cdot E_{\text{B}-\text{B}}}
$$

Because of partial ionic character arising from the polarity $\text{A}^{\delta+}-\text{B}^{\delta-}$, the actual bond energy $E_{\text{A}-\text{B, experimental}}$ is greater than the theoretical covalent energy. The resonance ionic-covalent energy stabilization is:

$$
\Delta = E_{\text{A}-\text{B}} - \sqrt{E_{\text{A}-\text{A}} \cdot E_{\text{B}-\text{B}}}
$$

Pauling related this excess ionic resonance energy $\Delta$ to the electronegativity difference $|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}|$:

$$
\boxed{|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}| = 0.208 \sqrt{\Delta} \quad (\text{if energies are in }\text{kcal}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})}
$$

$$
\boxed{|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}| = 0.1017 \sqrt{\Delta} \quad (\text{if energies are in }\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})}
$$

* **Arbitrary Standard Reference:** Pauling arbitrarily assigned a value of **$\chi_{\text{F}} = 4.0$** to Fluorine (the most electronegative element).

***

#### 2. Mulliken Scale (Absolute Scale)
Robert Mulliken (1934) proposed that an atom's attraction for electrons depends on both how tightly it holds its own electrons (Ionization Enthalpy) and how strongly it attracts additional electrons (Electron Affinity).

$$
\chi_{\text{Mulliken}} = \frac{IE + EA}{2} \quad (\text{where } IE \text{ and } EA \text{ are in }\text{eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1})
$$

If values are given in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$:

$$
\chi_{\text{Mulliken}} = \frac{IE + EA}{2 \times 96.485} = \frac{IE + EA}{540}
$$

**Conversion between Pauling and Mulliken Scales:**
Pauling values are smaller than Mulliken values by a factor of approximately $2.8$:

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{Pauling}} \approx \frac{\chi_{\text{Mulliken}}}{2.8} = \frac{IE + EA}{5.6} \quad (\text{in eV})}
$$

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{Pauling}} \approx \frac{IE + EA}{540 \times 2.8} \approx \frac{IE + EA}{1512} \quad (\text{in }\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})}
$$

***

#### 3. Allred-Rochow Scale (Electrostatic Force Scale)
A.L. Allred and E.G. Rochow (1958) defined electronegativity as the electrostatic force exerted by the effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) on the valence electrons at the covalent radius ($r_{\text{cov}}$):

$$
F = \frac{Z_{\text{eff}} \cdot e^2}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 \cdot r_{\text{cov}}^2}
$$

Using Slater's rules to calculate $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and setting the parameters to align with the Pauling scale:

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{AR}} = 0.359 \left( \frac{Z_{\text{eff}}}{r^2} \right) + 0.744}
$$

Where $r$ is the covalent radius in Angstroms ($\text{Å}$).

***

### Factors Influencing Electronegativity
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **Oxidation State of the Central Atom:**
   

$$
\chi \propto \text{Positive Oxidation State}
$$

   * Example: $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ has a higher effective nuclear charge and smaller ionic radius than $\text{Fe}^{2+}$. Hence:
     

$$
\chi(\text{Fe}^{3+}) > \chi(\text{Fe}^{2+})
$$

   * Example: $\text{MnO}_4^{-}$ ($\text{Mn}^{7+}$, acidic) vs. $\text{MnO}$ ($\text{Mn}^{2+}$, basic).
2. **Hybridization State:**
   Electronegativity increases directly with the percentage of $s$-character, because $s$-orbitals are more spherical, penetrative, and closer to the nucleus than $p$-orbitals:
   

$$
\% s\text{-character}: \quad sp\ (50\%) > sp^2\ (33.3\%) > sp^3\ (25\%)
$$

   

$$
\boxed{\chi: \quad \text{C}(sp) > \text{C}(sp^2) > \text{C}(sp^3)}
$$

   * Numerical values: $\text{C}(sp) \approx 3.29$, $\text{C}(sp^2) \approx 2.75$, $\text{C}(sp^3) \approx 2.48$. This explains why terminal alkynes ($\text{R}-\text{C}\equiv\text{C}-\text{H}$) are weakly acidic.

***

### Applications of Electronegativity

#### 1. Calculation of Partial Ionic Character (Hanny-Smyth Equation)
For a covalent single bond $\text{A}-\text{B}$:

$$
\boxed{\% \text{ Ionic Character} = 16|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}| + 3.5(\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}})^2}
$$

* If $|\Delta\chi| = 1.7 \implies \% \text{ Ionic Character} \approx 50\%$.
* If $|\Delta\chi| > 1.7 \implies$ Predominantly **ionic**.
* If $|\Delta\chi| < 1.7 \implies$ Predominantly **covalent**.

#### 2. Acid-Base Character of Oxides
Consider an element $\text{E}$ bonded to oxygen in the linkage $\text{E}-\text{O}-\text{H}$:
* If $|\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{E}}| > |\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{H}}|$: The bond $\text{E}-\text{O}$ cleaves heterolytically yielding $\text{E}^{+} + \text{OH}^{-}$. The substance acts as a **base**.
* If $|\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{E}}| < |\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{H}}|$: The bond $\text{O}-\text{H}$ cleaves yielding $\text{E}-\text{O}^{-} + \text{H}^{+}$. The substance acts as an **acid**.

$$
\boxed{\text{Acidic strength of oxides increases across a period: } \text{Na}_2\text{O} \text{ (strongly basic)} \to \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \text{ (amphoteric)} \to \text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7 \text{ (strongly acidic)}}
$$

# 3. Valence and Oxidation States

### Concept Differentiation
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

| Parameter | Valence (Valency) | Oxidation State / Number |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Combining capacity of an element; formal number of bonds formed. | Real or hypothetical charge an atom possesses when all bonding partners are removed as ions. |
| **Sign** | Always a pure, unsigned integer ($1, 2, 3, \dots$). | Can be positive, negative, zero, or fractional. |
| **Value in $\text{CH}_4$** | Carbon has valence = $4$ | Carbon has oxidation state = $-4$ |
| **Value in $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$** | Carbon has valence = $4$ | Carbon has oxidation state = $0$ |

***

### Periodic Trends in Valence
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **Representative Elements (s- and p-block):**
   * **Valence with respect to Hydrogen / Halogens:**
     Increases from $1$ to $4$, then decreases to $1$:
     * Group 1: $\text{LiH}$ (Valence 1)
     * Group 2: $\text{CaH}_2$ (Valence 2)
     * Group 13: $\text{AlH}_3$ (Valence 3)
     * Group 14: $\text{CH}_4$ (Valence 4)
     * Group 15: $\text{NH}_3$ (Valence 3)
     * Group 16: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Valence 2)
     * Group 17: $\text{HF}$ (Valence 1)
2. **Valence with respect to Oxygen:**
   Increases linearly from $1$ to $7$:
   * Group 1: $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$ (Valence 1)
   * Group 2: $\text{MgO}$ (Valence 2)
   * Group 13: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ (Valence 3)
   * Group 14: $\text{CO}_2$ (Valence 4)
   * Group 15: $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ (Valence 5)
   * Group 16: $\text{SO}_3$ (Valence 6)
   * Group 17: $\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$ (Valence 7)

***

### Anomalous Properties of Second Period Elements
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

The elements of the second period ($\text{Li}, \text{Be}, \text{B}, \text{C}, \text{N}, \text{O}, \text{F}$) differ significantly from their heavier group congeners due to:
1. **Extremely small atomic and ionic size.**
2. **High electronegativity and ionization enthalpy.**
3. **High charge-to-radius ratio ($\frac{e}{r}$, polarizing power).**
4. **Complete absence of vacant $d$-orbitals in the valence shell ($n=2$).**

#### Covariance and Covalency Restriction
* For Period 2 elements, the valence shell ($n=2$) contains only four orbitals ($2s, 2p_x, 2p_y, 2p_z$).
* **Maximum covalency for Period 2 elements is strictly 4.**
  * Example: Boron forms $[\text{BF}_4]^{-}$, but **cannot** form $[\text{BF}_6]^{3-}$.
  * In contrast, Aluminum ($n=3$) has accessible $3d$ orbitals and readily forms $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$.
  * Nitrogen forms $\text{NCl}_3$ and $\text{NF}_3$, but **cannot** form $\text{NCl}_5$ or $\text{NF}_5$. Phosphorus forms both $\text{PCl}_3$ and $\text{PCl}_5$.

#### $\pi$-Bonding Differences
* Period 2 elements have small, compact $p$-orbitals that can overlap laterally to form stable **$p\pi-p\pi$ multiple bonds** (e.g., $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}, \text{C}=\text{O}, \text{N}\equiv\text{N}, \text{N}=\text{O}$).
* Heavier elements (Period 3 onwards) have diffused, larger $p$-orbitals that cannot achieve effective sideways overlap. Instead, they form single $\sigma$-bonds or use $d$-orbitals for **$d\pi-p\pi$** or **$d\pi-d\pi$** bonding (e.g., $\text{R}_3\text{P}=\text{O}$, $\text{R}_2\text{S}=\text{O}$).

***

### Inert Pair Effect
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

In the heavier $p$-block elements (specifically periods 6 and 7: $\text{Tl}, \text{Pb}, \text{Bi}$), the valence $s$-electrons ($6s^2$) remain unshared and resist participation in chemical bonding.

* **Underlying Physical Origin:**
  The $6s$ electrons are preceded by filled $4f^{14}$ and $5d^{10}$ subshells. Both $f$ and $d$ orbitals have diffused spatial shapes and provide poor shielding of the nuclear charge:
  

$$
\text{Shielding efficiency: } s > p > d > f
$$

  The imperfect shielding allows the large nuclear charge ($Z = 81$ to $83$) to pull the valence $6s$ orbital closer to the nucleus (further reinforced by relativistic contraction). As a consequence, unpairing and promoting the $6s$ electrons requires an energy investment that cannot be offset by the bond energy of the two additional covalent bonds.

* **Chemical Consequences:**
  * Group 13: $\text{Tl}^{+}$ is more stable than $\text{Tl}^{3+}$. (In fact, $\text{Tl}^{3+}$ acts as a powerful **oxidizing agent**).
  * Group 14: $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ is more stable than $\text{Pb}^{4+}$. (Therefore, $\text{PbO}_2$ is a strong **oxidizing agent**: $\text{Pb}^{4+} + 2e^{-} \to \text{Pb}^{2+}$).
  * Group 14: $\text{Sn}^{2+}$ is less stable than $\text{Sn}^{4+}$. (Therefore, $\text{Sn}^{2+}$ acts as a **reducing agent**: $\text{Sn}^{2+} \to \text{Sn}^{4+} + 2e^{-}$).

# 4. Solved Exemplar & Advanced Problems

### Problem 1: Born-Haber Cycle Application to Electron Gain Enthalpies
**Question:** Calculate the electron gain enthalpy of chlorine atoms given the following standard thermodynamic data for sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$):
1. Sublimation enthalpy of $\text{Na}(s)$: $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^{\ominus} = +108.4\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
2. Ionization enthalpy of $\text{Na}(g)$: $\Delta_i H^{\ominus} = +495.8\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
3. Bond dissociation enthalpy of $\text{Cl}_2(g)$: $\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^{\ominus} = +242.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
4. Lattice enthalpy of $\text{NaCl}(s)$: $\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^{\ominus} = -788.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
5. Standard enthalpy of formation of $\text{NaCl}(s)$: $\Delta_f H^{\ominus} = -411.2\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Write the chemical equation for the standard enthalpy of formation:

$$
\text{Na}(s) + \frac{1}{2}\text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(s) \quad (\Delta_f H^{\ominus} = -411.2\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})
$$

Step 2: Construct the Born-Haber thermochemical cycle:

$$
\Delta_f H^{\ominus} = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Na}] + \Delta_i H^{\ominus}[\text{Na}] + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}_2] + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] + \Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^{\ominus}[\text{NaCl}]
$$

Step 3: Substitute the known values:

$$
-411.2 = 108.4 + 495.8 + \frac{1}{2}(242.0) + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] + (-788.0)
$$

$$
-411.2 = 108.4 + 495.8 + 121.0 - 788.0 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}]
$$

$$
-411.2 = 725.2 - 788.0 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}]
$$

$$
-411.2 = -62.8 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}]
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] = -411.2 + 62.8 = -348.4\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] = -348.4\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

### Problem 2: Electronegativity Calculation using Pauling's Formula
**Question:** The bond dissociation energies of $\text{H}-\text{H}$, $\text{Br}-\text{Br}$, and $\text{H}-\text{Br}$ bonds are $435\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, $192\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, and $368\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ respectively. Given that the Pauling electronegativity of hydrogen is $\chi_{\text{H}} = 2.1$, calculate the electronegativity of Bromine ($\chi_{\text{Br}}$).

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Compute theoretical purely covalent bond energy $E_{\text{H}-\text{Br, covalent}}$:

$$
E_{\text{covalent}} = \sqrt{E_{\text{H}-\text{H}} \cdot E_{\text{Br}-\text{Br}}} = \sqrt{435 \cdot 192} = \sqrt{83520} \approx 289.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Step 2: Calculate the ionic resonance stabilization energy $\Delta$:

$$
\Delta = E_{\text{experimental}} - E_{\text{covalent}} = 368 - 289.0 = 79.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Step 3: Apply the Pauling formula (in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$):

$$
|\chi_{\text{Br}} - \chi_{\text{H}}| = 0.1017 \times \sqrt{\Delta}
$$

$$
|\chi_{\text{Br}} - 2.1| = 0.1017 \times \sqrt{79.0} = 0.1017 \times 8.888 \approx 0.904
$$

Since Bromine is more electronegative than Hydrogen:

$$
\chi_{\text{Br}} = 2.1 + 0.904 = 3.004
$$

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{Br}} \approx 3.0}
$$

### Problem 3: Percentage Ionic Character
**Question:** Calculate the percentage ionic character of the $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond in methyl chloride ($\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$) if the electronegativities of Carbon and Chlorine are $2.5$ and $3.0$ respectively, using the Hannay-Smyth equation.

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Determine the electronegativity difference:

$$
|\Delta\chi| = |\chi_{\text{Cl}} - \chi_{\text{C}}| = 3.0 - 2.5 = 0.5
$$

Step 2: Apply the Hannay-Smyth equation:

$$
\% \text{ Ionic Character} = 16|\Delta\chi| + 3.5(\Delta\chi)^2
$$

$$
\% \text{ Ionic Character} = 16(0.5) + 3.5(0.5)^2 = 8.0 + 3.5(0.25) = 8.0 + 0.875 = 8.875\%
$$

$$
\boxed{\% \text{ Ionic Character of }\text{C}-\text{Cl} = 8.88\%}
$$

### Problem 4: Oxidation State vs. Covalency in Nitrogen Oxides (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
**Question:** In dinitrogen pentoxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$), what are the formal oxidation state and the maximum covalency of nitrogen? Explain why nitrogen does not exhibit a covalency equal to its oxidation state.

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Calculate the oxidation state of Nitrogen:

$$
2x + 5(-2) = 0 \implies 2x = 10 \implies x = +5
$$

The oxidation state of Nitrogen in $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ is **$+5$**.

Step 2: Determine the covalency from the Lewis structure:
In solid $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$, the molecule exists as $[\text{NO}_2]^{+}[\text{NO}_3]^{-}$. In the vapor phase, it exists as a symmetric molecule $\text{O}_2\text{N}-\text{O}-\text{NO}_2$. Each nitrogen atom forms:
* One single bond to the bridging oxygen atom ($\sigma$).
* One coordinate/dative bond to a terminal oxygen atom.
* One double bond ($\sigma + \pi$) to another terminal oxygen atom.
Total pairs of shared electrons per Nitrogen atom $= 4$.  
Therefore, the covalency of Nitrogen is **$4$**.

Step 3: Justification:
Nitrogen is in the second period ($2s^2 2p^3$). The maximum number of orbitals available for chemical bonding is **4** (one $2s$ and three $2p$ orbitals). Because Nitrogen lacks energetically accessible $2d$ orbitals, it cannot expand its coordination sphere or covalency beyond 4. Hence, its covalency ($4$) cannot match its oxidation state ($+5$).

$$
\boxed{\text{Oxidation State} = +5, \quad \text{Covalency} = 4}
$$

# 5. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

```
Common Mistakes & Traps:
1. Writing ΔegH for F > Cl    --> WRONG: Cl has more negative ΔegH than F (-349 vs -328 kJ/mol).
2. Forgetting the minus sign  --> WRONG: "ΔegH of Cl is 349 kJ/mol" (must specify -349 kJ/mol).
3. Confounding EA and ΔegH    --> WRONG: EA is positive for exothermicity, while ΔegH is negative.
4. Second electron gain       --> TRAP: ΔegH2 is ALWAYS POSITIVE (endothermic) for ALL atoms.
5. Max covalency of Period 2  --> TRAP: Nitrogen forms N2O5 (O.S. = +5), but its covalency is 4.
```

### Pitfall 1: Sign Conventions in $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ vs. $EA$
* **The Error:** Students write "The electron gain enthalpy of chlorine is $349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$."
* **NCERT Marking Criterion:** If the term **Enthalpy** is used, the **negative sign is mandatory** for exothermic processes.
  * Correct: $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Cl}) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  * Correct: $EA(\text{Cl}) = +349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  * Stating a positive $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ for chlorine implies an endothermic process, resulting in a zero mark.

### Pitfall 2: Ordering by Absolute Magnitude vs. Thermodynamic Value
* **The Trap:** A question asks: *"Arrange Group 17 elements in the order of increasing electron gain enthalpy."*
* **Examiner Mindset:**
  * If the question asks for **electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$)**: More negative numbers are mathematically smaller.
    

$$
\text{Cl } (-349) < \text{F } (-328) < \text{Br } (-325) < \text{I } (-295)
$$

  * If the question asks for the **tendency to gain an electron** or **magnitude of energy released ($|\Delta_{\text{eg}}H|$)**:
    

$$
\text{I} < \text{Br} < \text{F} < \text{Cl}
$$

* **Tip:** Always specify: *"In terms of magnitude of energy released: $\text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$. In terms of standard thermodynamic sign: $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Cl}) < \Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{F}) < \Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Br}) < \Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{I})$."*

### Pitfall 3: Second Electron Gain Enthalpy is Always Endothermic
* **The Error:** Students assume that because $\text{O}^{2-}$ has a stable noble-gas configuration ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$), its formation must be exothermic.
* **Reality:** The electrostatic repulsion between the incoming electron and the negatively charged ion ($\text{O}^{-}$) is larger than the attraction from the nucleus. Thus, $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2$ is **always positive (endothermic)** for every element without exception.

### Pitfall 4: Conflating Oxidation State with Covalency
* **The Error:** Claiming that Nitrogen has a covalency of $5$ in $\text{HNO}_3$ or $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$.
* **Fact:** Second period elements **cannot** exceed a covalency of 4 because they lack $d$-orbitals. The oxidation state of Nitrogen in $\text{HNO}_3$ is $+5$, but its covalency is strictly 4.

# 6. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Golden Revision Points

### 1. The "FONCl BrISCH" Electronegativity Memory Line
To compare bond polarities and oxidation numbers instantly, memorize the Pauling values using this descending order:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{F}\,(4.0) > \mathbf{O}\,(3.5) > \mathbf{N}\,(3.0) \approx \mathbf{Cl}\,(3.0) > \mathbf{Br}\,(2.8) > \mathbf{I}\,(2.5) \approx \mathbf{S}\,(2.5) \approx \mathbf{C}\,(2.5) > \mathbf{P}\,(2.1) \approx \mathbf{H}\,(2.1)}
$$

* $\text{N}$ and $\text{Cl}$ have identical Pauling electronegativities ($\approx 3.0$), but Nitrogen forms strong hydrogen bonds while Chlorine generally does not. This is because **$\text{N}$ is much smaller ($2p$) than $\text{Cl}$ ($3p$)**, giving it a significantly higher volume-charge density.

***

### 2. Period 2 vs. Period 3 Electron Gain "Step-Down" Rule
For negative electron gain enthalpy, **Period 3 elements are always more negative than their Period 2 group partners**:

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_{\text{negative}}: \quad \text{Period 3} > \text{Period 2}
$$

* Group 17: $\text{Cl} > \text{F}$
* Group 16: $\text{S} > \text{O}$
* Group 15: $\text{P} > \text{N}$
* Group 14: $\text{Si} > \text{C}$

*Exception in Group 16:* Oxygen drops all the way to the bottom of its group:

$$
\text{Negative }\Delta_{\text{eg}}H: \quad \text{S} > \text{Se} > \text{Te} > \text{Po} > \mathbf{O}
$$

***

### 3. Quick Acidic/Basic Oxide Diagnostic
For any normal oxide $\text{E}_x\text{O}_y$:
* **High Oxidation State ($\ge +5$):** Acidic (e.g., $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5, \text{CrO}_3, \text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7, \text{SO}_3, \text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7, \text{N}_2\text{O}_5$).
* **Intermediate Oxidation State ($+3, +4$):** Often Amphoteric (e.g., $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{ZnO}, \text{SnO}_2, \text{PbO}_2$).
* **Low Oxidation State ($+1, +2$):** Basic (e.g., $\text{Na}_2\text{O}, \text{CaO}, \text{BaO}, \text{MnO}$).
* **Neutral Oxides (Class 11 NCERT Non-negotiable List):**
  

$$
\boxed{\text{CO}, \quad \text{NO}, \quad \text{N}_2\text{O}, \quad \text{OF}_2 \text{ (oxygen fluoride)}}
$$

***

### 4. Group Oxidation State Boundaries
For elements of group $n$:
* **Maximum Group Oxidation State:**
  * For $s$-block: Equal to group number ($+1$ for Gp 1, $+2$ for Gp 2).
  * For $p$-block: $\text{Group Number} - 10$ (e.g., Group 15 is $+5$, Group 16 is $+6$, Group 17 is $+7$).
* **Minimum Oxidation State for $p$-block:**
  

$$
\text{Minimum O.S.} = (\text{Group Number} - 10) - 8
$$

  * Example for Sulfur (Group 16): $\text{Max} = +6$, $\text{Min} = 16 - 10 - 8 = -2$.
  * Any species in its maximum oxidation state (e.g., $\text{HNO}_3, \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4, \text{KMnO}_4$) can **only act as an oxidizing agent**.
  * Any species in its minimum oxidation state (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{S}, \text{HI}, \text{NH}_3$) can **only act as a reducing agent**.
