# 1. Historical Development of the Periodic Classification

Classification organizes the chemistry of the $118$ known elements into a systematic framework. Historically, chemists attempted to correlate elemental properties with their **atomic masses** ($A$), which was then considered the most fundamental property of matter.

***

### A. Dobereiner’s Law of Triads (1829) ⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Fundamental Principle & Definition
Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner grouped chemically similar elements into clusters of three, termed **Triads**. 

> **Döbereiner’s Law of Triads:**
> When elements of identical chemical characteristics are arranged in increasing order of their atomic weights, the atomic weight of the middle element is approximately equal to the arithmetic mean of the atomic weights of the other two elements.

#### 2. Mathematical Formulation & Quantitative Verification
For a triad consisting of elements $X_1$, $X_2$, and $X_3$ with respective atomic masses $A_1$, $A_2$, and $A_3$:

$$
A_2 \approx \frac{A_1 + A_3}{2}
$$

* **Triad 1: Alkali Metals (Group 1 Analogues)**
  * Elements: Lithium ($\text{Li}$), Sodium ($\text{Na}$), Potassium ($\text{K}$)
  * $A(\text{Li}) = 7.0\text{ u}$, $A(\text{K}) = 39.0\text{ u}$
  

$$
A(\text{Na}) = \frac{7.0 + 39.0}{2} = \frac{46.0}{2} = 23.0\text{ u} \quad (\text{Observed } A(\text{Na}) = 23.0\text{ u})
$$

* **Triad 2: Alkaline Earth Metals (Group 2 Analogues)**
  * Elements: Calcium ($\text{Ca}$), Strontium ($\text{Sr}$), Barium ($\text{Ba}$)
  * $A(\text{Ca}) = 40.0\text{ u}$, $A(\text{Ba}) = 137.0\text{ u}$
  

$$
A(\text{Sr})_{\text{calc}} = \frac{40.0 + 137.0}{2} = 88.5\text{ u} \quad (\text{Observed } A(\text{Sr}) = 87.6\text{ u})
$$

* **Triad 3: Halogens (Group 17 Analogues)**
  * Elements: Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$), Bromine ($\text{Br}$), Iodine ($\text{I}$)
  * $A(\text{Cl}) = 35.5\text{ u}$, $A(\text{I}) = 127.0\text{ u}$
  

$$
A(\text{Br})_{\text{calc}} = \frac{35.5 + 127.0}{2} = 81.25\text{ u} \quad (\text{Observed } A(\text{Br}) = 79.9\text{ u})
$$

#### 3. Critical Limitations
1. **Limited Scope:** Applicable only to a few elements. Döbereiner could identify only three valid triads at the time.
2. **Failure in Extended Groups:** In the halogen family, the triad $(\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br})$ completely fails:
   * $A(\text{F}) = 19.0\text{ u}$, $A(\text{Br}) = 79.9\text{ u}$
   

$$
A(\text{Cl})_{\text{calc}} = \frac{19.0 + 79.9}{2} = 49.45\text{ u} \neq 35.5\text{ u}
$$

3. **Failure with Dissimilar Chemistry:** Certain elements with nearly identical arithmetic means exhibit completely different chemical behavior (e.g., $\text{P}, \text{S}, \text{Cl}$), rendering atomic mass arithmetic insufficient on its own.

***

### B. Chancourtois' Telluric Helix (1862) ⭐⭐
French geologist A.E. Béguyer de Chancourtois arranged the elements in order of increasing atomic weights on a cylinder with a helical screw having a circumference of 16 units (the atomic weight of oxygen).
* **Observation:** Elements with similar properties fell on the same vertical line drawn along the cylinder.
* **Limitation:** The geometric representation was complex and received minimal recognition from mainstream chemists.

***

### C. Newlands’ Law of Octaves (1865) ⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Fundamental Principle & Definition
John Alexander Reina Newlands arranged the elements known at the time in increasing order of their atomic weights and observed a musical periodicity.

> **Newlands’ Law of Octaves:**
> When elements are arranged in increasing order of their atomic weights, every eighth element possesses chemical and physical properties analogous to the first element, similar to the eighth note in an octave of music ($sa, re, ga, ma, pa, dha, ni, sa$).

#### 2. Illustrative Sequence

$$
\begin{array}{ccccccc}
\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{2} & \mathbf{3} & \mathbf{4} & \mathbf{5} & \mathbf{6} & \mathbf{7} \\
\hline
\text{Li} & \text{Be} & \text{B} & \text{C} & \text{N} & \text{O} & \text{F} \\
\text{Na} & \text{Mg} & \text{Al} & \text{Si} & \text{P} & \text{S} & \text{Cl} \\
\text{K} & \text{Ca} & \text{Cr} & \text{Ti} & \text{Mn} & \text{Fe} & \text{Co/Ni}
\end{array}
$$

#### 3. Critical Limitations
1. **Light Elements Only:** The law held true only up to **Calcium** ($Z = 20$). Beyond Calcium, every eighth element failed to exhibit properties similar to the initial element (e.g., Chromium $\text{Cr}$ under Aluminum $\text{Al}$).
2. **Assumption of Known Elements:** Newlands assumed that only $56$ elements existed in nature and no new elements would be discovered.
3. **Improper Grouping of Dissimilar Elements:** To fit elements into his table, Newlands placed two elements into the same slot and grouped elements with entirely different properties together:
   * Cobalt ($\text{Co}$) and Nickel ($\text{Ni}$) were placed in the same column as Fluorine ($\text{F}$), Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$), and Bromine ($\text{Br}$).
   * Iron ($\text{Fe}$), which chemically resembles $\text{Co}$ and $\text{Ni}$, was placed far away alongside Oxygen and Sulfur.
4. **Discovery of Noble Gases:** Upon the discovery of noble gases (He, Ne, Ar), the eighth element no longer mirrored the first; the repetition pattern shifted to every **ninth** element, causing the Law of Octaves to break down.

***

### D. Lothar Meyer’s Atomic Volume Curve (1869) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Physical Principle
Lothar Meyer plotted physical properties of elements—notably **Atomic Volume**, melting point, and boiling point—against their **Atomic Weights**.

$$
\text{Atomic Volume} = \frac{\text{Gram Atomic Mass } (\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})}{\text{Density } (\text{g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3})}
$$

#### 2. Topographical Features of the Curve
When atomic volume is plotted on the $y$-axis against atomic weight on the $x$-axis, a periodic wave pattern emerges with well-defined topological sites:

```
 Atomic
 Volume
   ^          (Na)       (K)        (Rb)       (Cs)   <-- Crests (Alkali Metals)
   |           /\         /\         /\         /\
   |          /  \       /  \       /  \       /  \
   |      (Be)    (Mg)      (Ca)       (Sr)       (Ba)<-- Descending (Alkaline Earth Metals)
   |       /        \        /  \       /  \       /
   |      /     (F)  \   (Cl)    \  (Br)    \  (I)    <-- Ascending (Halogens)
   |     /____________\___/_______\___/_______\___/___
   |     [B, C, Si...]   [Transition metals]          <-- Troughs (Hard solids, high MP/BP)
   +----------------------------------------------------> Atomic Weight
```

* **Crests (Peaks):** Highly electropositive **Alkali metals** ($\text{Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs}$) occupy the peaks due to their large atomic radii and open crystal lattices.
* **Descending Slopes:** **Alkaline earth metals** ($\text{Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba}$) and slightly more electronegative elements occupy the descending slopes.
* **Ascending Slopes:** Electronegative **Halogens** ($\text{F, Cl, Br, I}$) occupy the ascending slopes directly preceding the peaks.
* **Troughs (Minima):** Moderately dense elements, non-metals of small atomic size ($\text{B, C}$), and hard **Transition elements** ($\text{Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, W}$) with high cohesive energies, high melting points, and high boiling points occupy the bottoms of the curve.

***

### E. Mendeleev’s Periodic Table (1869) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Mendeleev’s Periodic Law
Dmitri Ivanovich Mendeleev stated:

> **Mendeleev’s Periodic Law:**
> The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their **atomic weights**.

#### 2. Fundamental Structural Features
Mendeleev organized $63$ known elements into vertical columns called **Groups** and horizontal rows called **Periods**:
* **Groups:** Designated by Roman numerals $\text{I}$ through $\text{VIII}$ (Noble gases were later integrated as Group $0$). Groups $\text{I}$ to $\text{VII}$ are divided into two sub-groups: **Sub-group A** (normal/representative elements) and **Sub-group B** (transition elements).
* **Chemical Characterization through Oxide and Hydride Formulae:** Mendeleev used formulas of hydrides and oxides to assign elements to groups:
  * Group $\text{I}$: Hydride $RH$, Oxide $R_2O$
  * Group $\text{II}$: Hydride $RH_2$, Oxide $RO$
  * Group $\text{III}$: Hydride $RH_3$, Oxide $R_2O_3$
  * Group $\text{IV}$: Hydride $RH_4$, Oxide $RO_2$
  * Group $\text{V}$: Hydride $RH_3$, Oxide $R_2O_5$
  * Group $\text{VI}$: Hydride $RH_2$, Oxide $RO_3$
  * Group $\text{VII}$: Hydride $RH$, Oxide $R_2O_7$
  * Group $\text{VIII}$: Oxide $RO_4$

#### 3. Major Successes of Mendeleev’s Periodic Table
1. **Systematic Organization:** Grouped elements with similar properties, streamlining the study of elemental chemistry.
2. **Prediction of New Elements & Empty Slots:** Mendeleev left intentional gaps for undiscovered elements, designating them using the Sanskrit prefix *Eka-* (meaning "one beyond"):
   * **Eka-Aluminium** $\rightarrow$ Discovered as **Gallium** ($\text{Ga}$)
   * **Eka-Boron** $\rightarrow$ Discovered as **Scandium** ($\text{Sc}$)
   * **Eka-Silicon** $\rightarrow$ Discovered as **Germanium** ($\text{Ge}$)
   * **Eka-Manganese** $\rightarrow$ Discovered as **Technetium** ($\text{Tc}$)

#### Comparative Analysis: Mendeleev’s Predictions vs. Experimental Facts

$$
\begin{array}{|l|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Property & Eka-Aluminium (Pred.) & Gallium (Obs.) & Eka-Silicon (Pred.) & Germanium (Obs.) \\
\hline
\text{Atomic Mass} & \sim 68\text{ u} & 69.72\text{ u} & \sim 72\text{ u} & 72.61\text{ u} \\
\text{Density } (\text{g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}) & 5.9 & 5.904 & 5.5 & 5.35 \\
\text{Melting Point} & \text{Low} & 302.9\text{ K } (29.8^\circ\text{C}) & \text{High} & 1211.4\text{ K } (938.3^\circ\text{C}) \\
\text{Formula of Oxide} & E_2O_3 & \text{Ga}_2\text{O}_3 & EO_2 & \text{GeO}_2 \\
\text{Nature of Oxide} & \text{Amphoteric} & \text{Amphoteric} & \text{Weakly acidic} & \text{Weakly acidic} \\
\text{Formula of Chloride} & E\text{Cl}_3 & \text{GaCl}_3 & E\text{Cl}_4 & \text{GeCl}_4 \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

3. **Correction of Doubtful Atomic Masses:** Atomic weight values were recalculated based on valency considerations:
   

$$
\text{Atomic Weight} = \text{Equivalent Weight} \times \text{Valency}
$$

   * **Correction for Beryllium:** Beryllium was previously thought to have an equivalent weight of $4.5$ and a valency of $3$ (assumed atomic mass $= 13.5\text{ u}$, which would place it between Carbon and Nitrogen). Mendeleev recognized its bivalent nature ($\text{Valency} = 2$):
     

$$
\text{Atomic Mass} = 4.5 \times 2 = 9.0\text{ u}
$$

     This placed Beryllium correctly before Boron ($A = 11\text{ u}$) in Group II. Similar corrections were made for Indium ($\text{In}$), Gold ($\text{Au}$), and Platinum ($\text{Pt}$).

#### 4. Critical Anomalies / Defects of Mendeleev’s System
1. **Anomalous Pairs (Inverted Atomic Weights):** To preserve chemical similarity, elements with higher atomic masses were placed ahead of those with lower atomic masses:
   * **Argon** ($A = 39.95\text{ u}$) precedes **Potassium** ($A = 39.10\text{ u}$).
   * **Cobalt** ($A = 58.93\text{ u}$) precedes **Nickel** ($A = 58.71\text{ u}$).
   * **Tellurium** ($A = 127.60\text{ u}$) precedes **Iodine** ($A = 126.90\text{ u}$).
   * **Thorium** ($A = 232.04\text{ u}$) precedes **Protactinium** ($A = 231.04\text{ u}$).
2. **Ambiguity in Position of Hydrogen:** Hydrogen exhibits dual characteristics:
   * Resembles alkali metals (forms $H^+$, forms compounds like $\text{HCl}$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$).
   * Resembles halogens (forms diatomic $\text{H}_2$, forms hydride ions $H^-$, forms covalent compounds like $\text{CH}_4$).
3. **Position of Isotopes:** Isotopes possess distinct atomic masses ($^1_1\text{H}, ^2_1\text{H}, ^3_1\text{H}$ or $^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}, ^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$). If classification is based on atomic mass, each isotope would require its own distinct position.
4. **Dissimilar Elements Grouped Together:** Chemically distinct elements were grouped into the same numerical class:
   * Highly reactive alkali metals ($\text{Li, Na, K}$) were placed in Group $\text{IA}$, while relatively unreactive coinage metals ($\text{Cu, Ag, Au}$) were placed in Group $\text{IB}$.
5. **Similar Elements Separated:** Chemically similar elements were placed into different groups:
   * Platinum ($\text{Pt}$, Group $\text{VIII}$) and Gold ($\text{Au}$, Group $\text{IB}$).
   * Copper ($\text{Cu}$, Group $\text{IB}$) and Mercury ($\text{Hg}$, Group $\text{IIB}$).
6. **No Explanation for Periodicity:** The model offered no fundamental explanation for why properties recur at intervals of $2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32$.

# 2. Moseley’s Experiment & Modern Periodic Law ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In 1913, British physicist Henry Gwyn Jeffreys Moseley bombarded various target metals with high-energy cathode rays (electrons) and analyzed the characteristic X-rays emitted using crystal diffraction spectrometers.

```
       High-Voltage Anode Target Metal (Z)
                  |
  Cathode --------+=======> Characteristic X-Ray ($K_\alpha$)
  (Electron Beam) |
                  v
       Crystal Spectrometer (Diffraction: $n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$)
                  |
                  v
       Photographic Detector Plate [Yields Frequency $\nu$]
```

### A. Moseley’s Mathematical Law & Derivation
Moseley observed that the frequencies ($\nu$) of characteristic X-ray spectral lines (e.g., $K_\alpha$ lines) varied monotonically with the nuclear charge of the target element, rather than its atomic mass.

> **Moseley's Law:**
> The square root of the frequency ($\sqrt{\nu}$) of the characteristic X-ray emitted by an element is directly proportional to its atomic number ($Z$).

$$
\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)
$$

#### Parameter Definitions:
* $\nu$: Frequency of the emitted characteristic X-ray ($\text{Hz}$ or $\text{s}^{-1}$).
* $Z$: **Atomic Number** (equal to the number of positive charges/protons in the nucleus).
* $a$: Proportionality constant dependent on the spectral series (for the $K_\alpha$ series, $a = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4} R c}$, where $R$ is the Rydberg constant and $c$ is the speed of light).
* $b$: Nuclear screening/shielding constant:
  * For the $K$-series: $b \approx 1$ (due to screening by the remaining $1s$ electron).
  * For the $L$-series: $b \approx 7.4$.

#### Graph Analysis:
* Plotting $\sqrt{\nu}$ against atomic mass ($A$) produced irregular, non-linear scattering.
* Plotting $\sqrt{\nu}$ against atomic number ($Z$) produced a linear graph:

$$
\text{Slope} = a, \quad y\text{-intercept} = -ab, \quad x\text{-intercept} = b
$$

```
   \sqrt{\nu}
      ^
      |                 / (Linear response confirms Z as fundamental)
      |                /
      |               /  Slope = a
      |              /
      |             /
      |            /
      +-----------/--------------------> Z (Atomic Number)
     0|          b
```

### B. Theoretical Deduction from Bohr-Rydberg Quantum Mechanics
According to Bohr's theory of hydrogen-like species, the wavenumber $\bar{\nu}$ for an electronic transition from state $n_2$ to $n_1$ in an atom with effective nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}} = (Z - b)$ is:

$$
\bar{\nu} = \frac{\nu}{c} = R Z_{\text{eff}}^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) = R (Z - b)^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
$$

Solving for $\nu$:

$$
\nu = c R \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) (Z - b)^2
$$

Taking the square root:

$$
\sqrt{\nu} = \sqrt{c R \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)} \cdot (Z - b)
$$

Setting the constant prefactor to $a$:

$$
a = \sqrt{c R \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)}
$$

For the **$K_\alpha$ transition** ($n_1 = 1$, $n_2 = 2$):

$$
a = \sqrt{c R \left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{2^2}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4} c R}
$$

$$
\boxed{\sqrt{\nu} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4} c R} \cdot (Z - 1)}
$$

This result established that the physical and chemical properties of an element depend on its **nuclear charge ($Z$)**—and therefore its electronic structure—rather than its atomic mass.

***

### C. The Modern Periodic Law
Moseley's work led directly to the reformulation of the periodic law:

> **Modern Periodic Law (NCERT Formulation):**
> "The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers."

#### How Modern Periodic Law Resolves Mendeleev's Anomalies:
1. **Inverted Pairs:** When arranged by atomic number, anomalies naturally disappear:
   * $\text{Ar}$ ($Z = 18$) properly precedes $\text{K}$ ($Z = 19$).
   * $\text{Co}$ ($Z = 27$) properly precedes $\text{Ni}$ ($Z = 28$).
   * $\text{Te}$ ($Z = 52$) properly precedes $\text{I}$ ($Z = 53$).
   * $\text{Th}$ ($Z = 90$) properly precedes $\text{Pa}$ ($Z = 91$).
2. **Placement of Isotopes:** Isotopes share the same atomic number $Z$ (e.g., $^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$ and $^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$ both have $Z = 17$). They occupy the exact same position in the periodic table, eliminating the need for multiple slots.
3. **Identification of Missing Elements:** Because atomic number increments in integer steps ($\Delta Z = 1$), Moseley's law allowed chemists to determine the precise number of undiscovered elements between known elements (e.g., establishing that only elements with $Z = 43, 61, 72, 75$ remained undiscovered in those regions).

# 3. Modern Periodic Table (Long Form / Bohr's Table) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The Long Form of the Periodic Table is based on the **Aufbau Principle** and the **electronic configurations** of the elements. It arranges elements horizontally in order of increasing atomic number into **7 Periods** and vertically into **18 Groups**.

***

### A. Architectural Divisions: Periods

The principal quantum number $n$ of the valence shell determines the period of an element.

$$
\text{Period Number} = \text{Maximum Principal Quantum Number } (n_{\text{max}}) \text{ of the ground state electronic configuration}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|l|}
\hline
Period & Valence Shell  (n) & Orbitals Filled & Capacity  (e^-) & Classification / Elements \\
\hline
1^{\text{st}} & n = 1 & 1s & 2 & \text{Very Short Period: } \text{H } (Z=1) \text{ to } \text{He } (Z=2) \\
2^{\text{nd}} & n = 2 & 2s, 2p & 2 + 6 = 8 & \text{Short Period: } \text{Li } (Z=3) \text{ to } \text{Ne } (Z=10) \\
3^{\text{rd}} & n = 3 & 3s, 3p & 2 + 6 = 8 & \text{Short Period: } \text{Na } (Z=11) \text{ to } \text{Ar } (Z=18) \\
4^{\text{th}} & n = 4 & 4s, 3d, 4p & 2 + 10 + 6 = 18 & \text{Long Period: } \text{K } (Z=19) \text{ to } \text{Kr } (Z=36) \\
5^{\text{th}} & n = 5 & 5s, 4d, 5p & 2 + 10 + 6 = 18 & \text{Long Period: } \text{Rb } (Z=37) \text{ to } \text{Xe } (Z=54) \\
6^{\text{th}} & n = 6 & 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p & 2 + 14 + 10 + 6 = 32 & \text{Very Long Period: } \text{Cs } (Z=55) \text{ to } \text{Rn } (Z=86) \\
7^{\text{th}} & n = 7 & 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p & 2 + 14 + 10 + 6 = 32 & \text{Very Long Period (Complete): } \text{Fr } (Z=87) \text{ to } \text{Og } (Z=118) \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

#### Derivation of Element Capacity in a Given Period:
According to the Aufbau Principle and the $(n + l)$ rule, electrons enter orbitals in order of increasing energy:
1. For an **odd period** $n$:
   

$$
\text{Number of orbitals} = \frac{n+1}{2}
$$

 (for Period 1, one orbital: $1s$)
2. For an arbitrary period $n$ in terms of general capacity:
   * When $n$ is odd, maximum number of elements:
     

$$
N = \frac{(n + 1)^2}{2}
$$

     *(Applies strictly to periods where $f$ subshells are not yet accessible: Period 1 $\to 2$, Period 3 $\to 8$)*
   * When $n$ is even:
     

$$
N = \frac{(n + 2)^2}{2}
$$

     *(Period 2 $\to 8$, Period 4 $\to 18$)*
3. **General Orbital Infilling Rule:**
   For period $n$, the subshells fill in the sequence:
   

$$
ns \longrightarrow (n-2)f \longrightarrow (n-1)d \longrightarrow np
$$

   * Note: The $(n-2)f$ subshell fills only when $n \ge 6$.
   * The $(n-1)d$ subshell fills only when $n \ge 4$.

***

### B. Architectural Divisions: Groups (1 to 18)

Under IUPAC recommendations, vertical columns are numbered consecutively from **1 to 18**, replacing the older CAS/Mendeleev notation (IA–VIIA, VIII, IB–VIIB, 0).

Elements within a group share identical valence-shell electron configurations and exhibit similar chemical properties:

* **Group 1:** Alkali Metals ($ns^1$)
* **Group 2:** Alkaline Earth Metals ($ns^2$)
* **Groups 3–12:** Transition Elements / $d$-block ($ (n-1)d^{1-10} \, ns^{0-2} $)
* **Group 13:** Boron Family / Icosagens ($ns^2 \, np^1$)
* **Group 14:** Carbon Family / Crystallogens ($ns^2 \, np^2$)
* **Group 15:** Nitrogen Family / Pnictogens ($ns^2 \, np^3$)
* **Group 16:** Oxygen Family / Chalcogens ($ns^2 \, np^4$)
* **Group 17:** Halogens ($ns^2 \, np^5$)
* **Group 18:** Noble Gases / Aerogens ($ns^2 \, np^6$, except He: $1s^2$)

***

### C. Structural Divisions by Subshell ($s, p, d, f$ Blocks)

```
       |<--- s-block --->|                 |<----------- p-block ----------->|
       |  Group 1 & 2   |                 |         Group 13 to 18          |
       +-----------------+                 +---------------------------------+
       | ns^1 to ns^2    |                 |   ns^2 np^1  to  ns^2 np^6      |
       +-----------------+                 +---------------------------------+
                         |<-- d-block -->|
                         | Groups 3 - 12 |
                         +---------------+
                         |(n-1)d^1-10ns^1-2
                         +---------------+

                   |<----------- f-block (Inner-Transition) ----------->|
                   | Lanthanoids (4f): Ce (Z=58) to Lu (Z=71)           |
                   | Actinoids (5f):   Th (Z=90) to Lr (Z=103)          |
                   +----------------------------------------------------+
                   |          (n-2)f^1-14 (n-1)d^0-1 ns^2               |
                   +----------------------------------------------------+
```

#### 1. The $s$-Block Elements
* **Configuration:** $ns^{1-2}$ (where $n = 2-7$).
* **Characteristics:** Soft metals with low melting and boiling points, low ionization enthalpies, and high electropositivity.
* Form predominantly ionic compounds with oxidation state $+1$ (Group 1) or $+2$ (Group 2).
* Strong reducing agents; impart characteristic colors to a Bunsen burner flame.

#### 2. The $p$-Block Elements
* **Configuration:** $ns^2 \, np^{1-6}$ (where $n = 2-7$).
* Together with the $s$-block, these are called **Representative** or **Main Group Elements**.
* Includes metals, metalloids, and non-metals.
* Non-metallic character, electron gain enthalpy, and electronegativity increase from left to right across a period.
* Exhibit variable oxidation states, including group oxidation state and states lower by two units due to the **inert pair effect** in heavier members ($\text{Tl}^+, \text{Pb}^{2+}, \text{Bi}^{3+}$).

#### 3. The $d$-Block Elements (Transition Metals)
* **Configuration:** $(n-1)d^{1-10} \, ns^{0-2}$ (where $n = 4-7$).
* Metals characterized by variable oxidation states, formation of colored ions, paramagnetism, and catalytic activity.
* Bridge the electropositive $s$-block and the electronegative $p$-block.
* **Exceptions:** $\text{Zn}, \text{Cd}, \text{Hg}$ have completely filled $d$-orbitals in both their elemental states and common oxidation states ($(n-1)d^{10}\,ns^2$), and are therefore typically classified as non-transition or pseudo-transition metals.

#### 4. The $f$-Block Elements (Inner-Transition Metals)
* **Configuration:** $(n-2)f^{1-14} \, (n-1)d^{0-1} \, ns^2$ (where $n = 6, 7$).
* Two series, each containing 14 elements:
  * **Lanthanoids (4f-series):** Cerium ($Z = 58$) to Lutetium ($Z = 71$).
  * **Actinoids (5f-series):** Thorium ($Z = 90$) to Lawrencium ($Z = 103$).
* All actinoids are radioactive; elements past Uranium ($Z > 92$) are synthetic and termed **Transuranic Elements**.

***

# 4. IUPAC Nomenclature for Elements with $Z > 100$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

### A. Rationale & Historical Context
Elements with atomic numbers above 100 are typically synthesized in particle accelerators in minute quantities (often only a few atoms at a time) and decay rapidly. Historically, competitive claims between laboratories—notably the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (USA) and the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna (Russia)—led to priority disputes and conflicting names. 

For example, element $Z = 104$ was named **Rutherfordium** by American scientists and **Kurchatovium** by Soviet scientists. To resolve such controversies, the IUPAC Commission on Nomenclature of Inorganic Chemistry established a systematic nomenclature system based directly on the element's atomic number until discovery is officially validated.

***

### B. Structural Roots and Suffix Rules
The systematic name is derived directly from the digits of the atomic number using numerical roots derived from Latin and Greek:

$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Digit & Root & Abbreviation & Digit & Root & Abbreviation \\
\hline
0 & \text{nil} & \text{n} & 5 & \text{pent} & \text{p} \\
1 & \text{un} & \text{u} & 6 & \text{hex} & \text{h} \\
2 & \text{bi} & \text{b} & 7 & \text{sept} & \text{s} \\
3 & \text{tri} & \text{t} & 8 & \text{oct} & \text{o} \\
4 & \text{quad} & \text{q} & 9 & \text{enn} & \text{e} \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

#### Assembly Algorithm:
1. Concatenate the roots corresponding to the digits of the atomic number in order.
2. Append the suffix **"-ium"** to the end.
3. **Spelling Elision Rules:**
   * If `bi` or `tri` is followed immediately by the suffix `ium`, drop the duplicate 'i':
     

$$
\text{bi} + \text{ium} \longrightarrow \text{bium} \quad (\text{not biium})
$$

     

$$
\text{tri} + \text{ium} \longrightarrow \text{trium} \quad (\text{not triium})
$$

   * If `enn` is followed by `nil`, drop the duplicate 'n':
     

$$
\text{enn} + \text{nil} \longrightarrow \text{enil} \quad (\text{not ennnil})
$$

4. **Symbol Derivation:** Formed by capitalizing the first letter of each root:
   

$$
\text{Symbol} = \text{Root}_1(\text{Upper}) + \text{Root}_2(\text{Lower}) + \text{Root}_3(\text{Lower})
$$

# 5. Algorithmic Determination of Period, Group, and Block ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given an atomic number $Z$, determine the period, block, and group using this systematic procedure:

```
                  Input: Atomic Number (Z)
                             |
                             v
           Find Nearest Preceding Noble Gas [NG]
       (He: 2, Ne: 10, Ar: 18, Kr: 36, Xe: 54, Rn: 86)
                             |
                             v
           Write Electronic Configuration via Aufbau
                             |
                             v
               Identify Highest Principal QN (n)
                     ===> PERIOD = n
                             |
                             v
            Identify Subshell Receiving Last Electron
                             |
           +-----------------+-----------------+
           |                                   |
           v                                   v
      d or f block?                       s or p block?
           |                                   |
    +------+------+                     +------+------+
    |             |                     |             |
  Last e- in   Last e- in            Last e- in    Last e- in
 (n-2)f sub?  (n-1)d sub?             ns sub?       np sub?
    |             |                     |             |
    v             v                     v             v
BLOCK: f      BLOCK: d              BLOCK: s      BLOCK: p
GROUP: 3      GROUP:                GROUP:        GROUP:
(Always)      (n-1)d e- + ns e-     ns electrons  12 + np e-
```

### Determinative Rules:
1. **Period:** Equal to the principal quantum number ($n$) of the valence shell.
2. **Block:** Determined by the subshell that receives the last differentiating electron.
3. **Group:**
   * **$s$-Block:**
     

$$
\text{Group} = \text{Number of valence } s\text{-electrons}
$$

   * **$p$-Block:**
     

$$
\text{Group} = 12 + \text{Number of valence } p\text{-electrons} \quad \left[\text{or } 10 + (\text{valence } s + p\text{-electrons})\right]
$$

   * **$d$-Block:**
     

$$
\text{Group} = (\text{Number of } (n-1)d\text{ electrons}) + (\text{Number of } ns\text{ electrons})
$$

   * **$f$-Block:**
     

$$
\text{Group} = 3 \quad (\text{Applicable to all Lanthanoids and Actinoids without exception})
$$

### Problem 1: Determining Period, Group, and Block (NCERT Exemplar)
**Statement:**
Determine the block, period, and group in the modern periodic table for the elements with atomic numbers:
1. $Z = 35$
2. $Z = 56$
3. $Z = 24$
4. $Z = 64$

#### Detailed Solution:

* **Case 1: $Z = 35$**
  1. Nearest preceding noble gas: Argon ($_{18}\text{Ar}$).
  2. Electronic Configuration:
     

$$
_{35}\text{X} = [\text{Ar}]_{18} \, 3d^{10} \, 4s^2 \, 4p^5
$$

  3. Valence shell principal quantum number ($n_{\text{max}}$) $= 4 \implies \mathbf{Period = 4}$.
  4. The last differentiating electron enters a $4p$ orbital $\implies \mathbf{Block = p}$.
  5. Group calculation for $p$-block:
     

$$
\text{Group} = 12 + n_p = 12 + 5 = \mathbf{17} \quad (\text{Halogen family})
$$

* **Case 2: $Z = 56$**
  1. Nearest preceding noble gas: Xenon ($_{54}\text{Xe}$).
  2. Electronic Configuration:
     

$$
_{56}\text{X} = [\text{Xe}]_{54} \, 6s^2
$$

  3. Valence shell principal quantum number ($n_{\text{max}}$) $= 6 \implies \mathbf{Period = 6}$.
  4. The last electron enters the $6s$ orbital $\implies \mathbf{Block = s}$.
  5. Group calculation for $s$-block:
     

$$
\text{Group} = \text{valence } s\text{-electrons} = \mathbf{2} \quad (\text{Alkaline earth metal, Barium})
$$

* **Case 3: $Z = 24$**
  1. Electronic Configuration (accounting for half-filled stability):
     

$$
_{24}\text{Cr} = [\text{Ar}]_{18} \, 3d^5 \, 4s^1
$$

  2. Valence shell principal quantum number ($n_{\text{max}}$) $= 4 \implies \mathbf{Period = 4}$.
  3. The differentiating electron enters a $3d$ orbital $\implies \mathbf{Block = d}$.
  4. Group calculation for $d$-block:
     

$$
\text{Group} = (n-1)d\text{ electrons} + ns\text{ electrons} = 5 + 1 = \mathbf{6}
$$

* **Case 4: $Z = 64$ (Gadolinium)**
  1. Nearest preceding noble gas: Xenon ($_{54}\text{Xe}$).
  2. Electronic Configuration (with half-filled $4f^7$ stability):
     

$$
_{64}\text{Gd} = [\text{Xe}]_{54} \, 4f^7 \, 5d^1 \, 6s^2
$$

  3. Valence shell principal quantum number ($n_{\text{max}}$) $= 6 \implies \mathbf{Period = 6}$.
  4. The differentiating electron enters the $4f$ subshell $\implies \mathbf{Block = f}$.
  5. For any element in the $f$-block ($Z = 58-71$ or $Z = 90-103$):
     

$$
\mathbf{Group = 3}
$$

### Problem 2: Hypothetical Element Infilling & IUPAC Analysis
**Statement:**
An element has atomic number $Z = 120$. 
1. Derive its systematic IUPAC name and three-letter symbol.
2. Deduce its ground-state electronic configuration.
3. Predict its period, group, and block.
4. Predict the empirical formula of its oxide.

#### Detailed Solution:
1. **IUPAC Name & Symbol:**
   * $1 \rightarrow \text{un}$, $2 \rightarrow \text{bi}$, $0 \rightarrow \text{nil}$
   * $\text{un} + \text{bi} + \text{nil} + \text{ium} \rightarrow \mathbf{Unbinilium}$
   * Symbol: $\mathbf{Ubn}$

2. **Electronic Configuration:**
   * Element $Z = 118$ ($\text{Og}$) completes Period 7 with configuration $[\text{Rn}]_{86} \, 5f^{14} \, 6d^{10} \, 7s^2 \, 7p^6$.
   * Aufbau sequence after $7p$ requires infilling the $8s$ orbital:
     

$$
Z = 120: \quad [\text{Og}]_{118} \, 8s^2
$$

3. **Period, Group, and Block:**
   * Maximum principal quantum number $n = 8 \implies \mathbf{Period = 8}$.
   * Last electron enters the $8s$ orbital $\implies \mathbf{Block = s}$.
   * Valence electron count in $s$-subshell is $2 \implies \mathbf{Group = 2}$.

4. **Oxide Formula:**
   * Group 2 elements form divalent cations ($M^{2+}$).
   * Combining with oxide ion ($O^{2-}$):

$$

### Problem 3: Quantitative Moseley Analysis
**Statement:**
In a characteristic X-ray experiment, target metal $A$ ($Z_A = 25$, Manganese) emits $K_\alpha$ radiation at frequency $\nu_A = 1.42 \times 10^{18}\text{ Hz}$. Target metal $B$ emits $K_\alpha$ radiation at $\nu_B = 2.05 \times 10^{18}\text{ Hz}$. Using Moseley’s Law with screening constant $b = 1.0$:
1. Calculate the proportionality constant $a$.
2. Determine the atomic number ($Z_B$) and identity of target metal $B$.

#### Detailed Solution:
1. **Calculate constant $a$:**
   

$$
\sqrt{\nu_A} = a(Z_A - b)
$$

   

$$
\sqrt{1.42 \times 10^{18}\text{ s}^{-1}} = a(25 - 1.0)
$$

   

$$
1.1916 \times 10^9 = 24 \cdot a
$$

   

$$
a = \frac{1.1916 \times 10^9}{24} = \mathbf{4.965 \times 10^7\text{ Hz}^{1/2}}
$$

2. **Determine $Z_B$:**
   

$$
\sqrt{\nu_B} = a(Z_B - 1.0)
$$

   

$$
\sqrt{2.05 \times 10^{18}\text{ s}^{-1}} = 1.4318 \times 10^9\text{ Hz}^{1/2}
$$

   

$$
Z_B - 1.0 = \frac{1.4318 \times 10^9}{4.965 \times 10^7} \approx 28.84
$$

   

$$
Z_B = 28.84 + 1.0 = 29.84 \approx \mathbf{30}
$$

   Target metal $B$ has atomic number $Z = 30$, which corresponds to **Zinc ($\text{Zn}$)**.

|    - Trap: Stating that frequency is directly proportional to Z (\nu \propto Z).        |
|    - FACT: The square root of frequency is linear with Z (\sqrt{\nu} \propto Z).        |
|    - Plotting \nu vs Z yields a parabola, NOT a straight line.                          |

| 3. Misclassifying f-Block Element Groups:                                               |
|    - Students often mistakenly assign elements to Groups 4-14 based on valence e-.     |

| 4. Group Number Confusion in the p-Block:                                               |
|    - Trap: Writing the group number as equal to valence electrons (e.g., Ga = Group 3). |
|    - FACT: For p-block elements, you MUST add 12 to the number of p electrons:          |
|      Group = 12 + n_p (e.g., for Ga, 4p^1 -> Group = 12 + 1 = 13).                      |

# Electronic Configurations of Elements and Division into s, p, d, and f Blocks

# Class 11 Chemistry: Classification of Elements and Periodicity in Properties

## Comprehensive Lecture Note: Electronic Configurations of Elements and Division into $s$-, $p$-, $d$-, and $f$-Blocks

***

## 1. Pedagogical Theory, Fundamental Principles & Definitions

### 1.1 The Quantum-Mechanical Foundation of the Periodic Table ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The modern periodic law states:
> *"The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers."*

Under the quantum mechanical model of the atom, an electron in an atom is characterized by four quantum numbers:
1. **Principal Quantum Number ($n$):** Defines the principal electron shell, the average distance of the electron from the nucleus, and determines the **Period** in the Periodic Table.
   

$$
\text{Period Number} = \text{Maximum Principal Quantum Number } (n) \text{ of valence shell}
$$

2. **Azimuthal / Orbital Angular Momentum Quantum Number ($l$):** Defines the subshell (orbital shape) where $l \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n-1\}$.
   - $l = 0 \implies s\text{-orbital}$ (spherical)
   - $l = 1 \implies p\text{-orbital}$ (dumb-bell)
   - $l = 2 \implies d\text{-orbital}$ (clover-leaf / double dumb-bell)
   - $l = 3 \implies f\text{-orbital}$ (complex / diffused)
3. **Magnetic Quantum Number ($m_l$):** Defines spatial orientation: $m_l \in \{-l, \dots, 0, \dots, +l\}$, yielding $(2l+1)$ orbitals per subshell.
4. **Spin Quantum Number ($m_s$):** Defines intrinsic spin angular momentum: $m_s = \pm \frac{1}{2}$.

### 1.2 The Governing Rules for Electronic Configuration ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The distribution of electrons into orbitals follows three fundamental boundary conditions:

#### A. Aufbau Principle ($n+l$ Rule)
Orbitals are filled in the order of increasing energy:
- An orbital with a lower value of $(n+l)$ possesses lower energy and fills first.
- If two orbitals have the identical value of $(n+l)$, the orbital with the **lower value of $n$** possesses lower energy.

$$
\text{Energy sequence: } 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s < 5f < 6d < 7p
$$

#### B. Pauli Exclusion Principle
> *"No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers."*
Consequently, an individual orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons, and they must have antiparallel spins:

$$
N_{\text{max, subshell}} = 2(2l + 1)
$$

$$
N_{\text{max, shell } n} = 2n^2
$$

#### C. Hund’s Rule of Maximum Multiplicity
> *"Pairing of electrons in the degenerate orbitals of a subshell does not take place until each orbital belonging to that subshell is singly occupied with parallel spins."*

***

### 1.3 Division of the Periodic Table into Blocks ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The elements are categorized into four distinct blocks based strictly on the nature of the subshell into which the **last electron (differentiating electron)** enters:

$$
\text{Differentiating Electron} \in \begin{cases} 
s\text{-subshell} & \implies s\text{-block} \\ 
p\text{-subshell} & \implies p\text{-block} \\ 
(n-1)d\text{-subshell} & \implies d\text{-block} \\ 
(n-2)f\text{-subshell} & \implies f\text{-block} 
\end{cases}
$$

### 2.1 The $s$-Block Elements ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Definition:** Elements in which the differentiating electron enters the outermost $s$-orbital.
- **Valence Shell Electronic Configuration:**
  

$$
\mathbf{n s^{1-2}} \quad (n = 2 \text{ to } 7)
$$

- **Constituent Groups:**
  - **Group 1 (Alkali Metals):** General configuration $= [ \text{Noble Gas} ]\, ns^1$
  - **Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals):** General configuration $= [ \text{Noble Gas} ]\, ns^2$
- **General Characteristics:**
  1. Highly electropositive metals with low first ionization enthalpies:
     

$$
\mathrm{M(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_1} M^+(g) + e^-}
$$

  2. Exhibit fixed oxidation states: $+1$ for Group 1 and $+2$ for Group 2.
  3. Predominantly form ionic compounds (Exceptions: $\mathrm{LiCl}$ and $\mathrm{BeCl_2}$ exhibit significant covalent character due to high charge-to-radius ratio / polarizing power via Fajan's Rules).
  4. Highly reactive; metallic luster is rapidly tarnished in air. Never found free in nature.
  5. Act as powerful reducing agents. Flame coloration is imparted by thermally excited valence electrons returning to ground state (except $\mathrm{Be}$ and $\mathrm{Mg}$ whose electrons are held too tightly).

***

### 2.2 The $p$-Block Elements ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Definition:** Elements in which the last electron enters any of the three degenerate outermost $p$-orbitals.
- **Valence Shell Electronic Configuration:**
  

$$
\mathbf{n s^2\, n p^{1-6}} \quad (n = 2 \text{ to } 7)
$$

- **Constituent Groups:** Groups 13 to 18 (comprising 6 groups because a $p$-subshell can accommodate a maximum of $2 \times 3 = 6$ electrons).
  - Group 13 (Boron family): $ns^2\, np^1$
  - Group 14 (Carbon family): $ns^2\, np^2$
  - Group 15 (Pnictogens): $ns^2\, np^3$ (Extra stable half-filled $p^3$ subshell)
  - Group 16 (Chalcogens): $ns^2\, np^4$
  - Group 17 (Halogens): $ns^2\, np^5$ (High negative electron gain enthalpy)
  - Group 18 (Noble gases): $ns^2\, np^6$ (Except Helium: $1s^2$; completely filled octet, extraordinarily unreactive)
- **Representative Elements (Main Group Elements):**
  

$$
\text{Representative Elements} = s\text{-block} + p\text{-block (excluding Group 18)}
$$

- **General Characteristics:**
  1. Contains metals, metalloids (e.g., $\mathrm{B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te}$), and non-metals. Non-metallic character increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  2. Exhibit variable oxidation states. Maximum group oxidation state equals the total number of valence electrons:
     

$$
\text{Group Oxidation State} = \text{Group Number} - 10
$$

  3. **Inert Pair Effect:** Down heavier members of Groups 13, 14, 15, the reluctance of the valence $ns^2$ electrons to participate in bonding leads to stability of the $(\text{Group Oxidation State} - 2)$ state (e.g., $\mathrm{Tl^+ > Tl^{3+}}$, $\mathrm{Pb^{2+} > Pb^{4+}}$, $\mathrm{Bi^{3+} > Bi^{5+}}$).

***

### 2.3 The $d$-Block Elements (Transition Elements) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Definition:** Elements in which the differentiating electron enters the penultimate $(n-1)d$ subshell.
- **Valence Shell Electronic Configuration:**
  

$$
\mathbf{(n-1)d^{1-10}\; n s^{0-2}} \quad (n = 4 \text{ to } 7)
$$

- **Constituent Groups:** Groups 3 to 12 (10 columns spanning periods 4, 5, 6, 7).
- **NCERT Formal Definition of Transition Elements:**
  > *"A transition element is defined as an element which has an incompletely filled $d$-subshell in its ground state or in any one of its common oxidation states."*
- **The Non-Transition Anomaly of Group 12 ($\mathrm{Zn, Cd, Hg}$):**
  

$$
\mathrm{Zn}\; [Z=30]: [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^{10} 4s^2 \implies \mathrm{Zn^{2+}}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^{10}
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Cd}\; [Z=48]: [\mathrm{Kr}]\, 4d^{10} 5s^2 \implies \mathrm{Cd^{2+}}: [\mathrm{Kr}]\, 4d^{10}
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Hg}\; [Z=80]: [\mathrm{Xe}]\, 4f^{14} 5d^{10} 6s^2 \implies \mathrm{Hg^{2+}}: [\mathrm{Xe}]\, 4f^{14} 5d^{10}
$$

  They possess completely filled $d$-subshells ($d^{10}$) both in elemental forms and common oxidation states. Hence, **they are $d$-block elements, but NOT transition elements**.

***

### 2.4 The $f$-Block Elements (Inner Transition Elements) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Definition:** Elements in which the differentiating electron enters the anti-penultimate $(n-2)f$ subshell.
- **Valence Shell Electronic Configuration:**
  

$$
\mathbf{(n-2)f^{1-14}\; (n-1)d^{0-1}\; n s^2} \quad (n = 6, 7)
$$

- **Constituent Series:**
  1. **$4f$-Series (Lanthanoids):** Cerium ($Z=58$) to Lutetium ($Z=71$), 14 elements placed formally in Period 6, Group 3.
  2. **$5f$-Series (Actinoids):** Thorium ($Z=90$) to Lawrencium ($Z=103$), 14 elements placed formally in Period 7, Group 3.
  *(Note: Lanthanum, $\mathrm{La}$ ($Z=57$), and Actinium, $\mathrm{Ac}$ ($Z=89$), have $d^1$ configurations without $f$-electrons and belong strictly to the $d$-block).*
- **General Characteristics:**
  1. All are heavy metals with predominantly $+3$ oxidation states.
  2. Actinoids are all radioactive; elements past Uranium ($Z > 92$) are synthetic and termed **Transuranium / Transuranic elements**.
  3. Poor shielding of $4f$ electrons leads to steady contraction in atomic and ionic radii across the series (**Lanthanoid Contraction**).

***

### 2.5 Anomalous Electronic Configurations & Thermodynamic Stability ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Certain elements deviate from standard Aufbau filling to attain configurations of enhanced stability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Chromium } (Z = 24): & \quad [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^5 4s^1 \quad (\text{NOT } [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^4 4s^2) \\
\text{Copper } (Z = 29): & \quad [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^{10} 4s^1 \quad (\text{NOT } [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^9 4s^2)
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Physical Reasons for Stability:
1. **Symmetrical Distribution of Electron Charge:** Half-filled ($p^3, d^5, f^7$) and completely filled ($p^6, d^{10}, f^{14}$) subshells have spherically symmetrical charge distributions, minimizing inter-electronic shielding repulsions.
2. **Maximum Exchange Energy ($E_{ex}$):** Electrons with parallel spins in degenerate orbitals can exchange positions. The exchange releases stabilization energy:
   

$$
K_{\text{exchanges}} = \sum \frac{n_i(n_i - 1)}{2}
$$

   where $n_i$ is the number of electrons with parallel spin.

***

## 3. Derivations, Formulae & Step-by-Step Problem Solving

### 3.1 Derivation: Formula for Number of Elements in a Period ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Theorem
*Derive the theoretical maximum number of elements possible in a period characterized by principal quantum number $n$.*

#### Step-by-Step Derivation:
1. According to the Aufbau principle, filling of a period begins with orbital $ns$ and concludes when $np$ is filled (except $n=1$, which only contains $1s$).
2. The permitted orbitals filling in a period of principal quantum number $n$ must satisfy energy criteria:
   - For an orbital $(n', l)$ to be filled after $ns$ and up to $np$:
     

$$
\text{Energy}(ns) \le \text{Energy}(n', l) \le \text{Energy}(np)
$$

   - By the $(n+l)$ rule, orbitals filled between $ns$ and $np$ can include:
     - The valence $s$-orbital: $ns$ ($1$ orbital)
     - The inner $d$-orbitals: $(n-1)d$, provided $(n-1) \ge 3 \implies n \ge 4$ ($5$ orbitals)
     - The inner $f$-orbitals: $(n-2)f$, provided $(n-2) \ge 4 \implies n \ge 6$ ($7$ orbitals)
     - The valence $p$-orbitals: $np$, provided $n \ge 2$ ($3$ orbitals)

3. **General Formula:**
   

$$
\text{Total Orbitals Available } (N_{\text{orb}}) = \begin{cases} 
   \frac{n+1}{2}^2 = \frac{(n+1)^2}{4} & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} \\ 
   \frac{(n+2)^2}{4} & \text{if } n \text{ is even} 
   \end{cases} \quad (\text{for hypothetical models})
$$

   
   Direct orbital count for standard $s, p, d, f$ subshell filling:
   - **Period 1 ($n=1$):** Only $1s$ $\implies 1 \text{ orbital} \times 2 = \mathbf{2 \text{ elements}}$
   - **Period 2 ($n=2$):** $2s, 2p$ $\implies (1 + 3) = 4 \text{ orbitals} \times 2 = \mathbf{8 \text{ elements}}$
   - **Period 3 ($n=3$):** $3s, 3p$ $\implies (1 + 3) = 4 \text{ orbitals} \times 2 = \mathbf{8 \text{ elements}}$
     *(Note: $3d$ has $(n+l) = 3+2 = 5$, whereas $4s$ has $(n+l) = 4+0 = 4$; hence $3d$ fills in Period 4).*
   - **Period 4 ($n=4$):** $4s, 3d, 4p \implies (1 + 5 + 3) = 9 \text{ orbitals} \times 2 = \mathbf{18 \text{ elements}}$
   - **Period 5 ($n=5$):** $5s, 4d, 5p \implies (1 + 5 + 3) = 9 \text{ orbitals} \times 2 = \mathbf{18 \text{ elements}}$
   - **Period 6 ($n=6$):** $6s, 4f, 5d, 6p \implies (1 + 7 + 5 + 3) = 16 \text{ orbitals} \times 2 = \mathbf{32 \text{ elements}}$
   - **Period 7 ($n=7$):** $7s, 5f, 6d, 7p \implies (1 + 7 + 5 + 3) = 16 \text{ orbitals} \times 2 = \mathbf{32 \text{ elements}}$

***

### 3.2 Quantitative Derivation: Exchange Energy Comparison ($d^4 4s^2$ vs $d^5 4s^1$ for $\mathrm{Cr}$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Exchange Calculation Formulation:
Let $K$ be the exchange energy released per pair of parallel spins.
The number of possible exchange interactions among $n$ electrons with parallel spin is:

$$
N_{\text{pairs}} = {}^nC_2 = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}
$$

#### Case 1: Hypothetical $3d^4 4s^2$ Configuration for $\mathrm{Cr}$ ($Z = 24$)
- $d$-subshell has 4 electrons with parallel spin:
  

$$
N_{\text{ex}} = \frac{4(4-1)}{2} = \frac{12}{2} = 6 \text{ exchanges}
$$

- Total Stabilization Energy:
  

$$
\mathbf{E_{\text{stabilization}}} = \mathbf{6K}
$$

#### Case 2: Actual $3d^5 4s^1$ Configuration for $\mathrm{Cr}$ ($Z = 24$)
- $d$-subshell has 5 electrons with parallel spin:
  

$$
N_{\text{ex}} = \frac{5(5-1)}{2} = \frac{20}{2} = 10 \text{ exchanges}
$$

- Total Stabilization Energy:
  

$$
\mathbf{E_{\text{stabilization}}} = \mathbf{10K}
$$

$$
\Delta N_{\text{ex}} = 10 - 6 = 4 \text{ additional exchanges}
$$

$$
\mathbf{\Delta E_{\text{exchange}} = 4K \quad (\text{Substantial Thermodynamic Stabilization})}
$$

This extra exchange stabilization exceeds the pairing energy and the small promotion energy required to unpair an electron from $4s$ to promote it to $3d$.

***

### 3.3 Algorithmic Determination of Period, Group, and Block ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given an element with atomic number $Z$:

```
                                  DETERMINE BLOCK
                                         |
               +-------------------------+-------------------------+
               |                         |                         |
         Differentiating           Differentiating           Differentiating
        electron in ns            electron in np           electron in (n-1)d
               |                         |                         |
            s-BLOCK                   p-BLOCK                   d-BLOCK
               |                         |                         |
        Period = n                Period = n                Period = n
        Group  = ns count         Group  = 12 + np count    Group  = (ns) + (n-1)d
                                  (or ns + np + 10)
```

1. **To find Period:**
   

$$
\text{Period} = \max(n) \text{ in the written ground state configuration}
$$

2. **To find Block:**
   

$$
\text{Block} = \text{Orbital type receiving the last (differentiating) electron}
$$

3. **To find Group Number:**
   - **If $s$-block:**
     

$$
\text{Group Number} = \text{Number of valence } s\text{-electrons}
$$

   - **If $p$-block:**
     

$$
\text{Group Number} = 12 + \text{Number of valence } p\text{-electrons} \equiv 10 + \text{Number of } (s + p) \text{ electrons}
$$

   - **If $d$-block:**
     

$$
\text{Group Number} = \text{Number of } (n-1)d \text{ electrons} + \text{Number of } ns \text{ electrons}
$$

   - **If $f$-block:**
     

$$
\mathbf{\text{Group Number} = 3 \quad (\text{Constant for all Lanthanoids and Actinoids})}
$$

## 5. High-Yield Worked Examples & NCERT Exemplar Archetypes

### Problem 5.1 (NCERT Exemplar Archetype) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Question:** An element has ground-state electronic configuration $[\mathrm{Kr}]\, 4d^{10} 5s^2 5p^3$. Determine its atomic number, block, period, group, and the formula of its oxide.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Calculate Atomic Number ($Z$):**
   

$$
Z = Z(\mathrm{Kr}) + 10 + 2 + 3 = 36 + 10 + 2 + 3 = \mathbf{51}
$$

   The element is Antimony ($\mathrm{Sb}$).
2. **Determine Period:**
   

$$
\text{Maximum } n = 5 \implies \mathbf{\text{Period } 5}
$$

3. **Determine Block:**
   The last 3 electrons enter the $5p$ subshell $\implies \mathbf{p\text{-block}}$.
4. **Determine Group Number:**
   

$$
\text{Group} = 12 + p\text{-electrons} = 12 + 3 = \mathbf{15}
$$

   *(Alternatively: $10 + \text{valence electrons} = 10 + (2+3) = 15$)*.
5. **Formula of highest oxide:**
   Group 15 elements have maximum oxidation state $+5$:
   

$$
\mathrm{Sb^{5+} + O^{2-} \implies \mathbf{Sb_2O_5} \quad (\text{or dimerized } Sb_4O_{10})}
$$

***

### Problem 5.2 (Hypothetical Quantum Universe - Advanced IIT-JEE/Exemplar) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Question:** Suppose the magnetic spin quantum number $m_s$ could have three possible values: $m_s = -\frac{1}{2}, 0, +\frac{1}{2}$. How many elements would be present in the $4^{\text{th}}$ period of such a periodic table?

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Maximum Capacity of an Orbital:**
   Each orbital accommodates 1 electron per unique set of $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$.
   If $m_s$ takes 3 values, **each orbital can hold a maximum of 3 electrons**.
2. **Identify Orbitals Filled in Period 4:**
   Under the $(n+l)$ rule, filling in Period 4 follows: $4s \to 3d \to 4p$.
   - $4s$ subshell ($l=0$): $2l+1 = 1$ orbital
   - $3d$ subshell ($l=2$): $2l+1 = 5$ orbitals
   - $4p$ subshell ($l=1$): $2l+1 = 3$ orbitals
3. **Total Orbitals Available:**
   

$$
N_{\text{orbitals}} = 1 + 5 + 3 = 9 \text{ orbitals}
$$

4. **Calculate Total Number of Elements:**
   

$$
\text{Number of elements} = N_{\text{orbitals}} \times 3 = 9 \times 3 = \mathbf{27 \text{ elements}}
$$

***

### Problem 5.3 (Positioning of Superheavy Elements) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Question:** Predict the period, group, block, and electronic configuration of the element with atomic number $Z = 118$ and $Z = 120$.

**Solution for $Z = 118$:**
1. Preceding noble gas is Radon ($Z=86$).
2. Filling order:
   

$$
Z = 86 + 14\,(5f) + 10\,(6d) + 2\,(7s) + 6\,(7p) = 118
$$

   Full configuration: $[\mathrm{Rn}]\, 5f^{14} 6d^{10} 7s^2 7p^6$.
3. Block: Differentiating electron is in $7p \implies \mathbf{p\text{-block}}$.
4. Period: $\max(n) = \mathbf{7}$.
5. Group: $12 + 6 = \mathbf{18}$ (Noble gas, Oganesson, $\mathrm{Og}$).

**Solution for $Z = 120$:**
1. Period 7 terminates at $Z = 118$ ($[\mathrm{Og}]$).
2. Element $119$ enters $8s^1$; Element $120$ enters $8s^2$.
3. Configuration: $[\mathrm{Og}]\, 8s^2$.
4. Block: $\mathbf{s\text{-block}}$.
5. Period: $\mathbf{8}$.
6. Group: $\mathbf{2}$ (Alkaline earth metal series extended).

***

### Problem 5.4 (Exemplar: Identifying Transition vs Non-Transition d-block) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Question:** Why is Scandium ($Z=21$) classified as a transition element, but Zinc ($Z=30$) is not?

**Solution:**
- Electronic configuration of $\mathrm{Sc}$ ($Z=21$): $[\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^1 4s^2$.
  In its ground atomic state, it has an **incompletely filled $d$-subshell ($3d^1$)**. By IUPAC definition, Scandium is a transition element.
- Electronic configuration of $\mathrm{Zn}$ ($Z=30$): $[\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^{10} 4s^2$.
  In its ground atomic state, the $3d$-subshell is completely filled ($3d^{10}$).
  In its only stable and common oxidation state ($+2$):
  

$$
\mathrm{Zn^{2+}} = [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^{10}
$$

  The $d$-orbital remains completely filled ($d^{10}$). Hence, Zinc fails the criterion of incomplete $d$-subshell filling and is **not a transition element**.

## 6. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

| Trap / Misconception | What Students Wrongly Write | Correct Scientific Fact & NCERT Position |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Position of Helium** | Assigning Helium ($1s^2$) to the $s$-block due to $s^2$ valence. | Helium is placed in **Group 18 ($p$-block)** strictly due to chemical inertness and completely filled valence shell, though electronically it has an $s$-configuration. |
| **Hydrogen Ambiguity** | Placing Hydrogen unconditionally in Group 1. | Hydrogen has $1s^1$ configuration resembling alkali metals, but also resembles halogens ($1s^2$ noble gas minus one electron). NCERT places it **isolated at the top of the periodic table**. |
| **Period calculation of $d$- and $f$-blocks** | Stating that $3d$ elements belong to Period 3 because of the principal number $3$ in $3d$. | Period is determined by the **maximum principal quantum number ($n$) in the configuration**. For $3d$ series, valence $4s$ is present $\implies \mathbf{\text{Period } 4}$. |
| **Group of all $f$-block elements** | Calculating group number by summing $(n-2)f + (n-1)d + ns$ electrons. | ALL Lanthanoids and Actinoids strictly belong to **Group 3**. |
| **Electron Loss Order during Ionization** | Writing $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ configuration as $[\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^4 4s^2$ by removing electrons from $3d$. | **$4s$ electrons are lost first!** Although $4s$ fills before $3d$, once filled, $4s$ has higher energy and lies further outwards: $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}} = [\mathrm{Ar}]\, 3d^6 4s^0$. |
| **Definition of Transition Metal** | Defining transition metal simply as *"any element belonging to the $d$-block"*. | Must explicitly include: *"having partially filled $d$-orbitals in neutral atom or in any stable oxidation state"*. Omitting this loses full marks. |

\mathrm{He (2), \; Ne (10), \; Ar (18), \; Kr (36), \; Xe (54), \; Rn (86), \; Og (118)}

$$
\text{Group} = Z - 54 - 14 = Z - 68
$$

   - If in period 7 ($Z_{\text{prior}} = 86$) and $Z > 103$:
     

$$
\text{Group} = Z - 86 - 14 = Z - 100

# Periodic Trends in Physical Properties: Atomic and Ionic Radii, and Ionization Enthalpy

# Comprehensive Teaching Master-Note: Classification of Elements & Periodicity in Properties

**Target Audience:** Class 11 CBSE / NEET / JEE-Main & Advanced  
**Focus Area:** Periodic Trends in Physical Properties — Atomic & Ionic Radii, and Ionization Enthalpy  

***

## 1. Atomic and Ionic Radii

### 1.1 Conceptual Foundations & Theoretical Ambiguities ⭐⭐⭐
According to wave mechanics, an atom does not have a well-defined boundary. 

1. The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle states that one cannot simultaneously determine the exact position and momentum of an electron:
   

$$
\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}
$$

2. The electron probability distribution asymptotically approaches zero only at infinity:
   

$$
\lim_{r \to \infty} |\psi(r)|^2 = 0
$$

Therefore, the atomic radius cannot be measured directly for an isolated single atom. Instead, operational definitions are used based on the internuclear separation distance ($d$) between bonded or interacting atoms in condensed phases (gaseous, liquid, or crystalline states).

```
         Covalent Radius                      van der Waals Radius
   [ (•)--- d_cov ---(•) ]               [  ( • )  ]       [  ( • )  ]
        |<-- r_cov -->|                            |<-- d_vdw -->|
     r_cov = d_cov / 2                             r_vdw = d_vdw / 2
   (Overlap region present)                   (Non-bonded, touching boundary)
```

***

### 1.2 Operational Types of Radii ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### A. Covalent Radius ($r_{\text{cov}}$)
Defined as one-half the internuclear distance between two identical non-metallic atoms held together by a single covalent bond in a homonuclear diatomic molecule.

For a homonuclear diatomic system $A_2$:

$$
r_A = \frac{d_{A-A}}{2}
$$

*Example (Chlorine):* Internuclear distance in $\text{Cl}_2$ is measured via X-ray/electron diffraction as $198\text{ pm}$.

$$
r_{\text{cov}}(\text{Cl}) = \frac{198\text{ pm}}{2} = 99\text{ pm} = 0.99\text{ Å} = 0.099\text{ nm}
$$

**Heteronuclear Covalent Bond Correction (Stevenson-Schomaker Equation):**
If electronegativity differences ($\Delta \chi = |\chi_A - \chi_B|$) are significant, bond shortening occurs due to partial ionic character:

$$
d_{A-B} = r_A + r_B - 0.09 \cdot |\chi_A - \chi_B|
$$

*(where radii and internuclear distances are in Angstroms, $\text{Å}$)*

***

#### B. Metallic Radius ($r_{\text{met}}$)
Defined as one-half the internuclear distance between two adjacent metal ions in the close-packed metallic crystal lattice.

*Example (Copper):* The internuclear distance between two adjacent copper atoms in solid metallic copper is $256\text{ pm}$.

$$
r_{\text{met}}(\text{Cu}) = \frac{256\text{ pm}}{2} = 128\text{ pm} = 1.28\text{ Å}
$$

Because metallic bonds do not involve localized orbital interpenetration/overlap to the extent seen in covalent bonding, metallic bonds yield longer internuclear distances for comparable electronic structures.

***

#### C. van der Waals Radius ($r_{\text{vdw}}$)
Defined as one-half the distance between the nuclei of two non-bonded, identical atoms belonging to adjacent, neighboring molecules in the solid state.

It accounts for non-covalent, weak dispersion forces (London forces).

*Comparative Magnitude Hierarchy:*

$$
\boxed{r_{\text{vdw}} > r_{\text{met}} > r_{\text{cov}}}
$$

*Physical Basis:* Covalent radii involve orbital overlap; metallic radii involve metallic packing of ions sharing a delocalized conduction electron sea; van der Waals interactions involve purely electrostatic fluctuations between non-overlapping electron clouds.

***

### 1.3 Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) & Slater's Rules ⭐⭐⭐⭐

The valence electrons do not feel the full nuclear charge $Z$ because intervening inner-shell (core) electrons repel the outer electrons and shield/screen them from the nucleus:

$$
Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - \sigma
$$

where:
- $Z$ is the actual nuclear charge (atomic number).
- $\sigma$ (or $S$) is the screening/shielding constant.

#### Screening Power Hierarchy of Orbitals
Due to radial penetration closer to the nucleus:

$$
s > p > d > f
$$

Diffuse geometries ($d$ and $f$ orbitals) have inner nodes and radial distributions that do not efficiently block the nuclear charge, causing an increase in $Z_{\text{eff}}$ experienced by the valence shell.

***

### 1.4 Periodic Trends in Atomic Radii ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Across a Period (Left to Right)
- **Trend:** Atomic radius **decreases**.
- **Mechanism:** Electrons are sequentially added to the *same principal quantum level* ($n = \text{constant}$). The screening effect increases marginally ($\Delta \sigma \approx 0.35$ per electron), whereas nuclear charge increases by a full unit ($\Delta Z = +1$).
  

$$
\frac{d Z_{\text{eff}}}{d Z} > 0
$$

- The electrostatic pull on the outer valence electrons increases:
  

$$
F = \frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Z_{\text{eff}} \cdot e^2}{r^2}
$$

  pulling the electron cloud closer to the nucleus.

*Period 2 Values ($r_{\text{cov}}$ in pm):*

$$
\text{Li (152)} > \text{Be (111)} > \text{B (88)} > \text{C (77)} > \text{N (74)} > \text{O (66)} > \text{F (64)}
$$

> **Critical NCERT Note:** Noble gases (e.g., $\text{Ne} = 160\text{ pm}$) are excluded from this monotonic decrease because noble gases do not form covalent bonds under standard conditions. Their reported values reflect the **van der Waals radius**, which is structurally larger than the covalent radius of fluorine.

#### Down a Group (Top to Bottom)
- **Trend:** Atomic radius **increases**.
- **Mechanism:** Principal quantum number $n$ increases by 1 at each row. Successive shells are added:
  

$$
\text{Radial Probability Peak Distance } r \propto \frac{n^2}{Z_{\text{eff}}}
$$

- The increased distance from the nucleus and enhanced shielding by filled inner shells outweigh the increase in actual nuclear charge ($Z$).

***

### 1.5 Ionic Radii and Isoelectronic Species ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Cations
Formed when an atom loses one or more valence electrons:

$$
A(\text{g}) \longrightarrow A^{n+}(\text{g}) + n e^-
$$

A cation is **always smaller** than its parent neutral atom:

$$
\boxed{r_{\text{cation}} < r_{\text{atom}}}
$$

- **Reason 1:** Often, the entire outermost valence shell is completely stripped (e.g., $\text{Na } [2,8,1] \to \text{Na}^+ [2,8]$).
- **Reason 2:** The nuclear charge $Z$ remains unchanged while the number of electrons decreases. Thus, the effective nuclear charge per electron ($Z/e\text{ ratio}$) increases, pulling the remaining electrons inward:
  

$$
\left(\frac{Z}{e}\right)_{\text{cation}} > \left(\frac{Z}{e}\right)_{\text{neutral}}
$$

#### Anions
Formed when an atom gains one or more valence electrons:

$$
X(\text{g}) + n e^- \longrightarrow X^{n-}(\text{g})
$$

An anion is **always larger** than its parent neutral atom:

$$
\boxed{r_{\text{anion}} > r_{\text{atom}}}
$$

- **Reason:** Addition of electrons increases inter-electronic repulsions within the same valence shell without an increase in nuclear charge $Z$. To minimize repulsive potential energy, the electron cloud expands. The $Z/e$ ratio decreases:
  

$$
\left(\frac{Z}{e}\right)_{\text{anion}} < \left(\frac{Z}{e}\right)_{\text{neutral}}
$$

#### Isoelectronic Series
Species containing the identical total number of electrons but differing in nuclear charge $Z$.

*Representative 10-Electron Isoelectronic Series ($n=2$ core):*

$$
\text{N}^{3-},\ \text{O}^{2-},\ \text{F}^-,\ \text{Ne},\ \text{Na}^+,\ \text{Mg}^{2+},\ \text{Al}^{3+}
$$

| Species | $Z$ (Protons) | $e^-$ | $Z/e\text{ Ratio}$ | Radius ($\text{pm}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\text{N}^{3-}$ | 7 | 10 | 0.70 | 171 |
| $\text{O}^{2-}$ | 8 | 10 | 0.80 | 140 |
| $\text{F}^-$ | 9 | 10 | 0.90 | 133 |
| $\text{Na}^+$ | 11 | 10 | 1.10 | 102 |
| $\text{Mg}^{2+}$| 12 | 10 | 1.20 | 72 |
| $\text{Al}^{3+}$| 13 | 10 | 1.30 | 53.5 |

**Universal Law for Isoelectronic Series:**

$$
\boxed{\text{Ionic Radius} \propto \frac{1}{Z} \propto \frac{1}{Z/e}}
$$

As nuclear charge increases across an isoelectronic series, inward electrostatic attraction increases, progressively contracting the ionic radius.

## 2. Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$)

### 2.1 Thermodynamic Definition & Governing Conditions ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Ionization Enthalpy is the minimum amount of energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground electronic state:

$$
\boxed{X(\text{g}) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_1} X^+(\text{g}) + e^-(\text{g})}
$$

#### Strict Conditions for Measurement:
1. **Isolated State:** Atoms must be in the gaseous phase at low pressure so that interatomic/intermolecular interactions are effectively zero ($U_{\text{inter}} \to 0$).
2. **Ground State:** The atom must occupy its lowest possible thermodynamic and electronic energy state.
3. **Sign Convention:** Ionization enthalpy is always endothermic, hence:
   

$$
\Delta_i H > 0\quad (\text{SI Unit: }\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\text{ or }\text{eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1})
$$

   

$$
1\text{ eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1} = 96.485\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

***

### 2.2 Successive Ionization Enthalpies ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Electrons can be systematically stripped in sequence:
1. First Ionization Enthalpy:
   

$$
X(\text{g}) \longrightarrow X^+(\text{g}) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_1)
$$

2. Second Ionization Enthalpy:
   

$$
X^+(\text{g}) \longrightarrow X^{2+}(\text{g}) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_2)
$$

3. Third Ionization Enthalpy:
   

$$
X^{2+}(\text{g}) \longrightarrow X^{3+}(\text{g}) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_3)
$$

#### Governing Inequality:

$$
\boxed{\Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3 < \dots < \Delta_i H_n}
$$

**Thermodynamic/Physical Derivation:**
When removing an electron from a positively charged cation $X^{n+}$, the number of negatively charged electrons decreases while the central nuclear charge $Z$ remains constant.
- The net shielding ($\sigma$) drops significantly.
- The effective nuclear charge experienced by the remaining electrons increases:
  

$$
Z_{\text{eff}}(X^{n+}) > Z_{\text{eff}}(X^{(n-1)+})
$$

- Removing an electron requires overcoming the Coulombic attraction of an increasingly positive ionic core:
  

$$
\Delta U_{\text{coulombic}} = \int_{r_0}^\infty \frac{Z_{\text{eff}} \cdot e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} dr
$$

  which requires substantially more work.

***

### 2.3 Factors Governing Ionization Enthalpy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Ionization enthalpy is a multivariable function:

$$
\Delta_i H = f(Z_{\text{eff}}, n, \sigma, \text{Penetration}, \text{Exchange Energy})
$$

1. **Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$):**
   

$$
\Delta_i H \propto Z_{\text{eff}}
$$

   Stronger nuclear pull tightens the valence electrons and raises the energy needed to remove them.

2. **Principal Quantum Number / Atomic Size ($n, r$):**
   

$$
\Delta_i H \propto \frac{1}{r}
$$

   Larger distance from the nucleus reduces the electrostatic force ($F \propto 1/r^2$).

3. **Penetration Effect of Orbitals:**
   Electrons in orbitals that penetrate closer to the nucleus are more tightly bound. For the same principal quantum level $n$:
   

$$
\text{Penetration Power: } s > p > d > f
$$

   Therefore, removing an electron from an $s$-orbital requires more energy than from a $p$-orbital of the same shell:
   

$$
\Delta_i H(ns) > \Delta_i H(np)
$$

4. **Screening/Shielding Effect ($\sigma$):**
   

$$
\Delta_i H \propto \frac{1}{\sigma}
$$

   Greater shielding from inner shells decreases the effective pull of the nucleus on valence electrons.

5. **Stability of Half-Filled and Completely Filled Subshells:**
   Configurations with half-filled ($p^3, d^5, f^7$) or fully filled ($s^2, p^6, d^{10}, f^{14}$) subshells exhibit extra thermodynamic stability due to:
   - **Symmetrical charge distribution:** Minimizes inter-electronic repulsion.
   - **Maximum exchange energy ($K$):** Parallel-spin electrons exchange degenerate positions, releasing stabilizing quantum-mechanical exchange energy:
     

$$
E_{\text{ex}} = -K \cdot \binom{m}{2} = -K \frac{m(m-1)}{2}
$$

     where $m$ is the number of electrons with parallel spin. Disrupting these configurations requires extra energy.

***

### 2.4 Periodicity & Critical Anomalies ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

```
  IE (kJ/mol)
    ^
 Ne |                                                 *
    |                                            *
  F |                                       *
  N |                         *        *
  O |                             *
  C |                   *
 Be |         *
  B |              *
 Li |    *
    +-------------------------------------------------->
        Li   Be    B    C     N   O    F   Ne     (Period 2)
```

#### Anomaly 1: Beryllium vs. Boron ($\Delta_i H_1(\text{Be}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{B})$)
- **Data:** $\text{Be} = 899\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ vs. $\text{B} = 801\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
- **Configurations:**
  - $\text{Be } (Z=4): 1s^2\, 2s^2$
  - $\text{B } (Z=5): 1s^2\, 2s^2\, 2p^1$
- **Explanation:**
  1. **Penetration & Subshell Type:** The electron removed from $\text{Be}$ comes from a penetrating, completely filled $2s$-orbital. The electron removed from $\text{B}$ comes from a higher-energy, less penetrating $2p$-orbital.
  2. **Shielding:** The $2p^1$ electron in Boron is shielded from the nucleus by the inner $1s^2$ and the two $2s^2$ electrons, lowering its ionization energy relative to Beryllium.

#### Anomaly 2: Nitrogen vs. Oxygen ($\Delta_i H_1(\text{N}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{O})$)
- **Data:** $\text{N} = 1402\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ vs. $\text{O} = 1314\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
- **Configurations:**
  - $\text{N } (Z=7): 1s^2\, 2s^2\, 2p_x^1\, 2p_y^1\, 2p_z^1$ (Half-filled $2p^3$)
  - $\text{O } (Z=8): 1s^2\, 2s^2\, 2p_x^2\, 2p_y^1\, 2p_z^1$ ($2p^4$)
- **Explanation:**
  1. **Half-Filled Stability:** Nitrogen has an exchange-stabilized half-filled subshell with three electrons of parallel spin:
     

$$
\binom{3}{2} = \frac{3 \times 2}{2} = 3 \text{ exchange interactions}
$$

  2. **Inter-electronic Repulsion:** In Oxygen, the $2p_x$ orbital contains two paired electrons. Pairing two electrons in the same orbital volume introduces significant Coulombic repulsion:
     

$$
U_{\text{repulsion}} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12}}
$$

     This inter-electronic repulsion destabilizes the ground state of oxygen and offsets the increase in nuclear charge, making the $2p^4$ electron easier to remove than a $2p^3$ electron from nitrogen.

#### Anomaly 3: Noble Gases
Noble gases hold the maximum first ionization enthalpy in their respective periods due to their closed octet/duplet configurations ($ns^2\, np^6$ or $1s^2$), small atomic radii, and high effective nuclear charges.

#### Anomaly 4: Group 13 Irregularities (Gallium vs. Aluminium) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Data:** $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Al}) = 577\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ vs. $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Ga}) = 579\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
- **Mechanism (d-Block Contraction / Transition Metal Contraction):**
  - $\text{Al } (Z=13): [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^2\, 3p^1$
  - $\text{Ga } (Z=31): [\text{Ar}]\, 3d^{10}\, 4s^2\, 4p^1$
  Between $\text{Al}$ and $\text{Ga}$, ten electrons fill the inner $3d$ subshell. Because $d$-orbitals are diffuse, they offer poor screening ($\sigma_d < \sigma_p < \sigma_s$). Consequently, the $4p^1$ electron in $\text{Ga}$ experiences an increased $Z_{\text{eff}}$, causing Ga's atomic radius to contract slightly ($\text{Ga: } 135\text{ pm} < \text{Al: } 143\text{ pm}$) and its ionization enthalpy to be slightly higher than Aluminium's.

#### Anomaly 5: Group 14/13 Heavy Elements (Inert Pair Effect & Lanthanoid Contraction)
Between $\text{In}$ and $\text{Tl}$ (or $\text{Sn}$ and $\text{Pb}$), the filling of $4f^{14}$ orbitals causes the **Lanthanoid Contraction**. Because $f$-orbitals shield nuclear charge poorly, $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases significantly, leading to high ionization enthalpies for 6th-period elements (e.g., $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Tl}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{In})$).

## 3. Solved Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

### Example 1: Predicting Successive Ionization Enthalpies & Valency
**Problem:** The first five ionization enthalpies ($\Delta_i H_1$ to $\Delta_i H_5$) of an unknown element $E$ (in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) are:

$$
578, \quad 1817, \quad 2745, \quad 11578, \quad 14842
$$

1. Determine the number of valence electrons.
2. Deduce the stable formula for the oxide and chloride of element $E$.

**Detailed Solution:**
- **Step 1:** Analyze the energy differences ($\Delta(\Delta_i H) = \Delta_i H_{n+1} - \Delta_i H_n$):
  - $\Delta_i H_2 - \Delta_i H_1 = 1817 - 578 = 1239\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - $\Delta_i H_3 - \Delta_i H_2 = 2745 - 1817 = 928\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - $\Delta_i H_4 - \Delta_i H_3 = 11578 - 2745 = 8833\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
- **Step 2:** Locate the largest jump:
  

$$
\frac{\Delta_i H_4}{\Delta_i H_3} = \frac{11578}{2745} \approx 4.22
$$

  A sharp increase of over $8800\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ occurs after removing the 3rd electron.
- **Step 3:** The first three electrons are removed from the outer valence shell. The 4th electron must be removed from an inner core with a noble-gas configuration ($n-1$).
- **Conclusion:**
  

$$
\text{Number of valence electrons} = 3 \implies \text{Valency} = +3
$$

  - The stable oxide formula is $\mathbf{E_2O_3}$.
  - The stable chloride formula is $\mathbf{ECl_3}$.
  *(Element $E$ is Aluminium, $\text{Al}$)*

***

### Example 2: Isoelectronic Series Radius Ordering
**Problem:** Arrange the following species in order of increasing ionic radius:

$$
\text{Mg}^{2+}, \quad \text{O}^{2-}, \quad \text{Na}^+, \quad \text{F}^-, \quad \text{Al}^{3+}, \quad \text{N}^{3-}
$$

**Detailed Solution:**
- **Step 1:** Determine the total number of electrons for each species:
  - $\text{Mg}^{2+}: 12 - 2 = 10\, e^-$
  - $\text{O}^{2-}: 8 - (-2) = 10\, e^-$
  - $\text{Na}^+: 11 - 1 = 10\, e^-$
  - $\text{F}^-: 9 - (-1) = 10\, e^-$
  - $\text{Al}^{3+}: 13 - 3 = 10\, e^-$
  - $\text{N}^{3-}: 7 - (-3) = 10\, e^-$
  All six species are isoelectronic with Neon ($10\, e^-$).
- **Step 2:** Apply the relationship between radius and nuclear charge ($Z$):
  

$$
\text{Radius} \propto \frac{1}{Z}
$$

- **Step 3:** List the nuclear charge ($Z$) for each:
  

$$
Z(\text{N}) = 7 < Z(\text{O}) = 8 < Z(\text{F}) = 9 < Z(\text{Na}) = 11 < Z(\text{Mg}) = 12 < Z(\text{Al}) = 13
$$

- **Final Result:**
  

$$
\boxed{\text{Al}^{3+} < \text{Mg}^{2+} < \text{Na}^+ < \text{F}^- < \text{O}^{2-} < \text{N}^{3-}}
$$

### Example 3: Comparison of Second Ionization Enthalpies ($\Delta_i H_2$)
**Problem:** Explain why the second ionization enthalpy of Sodium ($\text{Na}$) is significantly higher than that of Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), whereas the first ionization enthalpy shows the reverse trend.

**Detailed Solution:**
1. **First Ionization Process:**
   - $\text{Na } ([\text{Ne}]\, 3s^1) \to \text{Na}^+ ([\text{Ne}]) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_1 = 496\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})$
   - $\text{Mg } ([\text{Ne}]\, 3s^2) \to \text{Mg}^+ ([\text{Ne}]\, 3s^1) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_1 = 737\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})$
   $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Mg}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{Na})$ because Magnesium has higher $Z$ ($12$ vs. $11$) and a stable, fully filled $3s^2$ orbital.

2. **Second Ionization Process:**
   - $\text{Na}^+ ([\text{Ne}]) \to \text{Na}^{2+} (1s^2\, 2s^2\, 2p^5) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_2 = 4562\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})$
   - $\text{Mg}^+ ([\text{Ne}]\, 3s^1) \to \text{Mg}^{2+} ([\text{Ne}]) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_2 = 1451\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})$

3. **Comparison:**
   - For $\text{Na}^+$, removing a second electron requires disrupting a stable, closed noble-gas octet ($2s^2\, 2p^6$) with principal quantum number $n=2$. This electron is held by a high effective nuclear charge ($Z=11$ acting on $10$ electrons).
   - For $\text{Mg}^+$, the second electron is removed from the higher $n=3$ valence shell ($3s^1$), leaving behind a stable noble gas configuration ($[\text{Ne}]$).
   - Therefore:
     

$$
\boxed{\Delta_i H_2(\text{Na}) \gg \Delta_i H_2(\text{Mg})}
$$

## 4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

| Trap / Misconception | Erroneous Student Thinking | Correct Physical & NCERT Reality |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Noble Gas Radii** | "Noble gas is at the extreme right of Period 2, so Neon must have the smallest radius." | Neon's reported atomic radius is its **van der Waals radius**, not a covalent radius. Hence, its reported value is larger than the covalent radius of Lithium ($\text{Ne} = 160\text{ pm} > \text{Li} = 152\text{ pm}$). |
| **Missing the Gaseous State** | Writing $X(\text{s}) \to X^+(\text{g}) + e^-$ for ionization enthalpy. | **Zero marks.** The thermodynamic definition requires the atom to be in the **isolated gaseous state**: $X(\text{g}) \to X^+(\text{g}) + e^-$. Enthalpy of sublimation ($\Delta_{\text{sub}} H$) must not be included. |
| **$\text{N}$ vs. $\text{O}$ Ionization Trend** | Attributing the anomaly only to "half-filled stability" without mentioning electron-electron repulsion. | Nitrogen's half-filled shell is stabilized by exchange energy, but **inter-electronic repulsion** between the paired $2p_x^2$ electrons in Oxygen is equally crucial in lowering its first ionization enthalpy. |
| **Sign of Ionization Enthalpy** | Reporting $\Delta_i H$ as negative because "energy is released when a cation is formed." | Ionization requires work against the Coulombic field of the nucleus. It is always an **endothermic** process: $\Delta_i H > 0$. |
| **$\text{Al}$ vs. $\text{Ga}$ Radius Trend** | Assuming $\text{Ga}$ must be larger than $\text{Al}$ simply because it is lower down the group. | The insertion of the $3d^{10}$ subshell provides poor screening. Consequently, $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases and the radius of $\text{Ga}$ contracts ($\text{Ga} \approx 135\text{ pm} < \text{Al} \approx 143\text{ pm}$). |

# Periodic Trends in Electron Gain Enthalpy, Electronegativity, and Valence/Oxidation States

# Comprehensive Lecture Notes: Periodic Trends in Electron Gain Enthalpy, Electronegativity, and Valence / Oxidation States

**Target Audience:** Class 11 CBSE / NEET / JEE Main & Advanced  
**Curriculum Alignment:** Class 11 NCERT Chemistry, Unit 3: *Classification of Elements and Periodicity in Properties*  

***

# 1. Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$)

### Conceptual Definition & Physical Law
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

When an electron is added to a neutral, isolated, gaseous atom in its electronic ground state to convert it into a gaseous negative ion (uninegative anion), the enthalpy change accompanying the process is defined as the **Electron Gain Enthalpy** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$).

$$
\text{X}(g) + e^{-} \xrightarrow{T,\,P} \text{X}^{-}(g) \quad (\Delta H = \Delta_{\text{eg}}H)
$$

* **Thermodynamic Sign Convention:**
  * If energy is released when an electron is added, the process is **exothermic**, and $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ takes a **negative sign** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H < 0$).
  * If energy must be supplied to force an electron into an unstable or already negatively charged species, the process is **endothermic**, and $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ takes a **positive sign** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H > 0$).
* **SI Unit:** $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ or $\text{eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1}$ ($1\text{ eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1} \approx 96.485\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).

***

### Electron Affinity ($EA$) vs. Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

Historically, the ease of adding an electron was quantified as **Electron Affinity** ($EA$), defined as the negative of the internal energy change ($\Delta U$) at absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$):

$$
EA_0 = -\Delta U_0
$$

Using standard thermodynamic relations for an ideal gas reaction:

$$
\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta(PV) = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT
$$

For the addition of an electron:

$$
\text{X}(g) + e^{-}(g) \longrightarrow \text{X}^{-}(g)
$$

Here, $\Delta n_g = 1 - (1 + 1) = -1$ (treating the electron gas as an ideal monatomic gas):

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H = \Delta U - RT
$$

Since $EA = -\Delta U$:

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -EA - RT
$$

At room temperature ($T = 298.15\text{ K}$):

$$
\frac{5}{2}RT \approx 6.197\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Taking into account the translational kinetic energy of the free electron gas ($\frac{5}{2}RT$ term in statistical thermodynamics):

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -EA - \frac{5}{2}RT
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \approx -EA - 2.48\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \quad (\text{at } 298.15\text{ K})}
$$

* **Conclusion:** When an atom has a high positive electron affinity ($EA > 0$, strong attraction for electrons), its electron gain enthalpy is **large and negative** ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \ll 0$).

***

### Successive Electron Gain Enthalpies
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **First Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_1$):**
   Usually **negative** (exothermic) for most neutral non-metals, because the incoming electron experiences a net electrostatic attraction from the nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}}$.
   

$$
\text{O}(g) + e^{-} \longrightarrow \text{O}^{-}(g) \quad \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_1 = -141\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

2. **Second Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2$):**
   **Always positive** (endothermic) for all elements without exception:
   

$$
\text{O}^{-}(g) + e^{-} \longrightarrow \text{O}^{2-}(g) \quad \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2 = +780\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Net Enthalpy for }\text{O}^{2-}(g): \Delta H_{\text{total}} = \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_1 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2 = -141 + 780 = +639\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

* **Physical Origin:** The second electron must be added against the strong electrostatic repulsion of the existing uninegative ion ($\text{O}^{-}$). Work must be done *on* the system to overcome inter-electronic repulsion, requiring external energy.
* **Classroom Application Question:** *Why does $\text{O}^{2-}$ exist abundantly in ionic lattices (e.g., $\text{MgO}$) if the formation of gaseous $\text{O}^{2-}(g)$ is highly endothermic?*  
  **Answer:** The high lattice enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^{\ominus}$) resulting from the interaction of the compact divalent cation ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) and divalent anion ($\text{O}^{2-}$) via Coulomb's Law:
  

$$
U \propto \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_0}
$$

  releases enough energy to compensate for the endothermic second electron gain enthalpy.

***

### Factors Affecting Electron Gain Enthalpy
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$):**
   

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \propto -Z_{\text{eff}}
$$

   Higher positive charge pulls the incoming electron more tightly $\implies$ greater release of energy $\implies$ more negative $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$.
2. **Atomic Radius ($r$):**
   

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H \propto \frac{1}{r}
$$

   As the radius increases, the distance between the nucleus and the incoming electron increases, diminishing electrostatic attraction $\implies$ less negative $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$.
3. **Electronic Configuration (Half-filled & Fully-filled Subshells):**
   Noble gases ($ns^2 np^6$), alkaline earth metals ($ns^2$), and Group 15 elements ($ns^2 np^3$) possess extra exchange energy stability. Forcing an electron into these configurations disrupts symmetry and incurs an energy penalty.

***

### Periodic Trends and Critical Anomalies
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

* **Across a Period (Left to Right):**
  $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases, atomic size decreases. The incoming electron experiences stronger electrostatic pull. Therefore, **$\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ becomes progressively more negative**.
* **Down a Group (Top to Bottom):**
  Principal quantum number $n$ increases, atomic size increases, shielding increases. The incoming electron enters a shell farther from the nucleus. Therefore, **$\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ becomes progressively less negative**.

```
[Noble Gases]       --> Completely filled valence shells (s²p⁶) --> ΔegH is LARGE POSITIVE
[Group 2: Be, Mg]   --> Stable s² configuration                 --> ΔegH is POSITIVE or NEAR ZERO
[Group 15: N, P]    --> Stable half-filled p³ configuration     --> N has positive/near zero ΔegH
[Period 2 vs. 3]    --> Small size of 2p causes high repulsion   --> Cl > F, S > O, P > N
```

#### Anomaly 1: The Group 17 Halogen Anomaly ($\text{Cl}$ vs. $\text{F}$)
Experimental data:

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Cl}) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{F}) = -328\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{|\Delta_{\text{eg}}H|: \text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I}}
$$

* **Mechanism:** Fluorine has valence electrons in the compact $2p$ subshell ($n=2$). The electron cloud density in this compact volume is exceptionally high. When an incoming electron enters the $2p$ orbital of $\text{F}$, it experiences strong inter-electronic repulsions from the existing 7 valence electrons.
* In Chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger $3p$ orbital ($n=3$). The electron density is lower and spread over a larger spatial region, so inter-electronic repulsions are significantly reduced. The net attractive force from the nucleus dominates.

#### Anomaly 2: Group 16 Chalcogen Anomaly ($\text{O}$ vs. $\text{S}$)

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{S}) = -200\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Se}) = -195\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Te}) = -190\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Po}) = -174\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{O}) = -141\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Negative value order: } \text{S} > \text{Se} > \text{Te} > \text{Po} > \text{O}}
$$

Oxygen has the **least negative** $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ in Group 16 because of the severe inter-electronic repulsion in its compact $2p^4$ shell.

#### Anomaly 3: Nitrogen vs. Carbon and Oxygen
* $\text{C}\ (2s^2 2p^2): \Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -122\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
* $\text{N}\ (2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1): \Delta_{\text{eg}}H = +31\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (or $\approx +2\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ depending on reference)
* $\text{O}\ (2s^2 2p^4): \Delta_{\text{eg}}H = -141\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
* Nitrogen has a symmetric, half-filled $2p^3$ configuration with maximum exchange energy ($K = 3$). Adding an electron forces pairing into one of the half-filled orbitals ($2p_x^2 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$), which introduces Coulombic pairing energy and reduces orbital symmetry.

# 2. Electronegativity ($\chi$)

### Definition and Conceptual Basis
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

**Electronegativity ($\chi$)** is a qualitative/relative property defined as:
> *The tendency of an atom in a chemical compound to attract a shared pair of electrons towards itself.*

* Unlike Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$) or Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$), which are measurable physical quantities of **isolated gaseous atoms**, electronegativity is **not an absolute measurable physical quantity**. It has no formal physical dimensions or SI units.

***

### Quantitative Scales of Electronegativity
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

#### 1. Pauling Scale (Based on Bond Energies)
Linus Pauling (1932) derived electronegativity values from thermochemical bond dissociation energies.

For a heteronuclear diatomic molecule $\text{A}-\text{B}$, if the bond were $100\%$ purely covalent, the bond energy $E_{\text{A}-\text{B}}$ would equal the geometric mean of the homonuclear bond energies $E_{\text{A}-\text{A}}$ and $E_{\text{B}-\text{B}}$:

$$
E_{\text{A}-\text{B, theoretical}} = \sqrt{E_{\text{A}-\text{A}} \cdot E_{\text{B}-\text{B}}}
$$

Because of partial ionic character arising from the polarity $\text{A}^{\delta+}-\text{B}^{\delta-}$, the actual bond energy $E_{\text{A}-\text{B, experimental}}$ is greater than the theoretical covalent energy. The resonance ionic-covalent energy stabilization is:

$$
\Delta = E_{\text{A}-\text{B}} - \sqrt{E_{\text{A}-\text{A}} \cdot E_{\text{B}-\text{B}}}
$$

Pauling related this excess ionic resonance energy $\Delta$ to the electronegativity difference $|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}|$:

$$
\boxed{|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}| = 0.208 \sqrt{\Delta} \quad (\text{if energies are in }\text{kcal}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})}
$$

$$
\boxed{|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}| = 0.1017 \sqrt{\Delta} \quad (\text{if energies are in }\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})}
$$

* **Arbitrary Standard Reference:** Pauling arbitrarily assigned a value of **$\chi_{\text{F}} = 4.0$** to Fluorine (the most electronegative element).

***

#### 2. Mulliken Scale (Absolute Scale)
Robert Mulliken (1934) proposed that an atom's attraction for electrons depends on both how tightly it holds its own electrons (Ionization Enthalpy) and how strongly it attracts additional electrons (Electron Affinity).

$$
\chi_{\text{Mulliken}} = \frac{IE + EA}{2} \quad (\text{where } IE \text{ and } EA \text{ are in }\text{eV}\cdot\text{atom}^{-1})
$$

If values are given in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$:

$$
\chi_{\text{Mulliken}} = \frac{IE + EA}{2 \times 96.485} = \frac{IE + EA}{540}
$$

**Conversion between Pauling and Mulliken Scales:**
Pauling values are smaller than Mulliken values by a factor of approximately $2.8$:

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{Pauling}} \approx \frac{\chi_{\text{Mulliken}}}{2.8} = \frac{IE + EA}{5.6} \quad (\text{in eV})}
$$

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{Pauling}} \approx \frac{IE + EA}{540 \times 2.8} \approx \frac{IE + EA}{1512} \quad (\text{in }\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})}
$$

***

#### 3. Allred-Rochow Scale (Electrostatic Force Scale)
A.L. Allred and E.G. Rochow (1958) defined electronegativity as the electrostatic force exerted by the effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) on the valence electrons at the covalent radius ($r_{\text{cov}}$):

$$
F = \frac{Z_{\text{eff}} \cdot e^2}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 \cdot r_{\text{cov}}^2}
$$

Using Slater's rules to calculate $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and setting the parameters to align with the Pauling scale:

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{AR}} = 0.359 \left( \frac{Z_{\text{eff}}}{r^2} \right) + 0.744}
$$

Where $r$ is the covalent radius in Angstroms ($\text{Å}$).

***

### Factors Influencing Electronegativity
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **Oxidation State of the Central Atom:**
   

$$
\chi \propto \text{Positive Oxidation State}
$$

   * Example: $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ has a higher effective nuclear charge and smaller ionic radius than $\text{Fe}^{2+}$. Hence:
     

$$
\chi(\text{Fe}^{3+}) > \chi(\text{Fe}^{2+})
$$

   * Example: $\text{MnO}_4^{-}$ ($\text{Mn}^{7+}$, acidic) vs. $\text{MnO}$ ($\text{Mn}^{2+}$, basic).
2. **Hybridization State:**
   Electronegativity increases directly with the percentage of $s$-character, because $s$-orbitals are more spherical, penetrative, and closer to the nucleus than $p$-orbitals:
   

$$
\% s\text{-character}: \quad sp\ (50\%) > sp^2\ (33.3\%) > sp^3\ (25\%)
$$

   

$$
\boxed{\chi: \quad \text{C}(sp) > \text{C}(sp^2) > \text{C}(sp^3)}
$$

   * Numerical values: $\text{C}(sp) \approx 3.29$, $\text{C}(sp^2) \approx 2.75$, $\text{C}(sp^3) \approx 2.48$. This explains why terminal alkynes ($\text{R}-\text{C}\equiv\text{C}-\text{H}$) are weakly acidic.

***

### Applications of Electronegativity

#### 1. Calculation of Partial Ionic Character (Hanny-Smyth Equation)
For a covalent single bond $\text{A}-\text{B}$:

$$
\boxed{\% \text{ Ionic Character} = 16|\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}}| + 3.5(\chi_{\text{A}} - \chi_{\text{B}})^2}
$$

* If $|\Delta\chi| = 1.7 \implies \% \text{ Ionic Character} \approx 50\%$.
* If $|\Delta\chi| > 1.7 \implies$ Predominantly **ionic**.
* If $|\Delta\chi| < 1.7 \implies$ Predominantly **covalent**.

#### 2. Acid-Base Character of Oxides
Consider an element $\text{E}$ bonded to oxygen in the linkage $\text{E}-\text{O}-\text{H}$:
* If $|\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{E}}| > |\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{H}}|$: The bond $\text{E}-\text{O}$ cleaves heterolytically yielding $\text{E}^{+} + \text{OH}^{-}$. The substance acts as a **base**.
* If $|\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{E}}| < |\chi_{\text{O}} - \chi_{\text{H}}|$: The bond $\text{O}-\text{H}$ cleaves yielding $\text{E}-\text{O}^{-} + \text{H}^{+}$. The substance acts as an **acid**.

$$
\boxed{\text{Acidic strength of oxides increases across a period: } \text{Na}_2\text{O} \text{ (strongly basic)} \to \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \text{ (amphoteric)} \to \text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7 \text{ (strongly acidic)}}
$$

# 3. Valence and Oxidation States

### Concept Differentiation
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

| Parameter | Valence (Valency) | Oxidation State / Number |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Combining capacity of an element; formal number of bonds formed. | Real or hypothetical charge an atom possesses when all bonding partners are removed as ions. |
| **Sign** | Always a pure, unsigned integer ($1, 2, 3, \dots$). | Can be positive, negative, zero, or fractional. |
| **Value in $\text{CH}_4$** | Carbon has valence = $4$ | Carbon has oxidation state = $-4$ |
| **Value in $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$** | Carbon has valence = $4$ | Carbon has oxidation state = $0$ |

***

### Periodic Trends in Valence
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

1. **Representative Elements (s- and p-block):**
   * **Valence with respect to Hydrogen / Halogens:**
     Increases from $1$ to $4$, then decreases to $1$:
     * Group 1: $\text{LiH}$ (Valence 1)
     * Group 2: $\text{CaH}_2$ (Valence 2)
     * Group 13: $\text{AlH}_3$ (Valence 3)
     * Group 14: $\text{CH}_4$ (Valence 4)
     * Group 15: $\text{NH}_3$ (Valence 3)
     * Group 16: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Valence 2)
     * Group 17: $\text{HF}$ (Valence 1)
2. **Valence with respect to Oxygen:**
   Increases linearly from $1$ to $7$:
   * Group 1: $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$ (Valence 1)
   * Group 2: $\text{MgO}$ (Valence 2)
   * Group 13: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ (Valence 3)
   * Group 14: $\text{CO}_2$ (Valence 4)
   * Group 15: $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ (Valence 5)
   * Group 16: $\text{SO}_3$ (Valence 6)
   * Group 17: $\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$ (Valence 7)

***

### Anomalous Properties of Second Period Elements
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

The elements of the second period ($\text{Li}, \text{Be}, \text{B}, \text{C}, \text{N}, \text{O}, \text{F}$) differ significantly from their heavier group congeners due to:
1. **Extremely small atomic and ionic size.**
2. **High electronegativity and ionization enthalpy.**
3. **High charge-to-radius ratio ($\frac{e}{r}$, polarizing power).**
4. **Complete absence of vacant $d$-orbitals in the valence shell ($n=2$).**

#### Covariance and Covalency Restriction
* For Period 2 elements, the valence shell ($n=2$) contains only four orbitals ($2s, 2p_x, 2p_y, 2p_z$).
* **Maximum covalency for Period 2 elements is strictly 4.**
  * Example: Boron forms $[\text{BF}_4]^{-}$, but **cannot** form $[\text{BF}_6]^{3-}$.
  * In contrast, Aluminum ($n=3$) has accessible $3d$ orbitals and readily forms $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$.
  * Nitrogen forms $\text{NCl}_3$ and $\text{NF}_3$, but **cannot** form $\text{NCl}_5$ or $\text{NF}_5$. Phosphorus forms both $\text{PCl}_3$ and $\text{PCl}_5$.

#### $\pi$-Bonding Differences
* Period 2 elements have small, compact $p$-orbitals that can overlap laterally to form stable **$p\pi-p\pi$ multiple bonds** (e.g., $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}, \text{C}=\text{O}, \text{N}\equiv\text{N}, \text{N}=\text{O}$).
* Heavier elements (Period 3 onwards) have diffused, larger $p$-orbitals that cannot achieve effective sideways overlap. Instead, they form single $\sigma$-bonds or use $d$-orbitals for **$d\pi-p\pi$** or **$d\pi-d\pi$** bonding (e.g., $\text{R}_3\text{P}=\text{O}$, $\text{R}_2\text{S}=\text{O}$).

***

### Inert Pair Effect
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

In the heavier $p$-block elements (specifically periods 6 and 7: $\text{Tl}, \text{Pb}, \text{Bi}$), the valence $s$-electrons ($6s^2$) remain unshared and resist participation in chemical bonding.

* **Underlying Physical Origin:**
  The $6s$ electrons are preceded by filled $4f^{14}$ and $5d^{10}$ subshells. Both $f$ and $d$ orbitals have diffused spatial shapes and provide poor shielding of the nuclear charge:
  

$$
\text{Shielding efficiency: } s > p > d > f
$$

  The imperfect shielding allows the large nuclear charge ($Z = 81$ to $83$) to pull the valence $6s$ orbital closer to the nucleus (further reinforced by relativistic contraction). As a consequence, unpairing and promoting the $6s$ electrons requires an energy investment that cannot be offset by the bond energy of the two additional covalent bonds.

* **Chemical Consequences:**
  * Group 13: $\text{Tl}^{+}$ is more stable than $\text{Tl}^{3+}$. (In fact, $\text{Tl}^{3+}$ acts as a powerful **oxidizing agent**).
  * Group 14: $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ is more stable than $\text{Pb}^{4+}$. (Therefore, $\text{PbO}_2$ is a strong **oxidizing agent**: $\text{Pb}^{4+} + 2e^{-} \to \text{Pb}^{2+}$).
  * Group 14: $\text{Sn}^{2+}$ is less stable than $\text{Sn}^{4+}$. (Therefore, $\text{Sn}^{2+}$ acts as a **reducing agent**: $\text{Sn}^{2+} \to \text{Sn}^{4+} + 2e^{-}$).

# 4. Solved Exemplar & Advanced Problems

### Problem 1: Born-Haber Cycle Application to Electron Gain Enthalpies
**Question:** Calculate the electron gain enthalpy of chlorine atoms given the following standard thermodynamic data for sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$):
1. Sublimation enthalpy of $\text{Na}(s)$: $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^{\ominus} = +108.4\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
2. Ionization enthalpy of $\text{Na}(g)$: $\Delta_i H^{\ominus} = +495.8\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
3. Bond dissociation enthalpy of $\text{Cl}_2(g)$: $\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^{\ominus} = +242.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
4. Lattice enthalpy of $\text{NaCl}(s)$: $\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^{\ominus} = -788.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
5. Standard enthalpy of formation of $\text{NaCl}(s)$: $\Delta_f H^{\ominus} = -411.2\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Write the chemical equation for the standard enthalpy of formation:

$$
\text{Na}(s) + \frac{1}{2}\text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(s) \quad (\Delta_f H^{\ominus} = -411.2\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1})
$$

Step 2: Construct the Born-Haber thermochemical cycle:

$$
\Delta_f H^{\ominus} = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Na}] + \Delta_i H^{\ominus}[\text{Na}] + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}_2] + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] + \Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^{\ominus}[\text{NaCl}]
$$

Step 3: Substitute the known values:

$$
-411.2 = 108.4 + 495.8 + \frac{1}{2}(242.0) + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] + (-788.0)
$$

$$
-411.2 = 108.4 + 495.8 + 121.0 - 788.0 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}]
$$

$$
-411.2 = 725.2 - 788.0 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}]
$$

$$
-411.2 = -62.8 + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}]
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] = -411.2 + 62.8 = -348.4\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\text{eg}}H^{\ominus}[\text{Cl}] = -348.4\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

***

### Problem 2: Electronegativity Calculation using Pauling's Formula
**Question:** The bond dissociation energies of $\text{H}-\text{H}$, $\text{Br}-\text{Br}$, and $\text{H}-\text{Br}$ bonds are $435\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, $192\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, and $368\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ respectively. Given that the Pauling electronegativity of hydrogen is $\chi_{\text{H}} = 2.1$, calculate the electronegativity of Bromine ($\chi_{\text{Br}}$).

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Compute theoretical purely covalent bond energy $E_{\text{H}-\text{Br, covalent}}$:

$$
E_{\text{covalent}} = \sqrt{E_{\text{H}-\text{H}} \cdot E_{\text{Br}-\text{Br}}} = \sqrt{435 \cdot 192} = \sqrt{83520} \approx 289.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Step 2: Calculate the ionic resonance stabilization energy $\Delta$:

$$
\Delta = E_{\text{experimental}} - E_{\text{covalent}} = 368 - 289.0 = 79.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Step 3: Apply the Pauling formula (in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$):

$$
|\chi_{\text{Br}} - \chi_{\text{H}}| = 0.1017 \times \sqrt{\Delta}
$$

$$
|\chi_{\text{Br}} - 2.1| = 0.1017 \times \sqrt{79.0} = 0.1017 \times 8.888 \approx 0.904
$$

Since Bromine is more electronegative than Hydrogen:

$$
\chi_{\text{Br}} = 2.1 + 0.904 = 3.004
$$

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\text{Br}} \approx 3.0}
$$

***

### Problem 3: Percentage Ionic Character
**Question:** Calculate the percentage ionic character of the $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond in methyl chloride ($\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$) if the electronegativities of Carbon and Chlorine are $2.5$ and $3.0$ respectively, using the Hannay-Smyth equation.

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Determine the electronegativity difference:

$$
|\Delta\chi| = |\chi_{\text{Cl}} - \chi_{\text{C}}| = 3.0 - 2.5 = 0.5
$$

Step 2: Apply the Hannay-Smyth equation:

$$
\% \text{ Ionic Character} = 16|\Delta\chi| + 3.5(\Delta\chi)^2
$$

$$
\% \text{ Ionic Character} = 16(0.5) + 3.5(0.5)^2 = 8.0 + 3.5(0.25) = 8.0 + 0.875 = 8.875\%
$$

$$
\boxed{\% \text{ Ionic Character of }\text{C}-\text{Cl} = 8.88\%}
$$

***

### Problem 4: Oxidation State vs. Covalency in Nitrogen Oxides (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
**Question:** In dinitrogen pentoxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$), what are the formal oxidation state and the maximum covalency of nitrogen? Explain why nitrogen does not exhibit a covalency equal to its oxidation state.

**Solution:**  
Step 1: Calculate the oxidation state of Nitrogen:

$$
2x + 5(-2) = 0 \implies 2x = 10 \implies x = +5
$$

The oxidation state of Nitrogen in $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ is **$+5$**.

Step 2: Determine the covalency from the Lewis structure:
In solid $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$, the molecule exists as $[\text{NO}_2]^{+}[\text{NO}_3]^{-}$. In the vapor phase, it exists as a symmetric molecule $\text{O}_2\text{N}-\text{O}-\text{NO}_2$. Each nitrogen atom forms:
* One single bond to the bridging oxygen atom ($\sigma$).
* One coordinate/dative bond to a terminal oxygen atom.
* One double bond ($\sigma + \pi$) to another terminal oxygen atom.
Total pairs of shared electrons per Nitrogen atom $= 4$.  
Therefore, the covalency of Nitrogen is **$4$**.

Step 3: Justification:
Nitrogen is in the second period ($2s^2 2p^3$). The maximum number of orbitals available for chemical bonding is **4** (one $2s$ and three $2p$ orbitals). Because Nitrogen lacks energetically accessible $2d$ orbitals, it cannot expand its coordination sphere or covalency beyond 4. Hence, its covalency ($4$) cannot match its oxidation state ($+5$).

$$
\boxed{\text{Oxidation State} = +5, \quad \text{Covalency} = 4}
$$

# 5. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

```
Common Mistakes & Traps:
1. Writing ΔegH for F > Cl    --> WRONG: Cl has more negative ΔegH than F (-349 vs -328 kJ/mol).
2. Forgetting the minus sign  --> WRONG: "ΔegH of Cl is 349 kJ/mol" (must specify -349 kJ/mol).
3. Confounding EA and ΔegH    --> WRONG: EA is positive for exothermicity, while ΔegH is negative.
4. Second electron gain       --> TRAP: ΔegH2 is ALWAYS POSITIVE (endothermic) for ALL atoms.
5. Max covalency of Period 2  --> TRAP: Nitrogen forms N2O5 (O.S. = +5), but its covalency is 4.
```

### Pitfall 1: Sign Conventions in $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ vs. $EA$
* **The Error:** Students write "The electron gain enthalpy of chlorine is $349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$."
* **NCERT Marking Criterion:** If the term **Enthalpy** is used, the **negative sign is mandatory** for exothermic processes.
  * Correct: $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Cl}) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  * Correct: $EA(\text{Cl}) = +349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  * Stating a positive $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$ for chlorine implies an endothermic process, resulting in a zero mark.

### Pitfall 2: Ordering by Absolute Magnitude vs. Thermodynamic Value
* **The Trap:** A question asks: *"Arrange Group 17 elements in the order of increasing electron gain enthalpy."*
* **Examiner Mindset:**
  * If the question asks for **electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$)**: More negative numbers are mathematically smaller.
    

$$
\text{Cl } (-349) < \text{F } (-328) < \text{Br } (-325) < \text{I } (-295)
$$

  * If the question asks for the **tendency to gain an electron** or **magnitude of energy released ($|\Delta_{\text{eg}}H|$)**:
    

$$
\text{I} < \text{Br} < \text{F} < \text{Cl}
$$

* **Tip:** Always specify: *"In terms of magnitude of energy released: $\text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$. In terms of standard thermodynamic sign: $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Cl}) < \Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{F}) < \Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{Br}) < \Delta_{\text{eg}}H(\text{I})$."*

### Pitfall 3: Second Electron Gain Enthalpy is Always Endothermic
* **The Error:** Students assume that because $\text{O}^{2-}$ has a stable noble-gas configuration ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$), its formation must be exothermic.
* **Reality:** The electrostatic repulsion between the incoming electron and the negatively charged ion ($\text{O}^{-}$) is larger than the attraction from the nucleus. Thus, $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_2$ is **always positive (endothermic)** for every element without exception.

### Pitfall 4: Conflating Oxidation State with Covalency
* **The Error:** Claiming that Nitrogen has a covalency of $5$ in $\text{HNO}_3$ or $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$.
* **Fact:** Second period elements **cannot** exceed a covalency of 4 because they lack $d$-orbitals. The oxidation state of Nitrogen in $\text{HNO}_3$ is $+5$, but its covalency is strictly 4.

# 6. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Golden Revision Points

### 1. The "FONCl BrISCH" Electronegativity Memory Line
To compare bond polarities and oxidation numbers instantly, memorize the Pauling values using this descending order:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{F}\,(4.0) > \mathbf{O}\,(3.5) > \mathbf{N}\,(3.0) \approx \mathbf{Cl}\,(3.0) > \mathbf{Br}\,(2.8) > \mathbf{I}\,(2.5) \approx \mathbf{S}\,(2.5) \approx \mathbf{C}\,(2.5) > \mathbf{P}\,(2.1) \approx \mathbf{H}\,(2.1)}
$$

* $\text{N}$ and $\text{Cl}$ have identical Pauling electronegativities ($\approx 3.0$), but Nitrogen forms strong hydrogen bonds while Chlorine generally does not. This is because **$\text{N}$ is much smaller ($2p$) than $\text{Cl}$ ($3p$)**, giving it a significantly higher volume-charge density.

***

### 2. Period 2 vs. Period 3 Electron Gain "Step-Down" Rule
For negative electron gain enthalpy, **Period 3 elements are always more negative than their Period 2 group partners**:

$$
\Delta_{\text{eg}}H_{\text{negative}}: \quad \text{Period 3} > \text{Period 2}
$$

* Group 17: $\text{Cl} > \text{F}$
* Group 16: $\text{S} > \text{O}$
* Group 15: $\text{P} > \text{N}$
* Group 14: $\text{Si} > \text{C}$

*Exception in Group 16:* Oxygen drops all the way to the bottom of its group:

$$
\text{Negative }\Delta_{\text{eg}}H: \quad \text{S} > \text{Se} > \text{Te} > \text{Po} > \mathbf{O}
$$

***

### 3. Quick Acidic/Basic Oxide Diagnostic
For any normal oxide $\text{E}_x\text{O}_y$:
* **High Oxidation State ($\ge +5$):** Acidic (e.g., $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5, \text{CrO}_3, \text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7, \text{SO}_3, \text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7, \text{N}_2\text{O}_5$).
* **Intermediate Oxidation State ($+3, +4$):** Often Amphoteric (e.g., $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{ZnO}, \text{SnO}_2, \text{PbO}_2$).
* **Low Oxidation State ($+1, +2$):** Basic (e.g., $\text{Na}_2\text{O}, \text{CaO}, \text{BaO}, \text{MnO}$).
* **Neutral Oxides (Class 11 NCERT Non-negotiable List):**
  

$$
\boxed{\text{CO}, \quad \text{NO}, \quad \text{N}_2\text{O}, \quad \text{OF}_2 \text{ (oxygen fluoride)}}
$$

***

### 4. Group Oxidation State Boundaries
For elements of group $n$:
* **Maximum Group Oxidation State:**
  * For $s$-block: Equal to group number ($+1$ for Gp 1, $+2$ for Gp 2).
  * For $p$-block: $\text{Group Number} - 10$ (e.g., Group 15 is $+5$, Group 16 is $+6$, Group 17 is $+7$).
* **Minimum Oxidation State for $p$-block:**
  

$$
\text{Minimum O.S.} = (\text{Group Number} - 10) - 8
$$

  * Example for Sulfur (Group 16): $\text{Max} = +6$, $\text{Min} = 16 - 10 - 8 = -2$.
  * Any species in its maximum oxidation state (e.g., $\text{HNO}_3, \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4, \text{KMnO}_4$) can **only act as an oxidizing agent**.
  * Any species in its minimum oxidation state (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{S}, \text{HI}, \text{NH}_3$) can **only act as a reducing agent**.

### 1.1 Periodic Trends in Chemical Reactivity ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
*(NCERT Core Concept & High-Frequency Exam Area)*

Chemical reactivity represents the tendency of an atom to undergo chemical combination by losing, gaining, or sharing electrons to achieve a stable electronic configuration (frequently an octet).

#### A. Reactivity across a Period (Left to Right)
Across a period from Group 1 to Group 17:
1. **Extreme Left (Group 1 - Alkali Metals):** Characterized by very low first ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1$), high negative standard oxidation potential ($E^\circ_{\text{ox}}$), and high electropositivity. These elements react predominantly by **losing** valence electrons:
   

$$
\text{M}(s) \longrightarrow \text{M}^+(g) + e^-
$$

2. **Extreme Right (Group 17 - Halogens):** Characterized by very high electron gain enthalpy with large negative magnitude ($\Delta_{eg} H$), high electronegativity ($\chi$), and high positive reduction potentials. These elements react predominantly by **gaining** an electron:
   

$$
\text{X}(g) + e^- \longrightarrow \text{X}^-(g)
$$

3. **The Center of the Periodic Table (Groups 13, 14):** Elements have intermediate electronegativities and high ionization enthalpies, resulting in a minimum in overall ionic reactivity and a predominance of **covalent sharing of electrons**.

```
Trend in Chemical Reactivity across a Period:
[High Reactivity (Metals)] ----> [Minimum (Group 13/14)] ----> [High Reactivity (Non-metals)]
   (Readily lose e⁻)               (Covalent bonding)               (Readily gain e⁻)
```

> **Formal NCERT Generalization:**  
> The chemical reactivity of elements is highest at the two extremes (extreme left and extreme right) and lowest in the center of a period. The maximum reactivity on the extreme left is manifested through ease of oxidation (reducing nature), while on the extreme right it is manifested through ease of reduction (oxidizing nature).

***

#### B. Periodicity of Acid-Base Characteristics of Oxides and Hydroxides ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The nature of oxides formed by elements across a period reflects their metallic or non-metallic character:

1. **Basic Oxides:** Formed by electropositive elements on the extreme left. When dissolved in water, they furnish hydroxide ions ($\text{OH}^-$):
   

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{O}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{Na}^+(aq) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq)
$$

2. **Amphoteric Oxides:** Formed by elements near the boundary between metals and non-metals (e.g., $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $\text{BeO}$, $\text{Ga}_2\text{O}_3$). They react with both acids and strong bases:
   

$$
\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 6\text{HCl}(aq) + 9\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2[\text{Al}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6]^{3+}(aq) + 6\text{Cl}^-(aq)
$$

   

$$
\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{Na}[\text{Al}(\text{OH})_4](aq)
$$

3. **Neutral Oxides:** Oxides that exhibit neither acidic nor basic properties (e.g., $\text{CO}$, $\text{NO}$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$).
4. **Acidic Oxides:** Formed by electronegative elements on the right. When dissolved in water, they furnish hydronium ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$):
   

$$
\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7(l) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{HClO}_4(aq) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}^+(aq) + 2\text{ClO}_4^-(aq)
$$

```
Period 3 Oxide Progression:
Na₂O       MgO        Al₂O₃        SiO₂          P₄O₁₀         SO₃          Cl₂O₇
Strongly   Basic      Amphoteric   Weakly        Acidic        Strongly     Very Strongly
Basic                              Acidic                      Acidic       Acidic
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------>
                  Electronegativity of Central Atom Increases
                   Acidic Character of Oxide Increases
```

### 1.2 Anomalous Properties of Second Period Elements ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The first member of each group of representative elements (Period 2: $\text{Li}$, $\text{Be}$, $\text{B}$, $\text{C}$, $\text{N}$, $\text{O}$, $\text{F}$) differs significantly from the heavier congeners (Periods 3 to 6) in the same group.

#### Causes of Anomalous Behavior:
1. **Exceptionally Small Covalent and Ionic Radii:** Leading to very high packing density, high ionization enthalpy, and high electronegativity.
2. **High Charge-to-Radius Ratio ($\frac{q}{r}$ or Ionic Potential, $\phi$):**
   

$$
\phi = \frac{\text{Cationic Charge } (z)}{\text{Cationic Radius } (r)}
$$

   High $\phi$ imparts pronounced polarizing power (Fajans' Rules), causing compounds of $\text{Li}^+$ and $\text{Be}^{2+}$ to be predominantly covalent compared to the purely ionic nature of downstream congeners.
3. **Absence of Low-Lying Valence $d$-Orbitals:**
   - Period 2 elements possess only four valence orbitals: one $2s$ and three $2p$.
   - **Strict Maximum Covalency Limit:** Second-period elements cannot expand their valence shell beyond 4.
   - Period 3+ elements possess vacant, accessible $nd$-orbitals, allowing valence shell expansion (hypervalency):
     - Boron forms $[\text{BF}_4]^-$, but Aluminum forms $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$.
     - Carbon forms $\text{CF}_4$, but Silicon forms $[\text{SiF}_6]^{2-}$.
     - Nitrogen forms only $\text{NF}_3$ and cannot form $\text{NCl}_5$ or $\text{NF}_5$, whereas Phosphorus forms $\text{PCl}_5$, $\text{PF}_5$, and $[\text{PCl}_6]^-$.
4. **Pronounced Ability to Form Multiple Bonds ($p\pi - p\pi$ Bonding):**
   - Period 2 elements form strong $p\pi - p\pi$ multiple bonds with themselves and with other small electronegative atoms ($\text{C}=\text{C}$, $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$, $\text{C}=\text{O}$, $\text{C}\equiv\text{N}$, $\text{N}=\text{O}$, $\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$).
   - Heavier congeners (such as $\text{Si}$, $\text{P}$, $\text{As}$) possess larger, diffuse $3p$ or $4p$ orbitals. Their lateral (sideways) overlap is weak due to interatomic repulsion of core shells, rendering $p\pi - p\pi$ bonding ineffective. Instead, they stabilize via single bonds with catenation or involve $d$-orbitals ($d\pi - p\pi$ or $d\pi - d\pi$).

***

### 1.3 Diagonal Relationships ⭐⭐⭐⭐
In the periodic table, certain elements of Period 2 display a striking similarity in physical and chemical properties to elements of Period 3 located diagonally down and to the right:
- **Lithium ($\text{Li}$, Group 1) $\longleftrightarrow$ Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$, Group 2)**
- **Beryllium ($\text{Be}$, Group 2) $\longleftrightarrow$ Aluminum ($\text{Al}$, Group 13)**
- **Boron ($\text{B}$, Group 13) $\longleftrightarrow$ Silicon ($\text{Si}$, Group 14)**

#### Fundamental Origin:
The diagonal similarity arises from the mutual cancellation of two opposing periodic vectors:
1. **Moving left to right across a period:**
   - Atomic/ionic radius **decreases**.
   - Nuclear charge **increases**.
   - Electronegativity and polarizing power ($\phi$) **increase**.
2. **Moving down a group:**
   - Atomic/ionic radius **increases**.
   - Effective nuclear charge remains roughly constant per valence electron while principal quantum number increases.
   - Electronegativity and polarizing power ($\phi$) **decrease**.

When traversing diagonally downwards and to the right:

$$
\Delta r_{\text{period}} (-) + \Delta r_{\text{group}} (+) \approx 0
$$

$$
\phi = \frac{z}{r} \approx \text{constant}
$$

Because their **ionic potentials ($\phi$)**, **electronegativities**, and **standard reduction potentials** are nearly identical, these diagonal pairs exhibit remarkably similar chemistry.

### 2.1 The Diagonal Pair: Lithium ($\text{Li}$) and Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Direct Reaction with Atmospheric Dinitrogen
Unlike all other alkali metals, $\text{Li}$ reacts directly with atmospheric $\text{N}_2$ at elevated temperatures, exactly like $\text{Mg}$, due to high lattice stabilization energy ($\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ$) of the small $\text{N}^{3-}$ ion interacting with small cations ($\text{Li}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
6\text{Li}(s) + \text{N}_2(g) &\xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Li}_3\text{N}(s) \\
3\text{Mg}(s) + \text{N}_2(g) &\xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Mg}_3\text{N}_2(s)
\end{aligned}
$$

*Contrast with Group 1:* $\text{Na}$, $\text{K}$, $\text{Rb}$, $\text{Cs}$ do not form stable nitrides under standard heating.

Both nitrides undergo complete hydrolysis in water to produce ammonia:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Li}_3\text{N}(s) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) &\longrightarrow 3\text{LiOH}(aq) + \text{NH}_3(g)\uparrow \\
\text{Mg}_3\text{N}_2(s) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) &\longrightarrow 3\text{Mg}(\text{OH})_2(s) + 2\text{NH}_3(g)\uparrow
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 2. Thermal Decomposition of Carbonates and Nitrates
- **Carbonate Pyrolysis:** The carbonates of $\text{Li}$ and $\text{Mg}$ decompose on heating because the small cations polarize the large, polarizable carbonate anion ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$), facilitating cleavage of the $\text{C}-\text{O}$ bond to yield stable, high-lattice-energy oxides:

$$
\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Li}_2\text{O}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

$$
\text{MgCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{MgO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

*Contrast:* Other alkali metal carbonates ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, $\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3$) are thermally stable up to their melting points ($> 800\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

- **Nitrate Pyrolysis:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
4\text{LiNO}_3(s) &\xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Li}_2\text{O}(s) + 4\text{NO}_2(g)\uparrow + \text{O}_2(g)\uparrow \\
2\text{Mg}(\text{NO}_3)_2(s) &\xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{MgO}(s) + 4\text{NO}_2(g)\uparrow + \text{O}_2(g)\uparrow
\end{aligned}
$$

*Contrast:* Other alkali metal nitrates decompose only to nitrites without releasing brown $\text{NO}_2$:

$$
2\text{NaNO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{NaNO}_2(s) + \text{O}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

#### 3. Deliquescence and Halide Hydration
Because of their high hydration enthalpies:
- Both $\text{LiCl}$ and $\text{MgCl}_2$ are deliquescent and crystallize from aqueous solution as hydrated salts:
  

$$
\text{LiCl}\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad \text{and} \quad \text{MgCl}_2\cdot 8\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{or } \text{MgCl}_2\cdot 6\text{H}_2\text{O})
$$

- $\text{NaCl}$ and $\text{KCl}$ do not form hydrates under ambient conditions.

### 2.2 The Diagonal Pair: Beryllium ($\text{Be}$) and Aluminum ($\text{Al}$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Action of Non-Oxidizing Acids and Passivation by Nitric Acid
- **Reaction with Dilute Hydrochloric Acid:**
  

$$
\text{Be}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{BeCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

  

$$
\text{Equivalent Ionic Form:} \quad \text{Be}(s) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow [\text{Be}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_4]^{2+}(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

  

$$
2\text{Al}(s) + 6\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3(aq) + 3\text{H}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

- **Passivation by Concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$:**
  Both $\text{Be}$ and $\text{Al}$ are rendered chemically passive by concentrated nitric acid due to the immediate formation of an impervious, protective, microscopic oxide film ($\text{BeO}$ and $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) on the metal surface:
  

$$
\text{M}(s) \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } \text{HNO}_3} \text{M}_x\text{O}_y \text{ (surface film)} \quad [\text{Halts further dissolution}]
$$

#### 2. Amphoteric Nature of Oxides and Hydroxides
Both metals dissolve in strong alkali solutions with the evolution of dihydrogen:
- **Beryllium:**
  

$$
\text{Be}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Na}_2[\text{Be}(\text{OH})_4](aq) + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

  

$$
\text{BeO}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2[\text{Be}(\text{OH})_4](aq) \quad (\text{Sodium Beryllate})
$$

  

$$
\text{Be(OH)}_2(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{BeCl}_2(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)
$$

- **Aluminum:**
  

$$
2\text{Al}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{Na}[\text{Al}(\text{OH})_4](aq) + 3\text{H}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

  

$$
\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{Na}[\text{Al}(\text{OH})_4](aq) \quad (\text{Sodium Tetrahydroxoaluminate})
$$

#### 3. Chloro-Bridged Dimeric and Polymeric Structures
Due to electron deficiency and high covalent character:
- **$\text{BeCl}_2$:** In the solid state, it exists as an infinite chain polymer with coordinate covalent bridging:

$$
\cdots-\text{Be}\begin{pmatrix}\mu\text{-Cl}\\\mu\text{-Cl}\end{pmatrix}\text{Be}\begin{pmatrix}\mu\text{-Cl}\\\mu\text{-Cl}\end{pmatrix}\text{Be}-\cdots
$$

In the vapor phase at temperatures up to $\approx 1200\ \text{K}$, it exists as a chloro-bridged dimer, $\text{Be}_2\text{Cl}_4$:

```
        Cl             Cl
          \    ↗      /
           Be        Be
          /      ↖    \
        Cl             Cl
```
*(where coordinate dative bonds $\text{Cl}\rightarrow\text{Be}$ satisfy beryllium's electron octet).* At higher temperatures ($>1200\ \text{K}$), it dissociates into a monomeric, linear molecule:

$$
\text{Be}_2\text{Cl}_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{BeCl}_2(g) \quad (\text{Linear}, sp\text{-hybridized})
$$

- **$\text{AlCl}_3$:** In non-polar solvents (e.g., benzene) and in the vapor phase below $400\ ^\circ\text{C}$, it dimerizes to $\text{Al}_2\text{Cl}_6$:

$$
2\text{AlCl}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{Al}_2\text{Cl}_6
$$

```
        Cl             Cl             Cl
          \           /  \           /
           Al        Cl   Al        Cl
             \      /       \      /
              Cl---Al        Cl---Al  ... [Polymer in solid BeCl₂ / Dimer Al₂Cl₆]
```
Both anhydrous $\text{BeCl}_2$ and $\text{AlCl}_3$ act as strong Lewis acids and are widely utilized as catalysts in Friedel-Crafts alkylation and acylation reactions.

#### 4. Carbide Hydrolysis Mechanism (Formation of Methane) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Both beryllium carbide and aluminum carbide contain the methanide ion ($\text{C}^{4-}$), yielding pure methane upon hydrolysis:

$$
\text{Be}_2\text{C}(s) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{Be}(\text{OH})_2(s) + \text{CH}_4(g)\uparrow
$$

$$
\text{Al}_4\text{C}_3(s) + 12\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 4\text{Al}(\text{OH})_3(s) + 3\text{CH}_4(g)\uparrow
$$

*Contrast with Calcium Carbide ($\text{CaC}_2$):* Group 2 heavier congener $\text{CaC}_2$ contains the acetylide ion ($\text{C}_2^{2-}$), yielding ethyne (acetylene):

$$
\text{CaC}_2(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{Ca}(\text{OH})_2(s) + \text{C}_2\text{H}_2(g)\uparrow
$$

### 2.3 Thermodynamic Derivation: Why Second-Period Fluorides & Oxides Exhibit High Lattice Stability
The anomalous thermal stability of second-period compounds with small anions ($\text{O}^{2-}$, $\text{N}^{3-}$, $\text{F}^-$) is quantitatively established using the **Kapustinskii Equation**:

$$
U_{\text{lattice}} \approx \frac{K \cdot \nu \cdot |z_+| \cdot |z_-|}{r_+ + r_-} \left( 1 - \frac{d}{r_+ + r_-} \right)
$$

*Where:*
- $K = 1.202 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
- $\nu$ is the total number of ions per formula unit
- $z_+, z_-$ are the integral charges of the cation and anion
- $r_+, r_-$ are the ionic radii in meters ($\text{m}$)
- $d = 3.45 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}$ ($0.345\ \text{Å}$)

#### Step-by-Step Stability Cleavage Analysis:
Consider the thermal decomposition:

$$
\text{M}_2\text{CO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{M}_2\text{O}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)
$$

The standard enthalpy of decomposition is:

$$
\Delta_r H^\circ \approx \Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ(\text{M}_2\text{CO}_3) - \Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ(\text{M}_2\text{O}) + \text{constant}
$$

1. In $\text{M}_2\text{O}$, the oxide ion $\text{O}^{2-}$ has a very small ionic radius ($r_{\text{O}^{2-}} \approx 140\ \text{pm}$).
2. For a small cation ($\text{Li}^+$, $r \approx 76\ \text{pm}$):
   

$$
\frac{1}{r_{\text{Li}^+} + r_{\text{O}^{2-}}} = \frac{1}{76 + 140} = \frac{1}{216\ \text{pm}} \quad (\text{Very large } U_{\text{lattice}})
$$

3. In $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$, the carbonate ion $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ has a large radius ($r \approx 185\ \text{pm}$).
4. The difference $\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ(\text{M}_2\text{O}) - \Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ(\text{M}_2\text{CO}_3)$ is overwhelmingly positive for $\text{Li}^+$, making the oxide thermodynamically preferred and driving decomposition at lower temperatures:

$$
\boxed{T_{\text{decomp}}(\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3) \ll T_{\text{decomp}}(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3) < T_{\text{decomp}}(\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3)}
$$

### Example 1: Covalency and Valence Shell Expansion ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**(NCERT In-Text Adapted Archetype)**

**Problem:**  
Explain using molecular orbital and valence shell considerations why boron forms $[\text{BF}_4]^-$ but cannot form $[\text{BF}_6]^{3-}$, whereas aluminum forms $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$.

**Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Ground State Configuration:**
   - $\text{B}\ (Z = 5): 1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^1$. Valence principal quantum number $n = 2$.
   - $\text{Al}\ (Z = 13): 1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^6\ 3s^2\ 3p^1$. Valence principal quantum number $n = 3$.
2. **Orbital Availability:**
   - For $n=2$, the permissible azimuthal quantum numbers are $l = 0\ (2s)$ and $l = 1\ (2p)$.
   - Maximum number of valence orbitals available for bonding in Boron:
     

$$
N_{\text{orbitals}} = 1 (2s) + 3 (2p) = 4
$$

   - Boron lacks $2d$ orbitals because $l$ cannot equal 2 when $n = 2$ ($l \le n - 1$).
   - Therefore, Boron can form a maximum of 4 covalent bonds (one $2s$ and three $2p$ orbitals yielding $sp^3$ hybridization). Its **maximum covalency is strictly 4**.
3. **Expansion in Aluminum:**
   - For Aluminum, $n = 3$. The permissible subshells are $3s, 3p,$ and $3d$.
   - Aluminum has vacant, energetically accessible $3d$ orbitals. It can expand its coordination number by utilizing $sp^3d^2$ hybrid orbitals.
4. **Final Conclusion:**
   - Boron terminates at coordination number 4 ($[\text{BF}_4]^-$).
   - Aluminum expands its coordination number to 6, forming the octahedral complex anion $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$.

### Example 2: Hydrolysis Behavior of Second Period Chlorides vs. Third Period Chlorides ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**(NCERT Exemplar Variant)**

**Problem:**  
Carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) does not undergo hydrolysis in water even on boiling, whereas silicon tetrachloride ($\text{SiCl}_4$) hydrolyzes vigorously and violently to give silicic acid. Explain the complete chemical pathway and mechanism.

**Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:**

#### Step 1: Nucleophilic Attack Requirement
Hydrolysis of a covalent non-metal halide proceeds via nucleophilic attack by the lone pair of electrons from a water molecule ($\text{H}_2\ddot{\text{O}}$) on the central atom:

$$
\text{H}_2\text{O}: + \ \text{MCl}_4 \longrightarrow [\text{Cl}_4\text{M} \leftarrow \text{OH}_2]
$$

#### Step 2: Carbon in $\text{CCl}_4$
- Carbon has the valence configuration $2s^2\ 2p^2$.
- It has no vacant $d$-orbitals to accept the lone pair of electrons from oxygen to form a five-coordinate transition state intermediate.
- Furthermore, four bulky chlorine atoms sterically shield the tiny carbon atom, preventing back-side attack.
- Consequently, $\text{CCl}_4$ is thermodynamically and kinetically inert toward nucleophilic hydrolysis.

#### Step 3: Silicon in $\text{SiCl}_4$
- Silicon has the valence configuration $3s^2\ 3p^2\ 3d^0$.
- It readily accommodates an incoming pair of electrons into an empty $3d$-orbital, expanding its coordination number from 4 to 5 in an unstable pentacoordinate intermediate:
  

$$
\text{SiCl}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow [\text{SiCl}_4(\text{OH}_2)] \xrightarrow{-\text{HCl}} \text{SiCl}_3(\text{OH})
$$

- This nucleophilic substitution ($S_N2$-type addition-elimination) repeats sequentially four times until all four chlorine atoms are replaced:
  

$$
\begin{aligned}
  \text{SiCl}_3(\text{OH}) + \text{H}_2\text{O} &\xrightarrow{-\text{HCl}} \text{SiCl}_2(\text{OH})_2 \\
  \text{SiCl}_2(\text{OH})_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} &\xrightarrow{-\text{HCl}} \text{SiCl}(\text{OH})_3 \\
  \text{SiCl}(\text{OH})_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} &\xrightarrow{-\text{HCl}} \text{Si(OH)}_4 \quad (\text{Silicic Acid / } \text{H}_4\text{SiO}_4)
  \end{aligned}
$$

#### Step 4: Overall Net Stoichiometry

$$
\boxed{\text{SiCl}_4(l) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{H}_4\text{SiO}_4(s) + 4\text{HCl}(aq)}
$$

### Example 3: Amphoteric Reactions Calculation and Balanced Equations ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**(CBSE Board Model Exam)**

**Problem:**  
Write separate, fully balanced net ionic equations to demonstrate the amphoteric behavior of:
1. Beryllium hydroxide $[\text{Be(OH)}_2]$
2. Aluminum oxide $[\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3]$

**Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:**

#### 1. Beryllium Hydroxide $[\text{Be(OH)}_2]$
- **Acting as a Base (Reaction with an acid, $\text{H}^+$):**
  

$$
\text{Be(OH)}_2(s) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Be}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)
$$

  

$$
\text{In hydrated complex form:} \quad \text{Be(OH)}_2(s) + 2\text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq) \longrightarrow [\text{Be}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_4]^{2+}(aq)
$$

- **Acting as an Acid (Reaction with a strong base, $\text{OH}^-$):**
  

$$
\text{Be(OH)}_2(s) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq) \longrightarrow [\text{Be}(\text{OH})_4]^{2-}(aq) \quad (\text{Beryllate ion})
$$

#### 2. Aluminum Oxide $[\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3]$
- **Acting as a Base (Reaction with an acid, $\text{H}^+$):**
  

$$
\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 6\text{H}^+(aq) + 9\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2[\text{Al}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6]^{3+}(aq)
$$

- **Acting as an Acid (Reaction with a strong base, $\text{OH}^-$):**
  

$$
\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2[\text{Al}(\text{OH})_4]^-(aq) \quad (\text{Aluminate ion})
$$

# SECTION 4: EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

***

### Trap 1: Confusing "Valency" with "Covalency"
- **The Student Error:** Stating that nitrogen has a valency of 5 in $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$.
- **Correction:**  
  The oxidation state of nitrogen in $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ is $+5$, but its **covalency** is strictly **4**.
  

$$
\text{Covalency} = \text{Total number of electron pairs shared (bonds formed)}
$$

  Since nitrogen has only four valence orbitals ($2s, 2p_x, 2p_y, 2p_z$), it can form a maximum of 4 covalent/coordinate bonds. **It can never have a covalency of 5.**

***

### Trap 2: Thermal Decomposition Products of Group 1 Nitrates
- **The Student Error:** Assuming $\text{LiNO}_3$ decomposes like $\text{NaNO}_3$ to give $\text{LiNO}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$.
- **The Reality:**  
  

$$
\text{NaNO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{NaNO}_2 + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \quad (\text{No brown fumes of } \text{NO}_2)
$$

  

$$
\text{LiNO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Li}_2\text{O} + 2\text{NO}_2\uparrow + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2\uparrow \quad (\text{Pungent brown fumes of } \text{NO}_2\text{ evolve})
$$

  Examiners frequently test this distinction in identification and stoichiometry questions.

***

### Trap 3: Hydrolysis Products of Carbides ($\text{Be}_2\text{C}$ vs. $\text{CaC}_2$)
- **The Student Error:** Writing that all alkaline earth metal carbides yield acetylene ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$) on hydrolysis.
- **The Reality:**  
  - $\text{Be}_2\text{C}$ has the methanide ion ($\text{C}^{4-}$), releasing **$\text{CH}_4$** (methane).
  - $\text{Mg}_2\text{C}_3$ has the allylenide ion ($\text{C}_3^{4-}$), releasing **$\text{CH}_3-\text{C}\equiv\text{CH}$** (propyne).
  - $\text{CaC}_2$ has the acetylide ion ($\text{C}_2^{2-}$), releasing **$\text{CH}\equiv\text{CH}$** (ethyne).

***

### Trap 4: Sign and Magnitude Confusion in Reactivity Metrics
- **The Student Error:** Writing "Chlorine has the highest electron gain enthalpy, so it has the lowest reactivity."
- **The Reality:**  
  Electron gain enthalpy is thermodynamically **negative** ($\Delta_{eg} H < 0$).
  A "large negative value" indicates a high driving force to capture an electron. Non-metallic chemical reactivity scales directly with how readily the element captures an electron to form an anion.

**Context:**  
In aerospace propulsion systems and solid-state battery manufacturing, alkali and alkaline earth metals serve critical functions due to their low reduction potentials and high reactivity. An advanced materials laboratory investigates five hypothetical or unlabelled main-group elements ($A, B, C, D,$ and $E$) belonging to the third period of the modern periodic table ($Z = 11$ to $17$). The experimental thermodynamic data measured under standard conditions are tabulated below:

| Element | Successive Ionization Enthalpies ($\Delta_i H$ in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) | | | Electronegativity (Pauling Scale) | Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$ in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| | $\Delta_i H_1$ | $\Delta_i H_2$ | $\Delta_i H_3$ | | |
| **A** | $496$ | $4562$ | $6910$ | $0.9$ | $-53$ |
| **B** | $738$ | $1451$ | $7733$ | $1.2$ | $-40$ |
| **C** | $578$ | $1817$ | $2745$ | $1.5$ | $-44$ |
| **D** | $1012$ | $1907$ | $2914$ | $2.1$ | $-200$ |
| **E** | $1251$ | $2298$ | $3822$ | $3.0$ | $-349$ |

**Sub-questions:**
1. Identify the elements $A$ and $B$. Justify the dramatic jump observed between $\Delta_i H_1$ and $\Delta_i H_2$ for element $A$, and between $\Delta_i H_2$ and $\Delta_i H_3$ for element $B$. *(1 Mark)*
2. Element $C$ exhibits a lower first ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1 = 578\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) than Element $B$ ($\Delta_i H_1 = 738\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$), despite having a higher nuclear charge ($Z$). Explain this thermodynamic anomaly based on subshell penetration and shielding. *(1 Mark)*
3. Write the balanced chemical formula and predict the acid-base character of the binary oxide formed by Element $A$ versus that formed by Element $E$. *(1 Mark)*
4. Calculate the enthalpy change ($\Delta H^\circ$) in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ for the vapor-phase redox disproportionation process: *(1 Mark)*
   

$$
2E_{(g)} \longrightarrow E^+_{(g)} + E^-_{(g)}
$$

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Identification and Enthalpy Jump Justification:**
   - Element $A$ has valence configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^1$ (Sodium, $\text{Na}$). The first electron is removed from the $3s$ subshell. The removal of the second electron requires breaking into the stable, noble-gas core octet ($2s^2 2p^6$), where effective nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}}$ is significantly elevated and principal quantum number decreases from $n=3$ to $n=2$, causing an enormous jump ($\approx 9\times$). **[½ Mark]**
   - Element $B$ has valence configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2$ (Magnesium, $\text{Mg}$). Its first two electrons are removed from the $3s$ orbital. The third ionization requires removing an electron from the completely filled noble-gas core ($2p^6$), leading to the observed jump between $\Delta_i H_2$ and $\Delta_i H_3$. **[½ Mark]**

2. **Penetration and Shielding Anomaly:**
   - Element $B$ ($\text{Mg}$): Valence configuration is $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2$. The electron is removed from a fully filled, spherically symmetric $3s$ orbital possessing high penetration power. **[½ Mark]**
   - Element $C$ ($\text{Al}$): Valence configuration is $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p^1$. The valence electron resides in the $3p$ orbital, which is less penetrating and effectively shielded from the nuclear charge by the inner electrons as well as the paired $3s^2$ electrons. Hence, the $3p$ electron is held less tightly and requires less energy to remove ($\Delta_i H_1(\text{Al}) < \Delta_i H_1(\text{Mg})$). **[½ Mark]**

3. **Oxides and Acid-Base Nature:**
   - Element $A$ forms $A_2\text{O}$ ($\text{Na}_2\text{O}$), which hydrolyzes to give a strongly basic solution:
     

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{O}_{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)} \longrightarrow 2\text{NaOH}_{(aq)}
$$

     Nature: **Strongly Basic**. **[½ Mark]**
   - Element $E$ forms $E_2\text{O}_7$ ($\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$) or other higher oxides, which hydrolyze to give strong oxyacids:
     

$$
\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_{7(l)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)} \longrightarrow 2\text{HClO}_{4(aq)}
$$

     Nature: **Strongly Acidic**. **[½ Mark]**

4. **Enthalpy Calculation:**
   - The overall gas-phase disproportionation reaction can be split into two thermochemical steps:
     1. Ionization of one neutral atom:
        

$$
E_{(g)} \longrightarrow E^+_{(g)} + e^- \quad \Delta H_1 = \Delta_i H_1(E) = +1251\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

     2. Electron capture by second neutral atom:
        

$$
E_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow E^-_{(g)} \quad \Delta H_2 = \Delta_{eg}H(E) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

     **[½ Mark for thermochemical cycle setup]**
   - Net reaction enthalpy:
     

$$
\Delta H^\circ = \Delta_i H_1(E) + \Delta_{eg}H(E) = 1251\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} + (-349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) = +902\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

     **[½ Mark for correct sign, value, and unit]**

#### Question 3
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

- **Assertion (A):** The first ionization enthalpy of Nitrogen ($\text{N}$) is greater than that of Oxygen ($\text{O}$), but the second ionization enthalpy of Oxygen is significantly higher than that of Nitrogen.
- **Reason (R):** Half-filled and completely filled subshells possess extra stability due to high exchange energy and symmetrical charge distribution.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(a)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - $\Delta_i H_1$: $\text{N} = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^3$ (half-filled $2p$, exchange pairs $= 3$) versus $\text{O} = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$ (removal of an electron from $\text{O}$ relieves electron-electron repulsion and yields a stable half-filled $2p^3$ state). Thus, $\Delta_i H_1(\text{N}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{O})$.
  - $\Delta_i H_2$: $\text{O}^+ = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^3$ (half-filled configuration, high exchange energy) versus $\text{N}^+ = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$. Removing an electron from the stable $2p^3$ configuration of $\text{O}^+$ requires far greater energy.
  - Reason (R) correctly explains both statements through exchange energy and symmetry.

***

#### Question 4
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

- **Assertion (A):** The electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) of Chlorine is more negative than that of Fluorine.
- **Reason (R):** The incoming electron experiences greater interelectronic repulsions in the compact $2p$ subshell of Fluorine than in the larger $3p$ subshell of Chlorine.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(a)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - Standard thermodynamic values: $\Delta_{eg}H(\text{Cl}) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ and $\Delta_{eg}H(\text{F}) = -328\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
  - Fluorine has an exceptionally small atomic radius ($r \approx 72\text{ pm}$). When an additional electron enters the compact $2p$ orbital, it experiences intense electrostatic repulsion from the seven valence electrons residing in a very small volume.
  - In Chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger $3p$ orbital ($n=3$), where electron density is lower and interelectronic repulsion is minimized. Thus, (R) is the exact causal explanation of (A).

***

#### Question 5
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

- **Assertion (A):** The ionic radius of $\text{O}^{2-}$ is larger than that of $\text{F}^-$, even though both species are isoelectronic.
- **Reason (R):** For isoelectronic anions, ionic radius increases as the magnitude of nuclear charge ($Z$) increases.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(c)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - Both $\text{O}^{2-}$ and $\text{F}^-$ possess 10 electrons ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$).
  - Nuclear charge: $\text{O}^{2-}$ has $Z = 8$ protons pulling 10 electrons ($Z/e = 0.8$), while $\text{F}^-$ has $Z = 9$ protons pulling 10 electrons ($Z/e = 0.9$).
  - The lower nuclear charge in $\text{O}^{2-}$ exerts a weaker inward pull, causing a larger electron cloud radius. Thus, Assertion (A) is **true**.
  - Reason (R) claims that ionic radius increases as $Z$ increases. This is **false**; as nuclear charge increases in an isoelectronic series, effective nuclear attraction increases and the ionic radius *decreases*.

***

#### Question 6
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐ [Core]

- **Assertion (A):** Boron forms $[\text{BF}_4]^-$ but cannot form $[\text{BF}_6]^{3-}$, whereas Aluminium readily forms $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$.
- **Reason (R):** Boron has a much higher first ionization enthalpy than Aluminium.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(b)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - Boron belongs to Period 2 and has the valence shell configuration $2s^2 2p^1$. The second shell contains only four orbitals (one $2s$ and three $2p$), lacking energetically accessible $d$-orbitals. Its maximum covalency is restricted to 4. Aluminium belongs to Period 3 and possesses vacant $3d$ orbitals, permitting expansion of its octet and a maximum covalency of 6. Thus, Assertion (A) is **true**.
  - Boron has a smaller radius and higher ionization enthalpy than Aluminium; thus, Reason (R) is a **true statement** on its own.
  - However, the inability of Boron to expand its coordination number is governed by the absence of $d$-orbitals (covalency ceiling), NOT by its ionization enthalpy. Thus, (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).

### Higher Order Thinking Skills (HOTS) & Multi-Concept Application Problems

#### Question 7
**[CBSE Competency HOTS, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

The successive ionization enthalpies ($\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) of an element $M$ are given below:

$$
\Delta_i H_1 = 801, \quad \Delta_i H_2 = 2427, \quad \Delta_i H_3 = 3660, \quad \Delta_i H_4 = 25026, \quad \Delta_i H_5 = 32827
$$

1. Determine the number of valence electrons present in the neutral atom $M$ and identify its group number in the Modern Periodic Table. *(1 Mark)*
2. Predict the empirical formula of the chloride and sulfate formed by element $M$. *(1 Mark)*
3. Does the aqueous solution of the chloride of element $M$ conduct electricity? Justify your answer based on polarization rules (Fajans' Rules). *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Valence Electrons and Group Determination:**
   - Examining the differences:
     - $\Delta_i H_2 - \Delta_i H_1 = 2427 - 801 = 1626\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
     - $\Delta_i H_3 - \Delta_i H_2 = 3660 - 2427 = 1233\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
     - $\Delta_i H_4 - \Delta_i H_3 = 25026 - 3660 = 21366\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
   - An enormous jump occurs after the loss of 3 electrons (between the 3rd and 4th ionization). This demonstrates that the 4th electron must be removed from an inner, stable noble gas core configuration.
   - Number of valence electrons = **3**. Group number = $10 + 3 =$ **Group 13**. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Formulas of Compounds:**
   - Valence oxidation state $= +3$.
   - Chloride formula:
     

$$
M^{3+} + 3\text{Cl}^- \longrightarrow M\text{Cl}_3
$$

   - Sulfate formula:
     

$$
2M^{3+} + 3\text{SO}_4^{2-} \longrightarrow M_2(\text{SO}_4)_3
$$

     **[1 Mark: ½ Mark for each formula]**

3. **Electrical Conductivity and Fajans' Rule Justification:**
   - According to Fajans' rules, a cation with a high positive charge ($+3$) and relatively small ionic radius possesses an exceptionally high charge density ($\phi = \text{charge}/\text{radius}$) and polarizes the large, polarizable electron cloud of the chloride ion ($\text{Cl}^-$).
   - This results in significant covalent character in anhydrous $M\text{Cl}_3$ (e.g., $\text{AlCl}_3$ or $\text{BCl}_3$).
   - However, when dissolved in **aqueous solution**, the high hydration enthalpy of $M^{3+}$ releases enough energy to overcome the lattice/covalent bond energy, generating hydrated ions:
     

$$
M\text{Cl}_{3(s)} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)} \longrightarrow [M(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6]^{3+}_{(aq)} + 3\text{Cl}^-_{(aq)}
$$

   - Consequently, the aqueous solution contains mobile ions and **conducts electricity**. **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 8
**[CBSE Competency HOTS, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

An element $Z$ belongs to the fourth period and group 16 of the periodic table.
1. Write down its complete electron configuration, identify the element, and determine the total number of unpaired electrons in its ground state. *(1 Mark)*
2. Compare its atomic radius and second electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H_2$) with Sulfur ($Z=16$). *(1 Mark)*
3. Why is the second electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H_2$) for Group 16 elements always positive (endothermic), whereas their first electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H_1$) is negative (exothermic)? Support your answer with a thermodynamic energy term. *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Identification and Configuration:**
   - Period 4, Group 16:
     

$$
Z = 18 (\text{Ar core}) + 10 (3d) + 2 (4s) + 4 (4p) = 34 \quad (\text{Selenium, } \text{Se})
$$

   - Electronic Configuration:
     

$$
[\text{Ar}]\, 3d^{10}\, 4s^2\, 4p^4 \quad \text{or} \quad 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^4
$$

   - In the $4p^4$ subshell: Hund's rule gives $(\uparrow\downarrow)(\uparrow)(\uparrow)$.
   - Total number of unpaired electrons = **2**. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Comparison with Sulfur:**
   - **Atomic Radius:** Selenium has an additional principal energy shell ($n=4$ versus $n=3$ in $\text{S}$). Hence, $\text{Radius}(\text{Se}) > \text{Radius}(\text{S})$. **[½ Mark]**
   - **$\Delta_{eg}H_2$:** Adding a second electron forms the divalent anion ($\text{Se}^{2-}$ vs $\text{S}^{2-}$). Because the charge is distributed over a larger volume in $\text{Se}^-$, electrostatic repulsion against the incoming second electron is slightly lower, but both remain strongly positive (endothermic). **[½ Mark]**

3. **Endothermic Nature of $\Delta_{eg}H_2$:**
   - The first electron addition is exothermic:
     

$$
\text{X}_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow \text{X}^-_{(g)} \quad (\Delta_{eg}H_1 < 0)
$$

     The incoming electron experiences net attraction from the neutral nucleus.
   - The second electron addition involves adding a negative charge to an already negatively charged mono-anion:
     

$$
\text{X}^-_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow \text{X}^{2-}_{(g)} \quad (\Delta_{eg}H_2 > 0)
$$

   - Strong electrostatic repulsions between the negative ion and the incoming electron outweigh the nuclear attraction. Work must be done to force the electron into the orbital, making the process **thermodynamically endothermic** ($\Delta_{eg}H_2 > 0$). **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 9
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.14, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

What is the basic difference between the terms **electron gain enthalpy** and **electronegativity**? Tabulate three fundamental operational distinctions between these two periodic parameters.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

**Fundamental Conceptual Difference:**  
Electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) is a thermodynamic, directly measurable energetic quantity referring to the enthalpy change when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state. In contrast, electronegativity is an empirical, non-measurable relative tendency of an atom in a chemical compound to attract the shared pair of electrons toward itself. **[1 Mark]**

| Parameter | Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) | Electronegativity ($\chi$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical Nature** | Absolute thermodynamic property representing energy released or absorbed ($\Delta H$). | Relative, dimensionless qualitative property (scale-based). |
| **System State** | Measured for an isolated, gaseous atom: $\text{X}_{(g)} + e^- \to \text{X}^-_{(g)}$. | Observed for a bonded atom within a covalent chemical molecule: $\text{A}-\text{B}$. |
| **Units** | Measured in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ or $\text{eV}/\text{atom}$. | Dimensionless (Pauling, Mulliken, or Allred-Rochow scales). |
| **Constancy** | Has a fixed value for a given element under standard conditions. | Varies depending on oxidation state, hybridization, and bonding environment. |

*(Any three points: 3 × ½ Mark = 1½ Marks; Distinction clarity = ½ Mark)*

#### Question 10
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.17 & Q3.18, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

How do you explain the following periodic variations?
1. The first ionization enthalpy of Sodium ($\text{Na}$) is lower than that of Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), but its second ionization enthalpy is much higher than that of Magnesium. *(1½ Marks)*
2. The radius of an anion is always larger than its parent neutral atom, whereas the radius of a cation is always smaller than its parent neutral atom. *(1½ Marks)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Ionization Enthalpies of $\text{Na}$ versus $\text{Mg}$:**
   - **First Ionization:**
     

$$
\text{Na } (Z=11): [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^1 \implies \text{Na}^+: [\text{Ne}]
$$

     

$$
\text{Mg } (Z=12): [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^2 \implies \text{Mg}^+: [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^1
$$

     Magnesium has a higher nuclear charge ($Z = 12$) and an electron is being removed from a completely filled, stable $3s^2$ orbital with higher penetration. Hence, $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Na}) < \Delta_i H_1(\text{Mg})$. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Second Ionization:**
     For the second ionization, electrons are removed from the unipositive ions:
     

$$
\text{Na}^+: 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 \quad (\text{Stable noble gas octet, } n=2)
$$

     

$$
\text{Mg}^+: 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1 \quad (\text{Single valence electron, } n=3)
$$

     Removing an electron from $\text{Na}^+$ requires disrupting a stable closed-shell octet with a lower principal quantum number and higher effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$), whereas $\text{Mg}^+$ loses an electron from the $3s$ orbital to attain a stable octet. Hence, $\Delta_i H_2(\text{Na}) \gg \Delta_i H_2(\text{Mg})$. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Radii of Cations and Anions relative to Parent Atoms:**
   - **Cationic Radius:** When a neutral atom loses one or more electrons to form a cation, the number of protons remains constant while the number of electrons decreases. The effective nuclear charge per electron ($Z_{\text{eff}} = Z/e$) increases, pulling the remaining electron clouds closer to the nucleus. In many cases, an entire outermost shell is stripped away. Hence:
     

$$
r_{\text{cation}} < r_{\text{atom}}
$$

     **[¾ Mark]**
   - **Anionic Radius:** When an atom gains one or more electrons to form an anion, the nuclear charge remains unchanged while the number of electrons increases. The added electrons increase mutual electrostatic screening and interelectronic repulsion, forcing the electron cloud to expand outward. Hence:
     

$$
r_{\text{anion}} > r_{\text{atom}}
$$

     **[¾ Mark]**

#### Question 11
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.25, 2 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Which of the following pairs of elements would you expect to have lower first ionization enthalpy? Justify your choice in each case:
1. $\text{K}$ or $\text{Ca}$
2. $\text{S}$ or $\text{P}$

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **$\text{K}$ or $\text{Ca}$:**
   - **Choice:** Potassium ($\text{K}$) has the lower first ionization enthalpy. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Justification:** Both elements belong to Period 4. Across a period from left to right, effective nuclear charge increases ($Z=19$ for $\text{K}$, $Z=20$ for $\text{Ca}$). Potassium has a single $4s^1$ electron outside an argon core, which is shielded by inner shells and readily released to achieve noble-gas stability ($[\text{Ar}]$), whereas $\text{Ca}$ has a higher nuclear charge and a stable, fully filled $4s^2$ subshell. **[½ Mark]**

2. **$\text{S}$ or $\text{P}$:**
   - **Choice:** Sulfur ($\text{S}$) has the lower first ionization enthalpy. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Justification:** Phosphorus has the electronic configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p_x^1 3p_y^1 3p_z^1$, which has an extra-stable, exactly half-filled $3p$ subshell with maximal exchange energy. Sulfur has the configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p_x^2 3p_y^1 3p_z^1$. The presence of two paired electrons in one of the $3p$ orbitals of Sulfur creates localized electron-electron repulsion, making it energetically easier to eject one electron to reach a stable half-filled $3p^3$ configuration ($\Delta_i H_1(\text{S}) = 1000\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} < \Delta_i H_1(\text{P}) = 1012\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). **[½ Mark]**

$$
\text{K} < \text{Mg} < \text{Al} < \text{B}

#### Question 14
**[NCERT Exemplar MCQ, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Which of the following electronic configurations is associated with the highest value of second ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_2$)?
- **(A)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$
- **(B)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2$
- **(C)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$
- **(D)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^5$

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(A)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Explanation:**
  - For configuration (A) ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$, Sodium):
    - Removal of the first electron yields $\text{M}^+$ with configuration $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$ (noble gas core of Neon).
    - The second electron must be extracted from this stable, closed-shell $2p^6$ noble-gas octet with $n=2$, which has a much higher effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$). This requires an extremely high amount of energy ($\approx 4562\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).
  - For (B) ($\text{Mg}$), the second electron is removed from a $3s^1$ subshell, which is much easier.
  - For (C) ($\text{Ne}$) and (D) ($\text{F}$), the neutral atoms have high first ionization energies, but their monovalent cations do not require breaking into a lower principal energy shell in the same way as (A).

#### Question 15
**[NCERT Exemplar Analytical Problem, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Among the elements $\text{B}, \text{C}, \text{N}, \text{O},$ and $\text{F}$:
1. Which element has the highest first ionization enthalpy, and which has the lowest? *(1 Mark)*
2. Explain why the electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) of Fluorine is less negative than that of Chlorine, but Fluorine is nevertheless a much stronger oxidizing agent than Chlorine in aqueous solution. *(2 Marks)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Highest and Lowest First Ionization Enthalpy:**
   - **Highest:** Fluorine ($\text{F}$) has the highest first ionization enthalpy due to its smallest atomic size and highest effective nuclear charge across the period. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Lowest:** Boron ($\text{B}$) has the lowest first ionization enthalpy because its valence electron resides in a shielded $2p^1$ subshell, which is farther from the nucleus and less penetrating than the $2s$ subshell. **[½ Mark]**

2. **Electron Gain Enthalpy vs. Oxidizing Power (Electrochemical Cycle):**
   - **Electron Gain Enthalpy:** Fluorine has a very compact $2p$ orbital. When an extra electron is added, strong interelectronic repulsions arise within the small subshell, releasing less net energy ($\Delta_{eg}H = -328\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) compared to Chlorine ($\Delta_{eg}H = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). **[1 Mark]**
   - **Oxidizing Power ($E^\circ$ value):** Oxidizing power is measured by standard reduction potential ($E^\circ$), which depends on a three-step thermodynamic cycle (Born-Haber cycle for halogen reduction):
     

$$
\frac{1}{2}\text{X}_{2(g)} \xrightarrow{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{diss}}H^\circ} \text{X}_{(g)} \xrightarrow{\Delta_{eg}H^\circ} \text{X}^-_{(g)} \xrightarrow{\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ} \text{X}^-_{(aq)}
$$

     - Fluorine has a **very low bond dissociation enthalpy** ($\Delta_{\text{diss}}H^\circ(\text{F}_2) = 158.8\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) due to lone-pair repulsion in the small $\text{F}-\text{F}$ molecule.
     - The fluoride ion ($\text{F}^-$) is very small and possesses an **exceptionally high hydration enthalpy** ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ = -515\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).
     - The combination of low bond dissociation enthalpy and very negative hydration enthalpy outweighs the lower electron gain enthalpy, making the standard reduction potential of Fluorine ($E^\circ = +2.87\text{ V}$) significantly higher than that of Chlorine ($E^\circ = +1.36\text{ V}$). **[1 Mark]**

**[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 5 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

1. Give reasons for the following observations:
   - (i) Noble gases possess positive values of electron gain enthalpy. *(1 Mark)*
   - (ii) The ionic radius of $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ($67.5\text{ pm}$) is significantly smaller than that of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($86\text{ pm}$), even though both ions are isoelectronic. *(1 Mark)*

2. An element has atomic number $Z = 71$.
   - (i) Write its full electronic configuration. *(1 Mark)*
   - (ii) Identify its block, period, group, and IUPAC chemical name. *(1 Mark)*

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Reasoning Sub-questions:**
   - **(i) Positive Electron Gain Enthalpy of Noble Gases:**  
     Noble gases have completely filled valence subshells ($n s^2 n p^6$), which provide high structural symmetry and stability. An additional electron must enter the next higher principal quantum shell ($n+1)s$, where it is screened by inner electrons and experiences minimal nuclear attraction. Work must be done against electrostatic repulsion to introduce this electron, making $\Delta_{eg}H$ **positive (endothermic)**. **[1 Mark]**
   - **(ii) Radius of $\text{Al}^{3+}$ vs. $\text{Mg}^{2+}$:**  
     Both $\text{Al}^{3+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ are isoelectronic with Neon ($10$ electrons, $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$). However, $\text{Al}^{3+}$ has $13$ protons ($Z=13$) while $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has only $12$ protons ($Z=12$). The effective nuclear charge ratio ($Z/e$) is higher in $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ($\frac{13}{10} = 1.3$) than in $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($\frac{12}{10} = 1.2$). The greater positive nuclear charge draws the electron cloud inward more strongly, making the ionic radius of $\text{Al}^{3+}$ smaller. **[1 Mark]**
   - **(iii) Ionization Enthalpy of $\text{Ga}$ vs. $\text{Al}$ (d-block contraction):**  
     Between $\text{Al}$ ($Z=13$) and $\text{Ga}$ ($Z=31$), the ten $3d$-electrons are filled ($3d^{10}$). The diffuse shapes of $d$-orbitals provide **poor shielding** to the outer $4s$ and $4p$ electrons against the increased nuclear charge ($+10$). Consequently, the outer electrons in Gallium experience an unexpectedly high effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$). This causes transition-metal contraction (d-block contraction), holding the valence electrons more tightly and making $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Ga})$ slightly higher than that of $\text{Al}$. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Element $Z = 71$ Analysis:**
   - **(i) Electronic Configuration:**
     Noble gas kernel is Xenon ($\text{Xe}$, $Z=54$).
     

$$
[\text{Xe}]_{54}\, 4f^{14}\, 5d^1\, 6s^2
$$

     or expanded: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^6 4d^{10} 5s^2 5p^6 4f^{14} 5d^1 6s^2$. **[1 Mark]**
   - **(ii) Periodic Location and Identification:**
     - **Block:** **$d$-block** (the last differentiating electron enters the $5d$ subshell, completing the $4f$ series). *(Note: Often classified with lanthanoids as an $f$-block congener, but formally placed in the $d$-block).*
     - **Period:** **6** ($n=6$).
     - **Group:** **3** (Group III-B).
     - **Element Name:** **Lutetium ($\text{Lu}$)**. **[1 Mark: ¼ mark each for Block, Period, Group, Name]**

## 1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

### Hack 1.1: Rapid Period, Group, and Block Identification ($Z \le 118$)
Instead of writing full electronic configurations from $1s^2$, benchmark against the **Noble Gas Anchor Method**:

| Noble Gas | $Z$ | Period Completed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\text{He}$ | 2 | 1 |
| $\text{Ne}$ | 10 | 2 |
| $\text{Ar}$ | 18 | 3 |
| $\text{Kr}$ | 36 | 4 |
| $\text{Xe}$ | 54 | 5 |
| $\text{Rn}$ | 86 | 6 |
| $\text{Og}$ | 118 | 7 |

* **Period Number**: Next period after the preceding noble gas ($[\text{Preceding NG}] \implies \text{Period} = n_{\text{NG}} + 1$).
* **Block Determination**:
  * If $Z \in [57, 71]$: $Z = 57$ is $d$-block ($\text{La}$); $Z \in [58, 71]$ is $4f$ (Lanthanoids, Group 3).
  * If $Z \in [89, 103]$: $Z = 89$ is $d$-block ($\text{Ac}$); $Z \in [90, 103]$ is $5f$ (Actinoids, Group 3).
  * Otherwise, check the valence electron entry rule ($s, p, \text{or } d$).
* **Group Number Shortcuts**:
  * **$s$-block**: $\text{Group} = \text{number of valence } s\text{-electrons}$.
  * **$p$-block**: $\text{Group} = 12 + \text{number of } p\text{-electrons} = 10 + \text{total valence electrons } (ns + np)$.
  * **$d$-block**: $\text{Group} = (n-1)d \text{ electrons} + ns \text{ electrons}$.
  * **Fast $Z$-to-Group Shortcut for Period 4 ($Z = 21 \text{ to } 30$)**: 
    

$$
\text{Group} = Z - 18
$$

    *(e.g., $\text{Fe } (Z = 26) \implies 26 - 18 = 8$)*
  * **Fast $Z$-to-Group Shortcut for $Z = 104 \text{ to } 118$**:
    

$$
\text{Group} = \text{Last two digits of } Z
$$

    *(e.g., $Z = 114 \implies \text{Group } 14$; $Z = 117 \implies \text{Group } 17$)*

***

### Hack 1.2: Instant IUPAC Nomenclature of Elements with $Z > 100$
Memorize the root syllables using the index:

$$
\text{0: nil, 1: un, 2: bi, 3: tri, 4: quad, 5: pent, 6: hex, 7: sept, 8: oct, 9: enn} + \text{"ium"}
$$

* **Rule**: If `enn` meets `nil`, double 'n' stays (`ennnil`). If `bi` or `tri` meets `ium`, drop one 'i' (`bium`, not `biium`; `trium`, not `triium`).
* **Symbol Shortcut**: Take the first letter of each root in lowercase except the capitalized first letter:
  * $Z = 109$: $\text{un} + \text{nil} + \text{enn} + \text{ium} \implies Unnilennium$, Symbol: $Une$.
  * $Z = 118$: $\text{un} + \text{un} + \text{oct} + \text{ium} \implies Ununoctium$, Symbol: $Uuo$.

***

### Hack 1.3: Isoelectronic Species Radius Hack
For isoelectronic ions, number of electrons ($e^-$) is constant.

$$
\text{Ionic Radius} \propto \frac{1}{Z_{\text{eff}}} \propto \frac{1}{Z} \quad \left(\text{or } \propto \frac{\text{Negative Charge}}{\text{Positive Charge}}\right)
$$

* **Direct Ranking Template**:
  

$$
\text{Anion with highest negative charge} > \dots > \text{Neutral} > \dots > \text{Cation with highest positive charge}
$$

  *Example ($10e^-$ system)*:
  

$$
\text{C}^{4-} > \text{N}^{3-} > \text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Ne} > \text{Na}^+ > \text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Al}^{3+} > \text{Si}^{4+}
$$

  *Time saved*: 0 calculations needed. Count atomic number $Z$; largest $Z$ gives smallest radius.

***

### Hack 1.4: Successive Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$) Gap Decoders
When given successive ionization energies in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$:
1. Locate the **abnormal jump** (typically a 4- to 10-fold increase):
   

$$
\Delta_i H_{n} \ll \Delta_i H_{n+1}
$$

2. The number of valence electrons is **$n$**.
3. Group number in $s/p$ block:
   * If $n = 1 \implies \text{Group 1}$ (Alkali metal)
   * If $n = 2 \implies \text{Group 2}$ (Alkaline earth metal)
   * If $n = 3 \implies \text{Group 13}$
   * If $n = 4 \implies \text{Group 14}$
* *Stable cation formulation*: The jump after $\Delta_i H_n$ confirms that the stable oxidation state is $+n$ (e.g., jump between $\Delta_i H_2$ and $\Delta_i H_3 \implies \text{Forms } \text{M}^{2+}$ ion, stable halide is $\text{MCl}_2$).

***

## 2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

### Trap 2.1: The Missing "Isolated Gaseous Atom" Condition
* **The Pitfall**: Defining Ionization Enthalpy or Electron Gain Enthalpy without the terms **"isolated"** and **"gaseous"** state.
* **Examiner Deduction**: **$-1$ mark** out of 1.
* **Model Answer**:
  > *"Ionization enthalpy is the minimum energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an **isolated gaseous atom** ($X_{(g)}$) in its ground state to form a gaseous cation ($X^+_{(g)}$)."*
  * Always accompany definitions with state symbols:
    

$$
X_{(g)} \longrightarrow X^+_{(g)} + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H)
$$

    

$$
X_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow X^-_{(g)} \quad (\Delta_{eg} H)
$$

***

### Trap 2.2: Sign Conventions of Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg} H$) vs. Electron Affinity ($EA$)
* **The Pitfall**: Writing "Electron Gain Enthalpy of Chlorine is positive" or confusing $\Delta_{eg} H$ with $EA$.
  

$$
\Delta_{eg} H = - EA - \frac{5}{2}RT \approx -EA
$$

* **Deduction Points**:
  1. $\Delta_{eg} H_1$ for halogens is **highly negative** (exothermic process). Saying "Fluorine has lower electron gain enthalpy than Chlorine" is ambiguous.
  2. **Board Rule**: You must state: *"Chlorine has a **more negative** electron gain enthalpy than fluorine"* (or *"Chlorine releases more energy upon electron addition"*). Writing "Chlorine has higher $\Delta_{eg} H$" without the word "negative" can cost $\frac{1}{2}$ to 1 mark.
  3. **Second Electron Gain Enthalpy is ALWAYS positive (endothermic)**:
     

$$
\text{O}^-_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow \text{O}^{2-}_{(g)} \quad (\Delta_{eg} H_2 > 0)
$$

     *Reason required for full marks*: Electrostatic repulsion between the incoming electron and the negatively charged monovalent anion ($\text{O}^-$) overcomes any attractive force.

***

### Trap 2.3: Noble Gas Radii Comparison Trap
* **The Pitfall**: Writing that Neon has a smaller atomic radius than Fluorine because atomic radius decreases across a period.
* **CBSE Reality**:
  * In Period 2: Radius of $\text{Ne} > \text{F}$.
  * *Reason*: For noble gases, only **van der Waals radius** can be determined (non-bonded atoms), whereas for halogens, the **covalent radius** is measured. Van der Waals radii are systematically larger than covalent radii because non-bonded intermolecular distances are larger.
* **Evaluation Key**: Failing to mention **"covalent radius vs. van der Waals radius"** results in a loss of **1 mark**.

***

### Trap 2.4: Period 2 vs. Period 3 Anomaly Reasoning Pitfall ($\text{N vs. P}$, $\text{O vs. S}$, $\text{F vs. Cl}$)
When answering why $\Delta_{eg} H$ of $\text{F}$ is less negative than $\text{Cl}$, or why $\text{O}$ is less negative than $\text{S}$:
* **Incomplete Answer (0.5/1 mark)**: "Because Fluorine has a small size."
* **Full-Credit Keyword Checklist (1/1 mark)**:
  1. Small, compact size of the **$2p$ subshell** in Fluorine.
  2. **High interelectronic repulsions** (electron-electron repulsions) within the compact $2p$ orbital.
  3. Incoming electron experiences resistance; in Chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger, more diffuse **$3p$ subshell**.

***

### Trap 2.5: The Isoelectronic "Effective Nuclear Charge" Justification
* When asked: *"Why is $\text{Al}^{3+}$ smaller than $\text{Mg}^{2+}$?"*
* **Wrong answer**: "Because $\text{Al}$ loses 3 electrons and $\text{Mg}$ loses 2." (Gives 0 marks).
* **Marking Scheme Requirement**:
  1. State that both are **isoelectronic** (have $10$ electrons).
  2. Quote the proton-to-electron ratio ($Z/e^-$) or nuclear charge:
     

$$
\text{Al}^{3+} \implies Z = 13, e^- = 10 \quad \left(\frac{Z}{e^-} = 1.3\right)
$$

     

$$
\text{Mg}^{2+} \implies Z = 12, e^- = 10 \quad \left(\frac{Z}{e^-} = 1.2\right)
$$

  3. **Keyword**: Higher effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) in $\text{Al}^{3+}$ pulls the remaining electrons closer to the nucleus.

## 3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT High-Yield Realities)

### 3.1 First Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1$) Inversions (Period 2 & Period 3)
The general trend across a period is increasing $\Delta_i H_1$, but there are two distinct inversions per period:

```
Period 2:  Li  <  B  <  Be  <  C  <  O  <  N  <  F  <  Ne
           (2s1) (2p1) (2s2) (2p2) (2p4) (2p3) (2p5) (2p6)
```
* **$\text{Be} > \text{B}$**: Be has a fully filled $2s^2$ subshell which has higher penetration power and stability; in B, the electron must be removed from the higher-energy, less-penetrating $2p^1$ subshell (shielded by $2s^2$).
* **$\text{N} > \text{O}$**: N has an exactly half-filled $2p^3$ configuration ($2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$) exhibiting extra exchange energy stability; O ($2p^4$) has paired electrons in one $2p$ orbital, causing interelectronic repulsion that facilitates electron removal.

```
Period 3:  Na  <  Al  <  Mg  <  Si  <  S  <  P  <  Cl  <  Ar
```
* $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Mg}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{Al})$ ($3s^2$ vs $3p^1$)
* $\Delta_i H_1(\text{P}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{S})$ ($3p^3$ half-filled vs $3p^4$)

***

### 3.2 Second Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_2$) Classic Traps
* **$\Delta_i H_2$ of Alkali Metals ($\text{Group 1}$) is anomalously massive**:
  Removing a second electron from $\text{Na}^+$ requires disrupting a stable noble-gas core ($[\text{Ne}] 2s^2 2p^6 \to [\text{Ne}] 2s^2 2p^5$). Thus:
  

$$
\Delta_i H_2(\text{Na}) \gg \Delta_i H_2(\text{Mg})
$$

* **$\Delta_i H_2$ of $\text{O}$ vs $\text{N}$**:
  * $\text{O}^+ \implies 2s^2 2p^3$ (stable half-filled configuration).
  * $\text{N}^+ \implies 2s^2 2p^2$.
  * **Result**: $\Delta_i H_2(\text{O}) > \Delta_i H_2(\text{N})$ (opposite of $\Delta_i H_1$).

***

### 3.3 Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg} H$) Exact Sequence
* **Group 17 (Halogens)**:
  

$$
\text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I} \quad (\text{magnitude of negative } \Delta_{eg} H)
$$

  

$$
\Delta_{eg} H \text{ values (kJ/mol)}: \text{Cl } (-349) < \text{F } (-328) < \text{Br } (-325) < \text{I } (-295)
$$

* **Group 16 (Chalcogens) — NCERT Critical Anomaly**:
  Oxygen has the **least negative** $\Delta_{eg} H$ in its entire group due to extreme compactness:
  

$$
\text{S} > \text{Se} > \text{Te} > \text{Po} > O \quad (\text{magnitude of negative } \Delta_{eg} H)
$$

  

$$
\text{Most negative: } \text{S } (-200\text{ kJ/mol}) \quad \longrightarrow \quad \text{Least negative: } O  (-141\text{ kJ/mol})
$$

* **Positive $\Delta_{eg} H$ Elements (NCERT)**:
  * Noble gases: Large positive values (electron must enter next principal quantum shell).
  * Alkaline earth metals ($\text{Be}, \text{Mg}, \text{Ca}$): Nearly zero or slightly positive due to stable $ns^2$ subshells.
  * Nitrogen ($\text{N}$): Slightly positive/nearly zero due to extra stability of half-filled $2p^3$ shell.

***

### 3.4 Diagonal Relationship and Anomalous Behavior of Second-Period Elements
Second-period elements ($\text{Li}, \text{Be}, \text{B}$) show marked differences from heavier congeners due to:
1. Exceptionally small atomic/ionic size.
2. High charge-to-radius ratio ($\frac{q}{r}$, polarizing power).
3. High electronegativity.
4. **Non-availability of vacant $d$-orbitals** in the valence shell:
   * Max covalency of second-period elements $= 4$ (e.g., $[\text{BF}_4]^-$, $[\text{BeF}_4]^{2-}$).
   * Third-period elements expand octets due to vacant $d$-orbitals (e.g., $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$, $\text{SF}_6$, $\text{PCl}_5$).
* **Diagonal Pairs**:
  * $\text{Li}$ and $\text{Mg}$ (Similar size: $r_{\text{Li}^+} \approx 76\text{ pm}, r_{\text{Mg}^{2+}} \approx 72\text{ pm}$).
  * $\text{Be}$ and $\text{Al}$ (Both form amphoteric oxides: $\text{BeO}$ and $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, bridging covalent chlorides).
  * $\text{B}$ and $\text{Si}$ (Both form covalent hydrides/oxides, semiconductors).

***

### 3.5 Acidic/Basic Character of Oxides
* Moving left to right across a period:
  

$$
\text{Basic} \longrightarrow \text{Amphoteric} \longrightarrow \text{Acidic}
$$

  *Example (Period 3)*:
  

$$
\underbrace{\text{Na}_2\text{O}}_{\text{Strongly basic}} \quad \underbrace{\text{MgO}}_{\text{Basic}} \quad \underbrace{\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3}_{\text{Amphoteric}} \quad \underbrace{\text{SiO}_2}_{\text{Weakly acidic}} \quad \underbrace{\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}}_{\text{Acidic}} \quad \underbrace{\text{SO}_3}_{\text{Strongly acidic}} \quad \underbrace{\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7}_{\text{Very strongly acidic}}
$$

* Key Amphoteric Oxides: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{BeO}, \text{ZnO}, \text{SnO}, \text{PbO}$.
* Neutral Oxides: $\text{CO}, \text{NO}, \text{N}_2\text{O}$.
