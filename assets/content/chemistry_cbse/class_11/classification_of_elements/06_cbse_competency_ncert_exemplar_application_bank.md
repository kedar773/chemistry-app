#### Question 1
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 4 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

**Context:**  
In aerospace propulsion systems and solid-state battery manufacturing, alkali and alkaline earth metals serve critical functions due to their low reduction potentials and high reactivity. An advanced materials laboratory investigates five hypothetical or unlabelled main-group elements ($A, B, C, D,$ and $E$) belonging to the third period of the modern periodic table ($Z = 11$ to $17$). The experimental thermodynamic data measured under standard conditions are tabulated below:

| Element | Successive Ionization Enthalpies ($\Delta_i H$ in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) | | | Electronegativity (Pauling Scale) | Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$ in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| | $\Delta_i H_1$ | $\Delta_i H_2$ | $\Delta_i H_3$ | | |
| **A** | $496$ | $4562$ | $6910$ | $0.9$ | $-53$ |
| **B** | $738$ | $1451$ | $7733$ | $1.2$ | $-40$ |
| **C** | $578$ | $1817$ | $2745$ | $1.5$ | $-44$ |
| **D** | $1012$ | $1907$ | $2914$ | $2.1$ | $-200$ |
| **E** | $1251$ | $2298$ | $3822$ | $3.0$ | $-349$ |

**Sub-questions:**
1. Identify the elements $A$ and $B$. Justify the dramatic jump observed between $\Delta_i H_1$ and $\Delta_i H_2$ for element $A$, and between $\Delta_i H_2$ and $\Delta_i H_3$ for element $B$. *(1 Mark)*
2. Element $C$ exhibits a lower first ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1 = 578\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) than Element $B$ ($\Delta_i H_1 = 738\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$), despite having a higher nuclear charge ($Z$). Explain this thermodynamic anomaly based on subshell penetration and shielding. *(1 Mark)*
3. Write the balanced chemical formula and predict the acid-base character of the binary oxide formed by Element $A$ versus that formed by Element $E$. *(1 Mark)*
4. Calculate the enthalpy change ($\Delta H^\circ$) in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ for the vapor-phase redox disproportionation process: *(1 Mark)*
   

$$
2E_{(g)} \longrightarrow E^+_{(g)} + E^-_{(g)}
$$

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Identification and Enthalpy Jump Justification:**
   - Element $A$ has valence configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^1$ (Sodium, $\text{Na}$). The first electron is removed from the $3s$ subshell. The removal of the second electron requires breaking into the stable, noble-gas core octet ($2s^2 2p^6$), where effective nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}}$ is significantly elevated and principal quantum number decreases from $n=3$ to $n=2$, causing an enormous jump ($\approx 9\times$). **[½ Mark]**
   - Element $B$ has valence configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2$ (Magnesium, $\text{Mg}$). Its first two electrons are removed from the $3s$ orbital. The third ionization requires removing an electron from the completely filled noble-gas core ($2p^6$), leading to the observed jump between $\Delta_i H_2$ and $\Delta_i H_3$. **[½ Mark]**

2. **Penetration and Shielding Anomaly:**
   - Element $B$ ($\text{Mg}$): Valence configuration is $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2$. The electron is removed from a fully filled, spherically symmetric $3s$ orbital possessing high penetration power. **[½ Mark]**
   - Element $C$ ($\text{Al}$): Valence configuration is $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p^1$. The valence electron resides in the $3p$ orbital, which is less penetrating and effectively shielded from the nuclear charge by the inner electrons as well as the paired $3s^2$ electrons. Hence, the $3p$ electron is held less tightly and requires less energy to remove ($\Delta_i H_1(\text{Al}) < \Delta_i H_1(\text{Mg})$). **[½ Mark]**

3. **Oxides and Acid-Base Nature:**
   - Element $A$ forms $A_2\text{O}$ ($\text{Na}_2\text{O}$), which hydrolyzes to give a strongly basic solution:
     

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{O}_{(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)} \longrightarrow 2\text{NaOH}_{(aq)}
$$

     Nature: **Strongly Basic**. **[½ Mark]**
   - Element $E$ forms $E_2\text{O}_7$ ($\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$) or other higher oxides, which hydrolyze to give strong oxyacids:
     

$$
\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_{7(l)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)} \longrightarrow 2\text{HClO}_{4(aq)}
$$

     Nature: **Strongly Acidic**. **[½ Mark]**

4. **Enthalpy Calculation:**
   - The overall gas-phase disproportionation reaction can be split into two thermochemical steps:
     1. Ionization of one neutral atom:
        

$$
E_{(g)} \longrightarrow E^+_{(g)} + e^- \quad \Delta H_1 = \Delta_i H_1(E) = +1251\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

     2. Electron capture by second neutral atom:
        

$$
E_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow E^-_{(g)} \quad \Delta H_2 = \Delta_{eg}H(E) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

     **[½ Mark for thermochemical cycle setup]**
   - Net reaction enthalpy:
     

$$
\Delta H^\circ = \Delta_i H_1(E) + \Delta_{eg}H(E) = 1251\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} + (-349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) = +902\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

     **[½ Mark for correct sign, value, and unit]**

#### Question 2
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 4 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

**Context:**  
Superheavy elements (SHE) are synthesized in particle accelerators via heavy-ion fusion reactions. The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) has established a systematic nomenclature based on numerical roots for elements with $Z > 100$ until their discoveries are officially validated and permanent names are assigned. An unknown transuranic element $X$ was synthesized by bombarding a californium target ($^{249}_{98}\text{Cf}$) with calcium ions ($^{48}_{20}\text{Ca}$), releasing three neutrons:

$$
^{249}_{98}\text{Cf} + ^{48}_{20}\text{Ca} \longrightarrow \, ^{294}_{Z}X + 3\,^1_0 n
$$

**Sub-questions:**
1. Determine the atomic number ($Z$) of element $X$, provide its systematic IUPAC temporary name and symbol, and state the period and IUPAC group number to which it belongs. *(1 Mark)*
2. Deduce the complete condensed ground-state electronic configuration of element $X$ using the preceding noble gas kernel. *(1 Mark)*
3. Based on relativistic electronic stabilization effects, predict the most stable oxidation state of element $X$ and compare its metallic/non-metallic character relative to its lighter group congeners. *(1 Mark)*
4. Predict the chemical formula of the oxide formed by element $X$ in its maximum group valence state and determine its acidic, basic, or amphoteric nature. *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Atomic Number and IUPAC Designation:**
   - Conserving mass and atomic numbers:
     

$$
Z = 98 + 20 - 0 = 118
$$

     The atomic number is $Z = 118$. **[½ Mark]**
   - IUPAC Systematic Name: **Ununoctium**; Symbol: **Uuo** (Officially named Oganesson, $\text{Og}$).
   - Period: **7** ($n = 7$). Group: **18** (Noble gases, since $118 - 100 = 18$). **[½ Mark]**

2. **Electronic Configuration:**
   - The noble gas preceding $Z=118$ is Radon ($\text{Rn}$, $Z=86$).
   - Filling follows the Aufbau principle: $7s^2$, $5f^{14}$, $6d^{10}$, $7p^6$.
   - Ground state configuration:
     

$$
[\text{Rn}]_{86}\, 5f^{14}\, 6d^{10}\, 7s^2\, 7p^6
$$

     **[1 Mark for correct subshell sequence and electron counts]**

3. **Oxidation State and Relativistic Effects:**
   - Due to strong relativistic spin-orbit coupling in superheavy elements, the $7p_{1/2}$ subshell is stabilized and contracted while the $7p_{3/2}$ subshell is destabilized.
   - Hence, element 118 can lose its four outer $7p_{3/2}$ electrons or undergo oxidation to stable $+4$ or $+2$ states, unlike lighter group 18 gases which are generally inert (except xenon under extreme conditions).
   - Furthermore, due to the large atomic radius and polarizability, element 118 is predicted to exhibit semiconductor/metalloid or even post-transition metallic behavior at room temperature, rather than behaving as a simple cryogenic non-metallic gas. **[1 Mark]**

4. **Formula of Oxide and Nature:**
   - In its group valence state ($+4$ or inert $+0$; theoretically group valence $+6$ or $+4$ for higher fluorides/oxides), if it forms a dioxide $X\text{O}_2$:
     

$$
\text{Formula: } X\text{O}_2 \text{ or } \text{OgO}_2
$$

     Due to the increased electropositive/metallic nature down Group 18, this oxide is predicted to be **amphoteric to weakly basic**, unlike the strongly acidic higher oxides of non-metals ($\text{XeO}_3$). **[1 Mark]**

### Assertion-Reason Questions

**Directions:** In each of the following questions, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the correct option from:
- **(a)** Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
- **(b)** Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
- **(c)** (A) is true, but (R) is false.
- **(d)** (A) is false, but (R) is true.

#### Question 3
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

- **Assertion (A):** The first ionization enthalpy of Nitrogen ($\text{N}$) is greater than that of Oxygen ($\text{O}$), but the second ionization enthalpy of Oxygen is significantly higher than that of Nitrogen.
- **Reason (R):** Half-filled and completely filled subshells possess extra stability due to high exchange energy and symmetrical charge distribution.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(a)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - $\Delta_i H_1$: $\text{N} = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^3$ (half-filled $2p$, exchange pairs $= 3$) versus $\text{O} = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$ (removal of an electron from $\text{O}$ relieves electron-electron repulsion and yields a stable half-filled $2p^3$ state). Thus, $\Delta_i H_1(\text{N}) > \Delta_i H_1(\text{O})$.
  - $\Delta_i H_2$: $\text{O}^+ = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^3$ (half-filled configuration, high exchange energy) versus $\text{N}^+ = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$. Removing an electron from the stable $2p^3$ configuration of $\text{O}^+$ requires far greater energy.
  - Reason (R) correctly explains both statements through exchange energy and symmetry.

***

#### Question 4
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

- **Assertion (A):** The electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) of Chlorine is more negative than that of Fluorine.
- **Reason (R):** The incoming electron experiences greater interelectronic repulsions in the compact $2p$ subshell of Fluorine than in the larger $3p$ subshell of Chlorine.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(a)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - Standard thermodynamic values: $\Delta_{eg}H(\text{Cl}) = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ and $\Delta_{eg}H(\text{F}) = -328\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
  - Fluorine has an exceptionally small atomic radius ($r \approx 72\text{ pm}$). When an additional electron enters the compact $2p$ orbital, it experiences intense electrostatic repulsion from the seven valence electrons residing in a very small volume.
  - In Chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger $3p$ orbital ($n=3$), where electron density is lower and interelectronic repulsion is minimized. Thus, (R) is the exact causal explanation of (A).

***

#### Question 5
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

- **Assertion (A):** The ionic radius of $\text{O}^{2-}$ is larger than that of $\text{F}^-$, even though both species are isoelectronic.
- **Reason (R):** For isoelectronic anions, ionic radius increases as the magnitude of nuclear charge ($Z$) increases.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(c)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - Both $\text{O}^{2-}$ and $\text{F}^-$ possess 10 electrons ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$).
  - Nuclear charge: $\text{O}^{2-}$ has $Z = 8$ protons pulling 10 electrons ($Z/e = 0.8$), while $\text{F}^-$ has $Z = 9$ protons pulling 10 electrons ($Z/e = 0.9$).
  - The lower nuclear charge in $\text{O}^{2-}$ exerts a weaker inward pull, causing a larger electron cloud radius. Thus, Assertion (A) is **true**.
  - Reason (R) claims that ionic radius increases as $Z$ increases. This is **false**; as nuclear charge increases in an isoelectronic series, effective nuclear attraction increases and the ionic radius *decreases*.

***

#### Question 6
**[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐ [Core]

- **Assertion (A):** Boron forms $[\text{BF}_4]^-$ but cannot form $[\text{BF}_6]^{3-}$, whereas Aluminium readily forms $[\text{AlF}_6]^{3-}$.
- **Reason (R):** Boron has a much higher first ionization enthalpy than Aluminium.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(b)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Step-by-step Evaluation:**
  - Boron belongs to Period 2 and has the valence shell configuration $2s^2 2p^1$. The second shell contains only four orbitals (one $2s$ and three $2p$), lacking energetically accessible $d$-orbitals. Its maximum covalency is restricted to 4. Aluminium belongs to Period 3 and possesses vacant $3d$ orbitals, permitting expansion of its octet and a maximum covalency of 6. Thus, Assertion (A) is **true**.
  - Boron has a smaller radius and higher ionization enthalpy than Aluminium; thus, Reason (R) is a **true statement** on its own.
  - However, the inability of Boron to expand its coordination number is governed by the absence of $d$-orbitals (covalency ceiling), NOT by its ionization enthalpy. Thus, (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).

### Higher Order Thinking Skills (HOTS) & Multi-Concept Application Problems

#### Question 7
**[CBSE Competency HOTS, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

The successive ionization enthalpies ($\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) of an element $M$ are given below:

$$
\Delta_i H_1 = 801, \quad \Delta_i H_2 = 2427, \quad \Delta_i H_3 = 3660, \quad \Delta_i H_4 = 25026, \quad \Delta_i H_5 = 32827
$$

1. Determine the number of valence electrons present in the neutral atom $M$ and identify its group number in the Modern Periodic Table. *(1 Mark)*
2. Predict the empirical formula of the chloride and sulfate formed by element $M$. *(1 Mark)*
3. Does the aqueous solution of the chloride of element $M$ conduct electricity? Justify your answer based on polarization rules (Fajans' Rules). *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Valence Electrons and Group Determination:**
   - Examining the differences:
     - $\Delta_i H_2 - \Delta_i H_1 = 2427 - 801 = 1626\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
     - $\Delta_i H_3 - \Delta_i H_2 = 3660 - 2427 = 1233\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
     - $\Delta_i H_4 - \Delta_i H_3 = 25026 - 3660 = 21366\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
   - An enormous jump occurs after the loss of 3 electrons (between the 3rd and 4th ionization). This demonstrates that the 4th electron must be removed from an inner, stable noble gas core configuration.
   - Number of valence electrons = **3**. Group number = $10 + 3 =$ **Group 13**. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Formulas of Compounds:**
   - Valence oxidation state $= +3$.
   - Chloride formula:
     

$$
M^{3+} + 3\text{Cl}^- \longrightarrow M\text{Cl}_3
$$

   - Sulfate formula:
     

$$
2M^{3+} + 3\text{SO}_4^{2-} \longrightarrow M_2(\text{SO}_4)_3
$$

     **[1 Mark: ½ Mark for each formula]**

3. **Electrical Conductivity and Fajans' Rule Justification:**
   - According to Fajans' rules, a cation with a high positive charge ($+3$) and relatively small ionic radius possesses an exceptionally high charge density ($\phi = \text{charge}/\text{radius}$) and polarizes the large, polarizable electron cloud of the chloride ion ($\text{Cl}^-$).
   - This results in significant covalent character in anhydrous $M\text{Cl}_3$ (e.g., $\text{AlCl}_3$ or $\text{BCl}_3$).
   - However, when dissolved in **aqueous solution**, the high hydration enthalpy of $M^{3+}$ releases enough energy to overcome the lattice/covalent bond energy, generating hydrated ions:
     

$$
M\text{Cl}_{3(s)} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)} \longrightarrow [M(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6]^{3+}_{(aq)} + 3\text{Cl}^-_{(aq)}
$$

   - Consequently, the aqueous solution contains mobile ions and **conducts electricity**. **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 8
**[CBSE Competency HOTS, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

An element $Z$ belongs to the fourth period and group 16 of the periodic table.
1. Write down its complete electron configuration, identify the element, and determine the total number of unpaired electrons in its ground state. *(1 Mark)*
2. Compare its atomic radius and second electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H_2$) with Sulfur ($Z=16$). *(1 Mark)*
3. Why is the second electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H_2$) for Group 16 elements always positive (endothermic), whereas their first electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H_1$) is negative (exothermic)? Support your answer with a thermodynamic energy term. *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Identification and Configuration:**
   - Period 4, Group 16:
     

$$
Z = 18 (\text{Ar core}) + 10 (3d) + 2 (4s) + 4 (4p) = 34 \quad (\text{Selenium, } \text{Se})
$$

   - Electronic Configuration:
     

$$
[\text{Ar}]\, 3d^{10}\, 4s^2\, 4p^4 \quad \text{or} \quad 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^4
$$

   - In the $4p^4$ subshell: Hund's rule gives $(\uparrow\downarrow)(\uparrow)(\uparrow)$.
   - Total number of unpaired electrons = **2**. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Comparison with Sulfur:**
   - **Atomic Radius:** Selenium has an additional principal energy shell ($n=4$ versus $n=3$ in $\text{S}$). Hence, $\text{Radius}(\text{Se}) > \text{Radius}(\text{S})$. **[½ Mark]**
   - **$\Delta_{eg}H_2$:** Adding a second electron forms the divalent anion ($\text{Se}^{2-}$ vs $\text{S}^{2-}$). Because the charge is distributed over a larger volume in $\text{Se}^-$, electrostatic repulsion against the incoming second electron is slightly lower, but both remain strongly positive (endothermic). **[½ Mark]**

3. **Endothermic Nature of $\Delta_{eg}H_2$:**
   - The first electron addition is exothermic:
     

$$
\text{X}_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow \text{X}^-_{(g)} \quad (\Delta_{eg}H_1 < 0)
$$

     The incoming electron experiences net attraction from the neutral nucleus.
   - The second electron addition involves adding a negative charge to an already negatively charged mono-anion:
     

$$
\text{X}^-_{(g)} + e^- \longrightarrow \text{X}^{2-}_{(g)} \quad (\Delta_{eg}H_2 > 0)
$$

   - Strong electrostatic repulsions between the negative ion and the incoming electron outweigh the nuclear attraction. Work must be done to force the electron into the orbital, making the process **thermodynamically endothermic** ($\Delta_{eg}H_2 > 0$). **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 9
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.14, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

What is the basic difference between the terms **electron gain enthalpy** and **electronegativity**? Tabulate three fundamental operational distinctions between these two periodic parameters.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

**Fundamental Conceptual Difference:**  
Electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) is a thermodynamic, directly measurable energetic quantity referring to the enthalpy change when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state. In contrast, electronegativity is an empirical, non-measurable relative tendency of an atom in a chemical compound to attract the shared pair of electrons toward itself. **[1 Mark]**

| Parameter | Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) | Electronegativity ($\chi$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical Nature** | Absolute thermodynamic property representing energy released or absorbed ($\Delta H$). | Relative, dimensionless qualitative property (scale-based). |
| **System State** | Measured for an isolated, gaseous atom: $\text{X}_{(g)} + e^- \to \text{X}^-_{(g)}$. | Observed for a bonded atom within a covalent chemical molecule: $\text{A}-\text{B}$. |
| **Units** | Measured in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ or $\text{eV}/\text{atom}$. | Dimensionless (Pauling, Mulliken, or Allred-Rochow scales). |
| **Constancy** | Has a fixed value for a given element under standard conditions. | Varies depending on oxidation state, hybridization, and bonding environment. |

*(Any three points: 3 × ½ Mark = 1½ Marks; Distinction clarity = ½ Mark)*

#### Question 10
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.17 & Q3.18, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

How do you explain the following periodic variations?
1. The first ionization enthalpy of Sodium ($\text{Na}$) is lower than that of Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), but its second ionization enthalpy is much higher than that of Magnesium. *(1½ Marks)*
2. The radius of an anion is always larger than its parent neutral atom, whereas the radius of a cation is always smaller than its parent neutral atom. *(1½ Marks)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Ionization Enthalpies of $\text{Na}$ versus $\text{Mg}$:**
   - **First Ionization:**
     

$$
\text{Na } (Z=11): [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^1 \implies \text{Na}^+: [\text{Ne}]
$$

     

$$
\text{Mg } (Z=12): [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^2 \implies \text{Mg}^+: [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^1
$$

     Magnesium has a higher nuclear charge ($Z = 12$) and an electron is being removed from a completely filled, stable $3s^2$ orbital with higher penetration. Hence, $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Na}) < \Delta_i H_1(\text{Mg})$. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Second Ionization:**
     For the second ionization, electrons are removed from the unipositive ions:
     

$$
\text{Na}^+: 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 \quad (\text{Stable noble gas octet, } n=2)
$$

     

$$
\text{Mg}^+: 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1 \quad (\text{Single valence electron, } n=3)
$$

     Removing an electron from $\text{Na}^+$ requires disrupting a stable closed-shell octet with a lower principal quantum number and higher effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$), whereas $\text{Mg}^+$ loses an electron from the $3s$ orbital to attain a stable octet. Hence, $\Delta_i H_2(\text{Na}) \gg \Delta_i H_2(\text{Mg})$. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Radii of Cations and Anions relative to Parent Atoms:**
   - **Cationic Radius:** When a neutral atom loses one or more electrons to form a cation, the number of protons remains constant while the number of electrons decreases. The effective nuclear charge per electron ($Z_{\text{eff}} = Z/e$) increases, pulling the remaining electron clouds closer to the nucleus. In many cases, an entire outermost shell is stripped away. Hence:
     

$$
r_{\text{cation}} < r_{\text{atom}}
$$

     **[¾ Mark]**
   - **Anionic Radius:** When an atom gains one or more electrons to form an anion, the nuclear charge remains unchanged while the number of electrons increases. The added electrons increase mutual electrostatic screening and interelectronic repulsion, forcing the electron cloud to expand outward. Hence:
     

$$
r_{\text{anion}} > r_{\text{atom}}
$$

     **[¾ Mark]**

#### Question 11
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.25, 2 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Which of the following pairs of elements would you expect to have lower first ionization enthalpy? Justify your choice in each case:
1. $\text{K}$ or $\text{Ca}$
2. $\text{S}$ or $\text{P}$

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **$\text{K}$ or $\text{Ca}$:**
   - **Choice:** Potassium ($\text{K}$) has the lower first ionization enthalpy. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Justification:** Both elements belong to Period 4. Across a period from left to right, effective nuclear charge increases ($Z=19$ for $\text{K}$, $Z=20$ for $\text{Ca}$). Potassium has a single $4s^1$ electron outside an argon core, which is shielded by inner shells and readily released to achieve noble-gas stability ($[\text{Ar}]$), whereas $\text{Ca}$ has a higher nuclear charge and a stable, fully filled $4s^2$ subshell. **[½ Mark]**

2. **$\text{S}$ or $\text{P}$:**
   - **Choice:** Sulfur ($\text{S}$) has the lower first ionization enthalpy. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Justification:** Phosphorus has the electronic configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p_x^1 3p_y^1 3p_z^1$, which has an extra-stable, exactly half-filled $3p$ subshell with maximal exchange energy. Sulfur has the configuration $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p_x^2 3p_y^1 3p_z^1$. The presence of two paired electrons in one of the $3p$ orbitals of Sulfur creates localized electron-electron repulsion, making it energetically easier to eject one electron to reach a stable half-filled $3p^3$ configuration ($\Delta_i H_1(\text{S}) = 1000\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} < \Delta_i H_1(\text{P}) = 1012\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). **[½ Mark]**

#### Question 12
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.30, 2 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Considering the elements $\text{B}, \text{Al}, \text{Mg},$ and $\text{K}$, write the correct order of their:
1. Metallic character
2. Non-metallic character

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Metallic Character:**
   - Metallic character increases down a group (due to increasing atomic size and decreasing ionization energy) and decreases across a period from left to right (due to increasing nuclear charge).
   - $\text{K}$ belongs to Group 1, Period 4 (alkali metal, lowest ionization enthalpy).
   - $\text{Mg}$ belongs to Group 2, Period 3.
   - $\text{Al}$ belongs to Group 13, Period 3.
   - $\text{B}$ belongs to Group 13, Period 2 (metalloid/non-metal behavior).
   - Correct increasing order of metallic character:
     

$$
\text{B} < \text{Al} < \text{Mg} < \text{K}
$$

     **[1 Mark]**

2. **Non-Metallic Character:**
   - Non-metallic character is the inverse of metallic character; it increases across a period and decreases down a group.
   - Correct increasing order of non-metallic character:
     

$$
\text{K} < \text{Mg} < \text{Al} < \text{B}
$$

     **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 13
**[NCERT Exercise Q3.39, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Use the periodic table to identify:
1. An element with five electrons in the outer subshell. *(1 Mark)*
2. An element that tends to lose two electrons to attain a noble-gas configuration. *(1 Mark)*
3. An element that tends to gain two electrons to attain a noble-gas configuration. *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Five electrons in the outer subshell:**
   - The outer subshell configuration corresponds to $n p^5$ (Group 17 - Halogens).
   - **Examples:** Fluorine ($\text{F}$), Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$), Bromine ($\text{Br}$), or Iodine ($\text{I}$).
   - *Electronic configuration example:* $\text{Cl}: [\text{Ne}]\, 3s^2 3p^5$. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Tends to lose two electrons:**
   - Elements with valence shell configuration $n s^2$ (Group 2 - Alkaline Earth Metals) lose two electrons to form $M^{2+}$ with a noble-gas core.
   - **Examples:** Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), Calcium ($\text{Ca}$), Strontium ($\text{Sr}$), or Barium ($\text{Ba}$).
   - *Reaction:* $\text{Mg} \longrightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2e^-$ ($[\text{Ne}]$ configuration). **[1 Mark]**

3. **Tends to gain two electrons:**
   - Elements with valence shell configuration $n s^2 n p^4$ (Group 16 - Chalcogens) gain two electrons to complete their valence shell octet ($n s^2 n p^6$).
   - **Examples:** Oxygen ($\text{O}$), Sulfur ($\text{S}$), or Selenium ($\text{Se}$).
   - *Reaction:* $\text{O} + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{O}^{2-}$ ($[\text{Ne}]$ configuration). **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 14
**[NCERT Exemplar MCQ, 1 Mark]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Which of the following electronic configurations is associated with the highest value of second ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_2$)?
- **(A)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$
- **(B)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2$
- **(C)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$
- **(D)** $1s^2 2s^2 2p^5$

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme
- **Correct Option:** **(A)** **[1 Mark]**
- **Explanation:**
  - For configuration (A) ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$, Sodium):
    - Removal of the first electron yields $\text{M}^+$ with configuration $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$ (noble gas core of Neon).
    - The second electron must be extracted from this stable, closed-shell $2p^6$ noble-gas octet with $n=2$, which has a much higher effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$). This requires an extremely high amount of energy ($\approx 4562\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).
  - For (B) ($\text{Mg}$), the second electron is removed from a $3s^1$ subshell, which is much easier.
  - For (C) ($\text{Ne}$) and (D) ($\text{F}$), the neutral atoms have high first ionization energies, but their monovalent cations do not require breaking into a lower principal energy shell in the same way as (A).

#### Question 15
**[NCERT Exemplar Analytical Problem, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Among the elements $\text{B}, \text{C}, \text{N}, \text{O},$ and $\text{F}$:
1. Which element has the highest first ionization enthalpy, and which has the lowest? *(1 Mark)*
2. Explain why the electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$) of Fluorine is less negative than that of Chlorine, but Fluorine is nevertheless a much stronger oxidizing agent than Chlorine in aqueous solution. *(2 Marks)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Highest and Lowest First Ionization Enthalpy:**
   - **Highest:** Fluorine ($\text{F}$) has the highest first ionization enthalpy due to its smallest atomic size and highest effective nuclear charge across the period. **[½ Mark]**
   - **Lowest:** Boron ($\text{B}$) has the lowest first ionization enthalpy because its valence electron resides in a shielded $2p^1$ subshell, which is farther from the nucleus and less penetrating than the $2s$ subshell. **[½ Mark]**

2. **Electron Gain Enthalpy vs. Oxidizing Power (Electrochemical Cycle):**
   - **Electron Gain Enthalpy:** Fluorine has a very compact $2p$ orbital. When an extra electron is added, strong interelectronic repulsions arise within the small subshell, releasing less net energy ($\Delta_{eg}H = -328\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) compared to Chlorine ($\Delta_{eg}H = -349\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). **[1 Mark]**
   - **Oxidizing Power ($E^\circ$ value):** Oxidizing power is measured by standard reduction potential ($E^\circ$), which depends on a three-step thermodynamic cycle (Born-Haber cycle for halogen reduction):
     

$$
\frac{1}{2}\text{X}_{2(g)} \xrightarrow{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{diss}}H^\circ} \text{X}_{(g)} \xrightarrow{\Delta_{eg}H^\circ} \text{X}^-_{(g)} \xrightarrow{\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ} \text{X}^-_{(aq)}
$$

     - Fluorine has a **very low bond dissociation enthalpy** ($\Delta_{\text{diss}}H^\circ(\text{F}_2) = 158.8\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) due to lone-pair repulsion in the small $\text{F}-\text{F}$ molecule.
     - The fluoride ion ($\text{F}^-$) is very small and possesses an **exceptionally high hydration enthalpy** ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ = -515\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).
     - The combination of low bond dissociation enthalpy and very negative hydration enthalpy outweighs the lower electron gain enthalpy, making the standard reduction potential of Fluorine ($E^\circ = +2.87\text{ V}$) significantly higher than that of Chlorine ($E^\circ = +1.36\text{ V}$). **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 16
**[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 5 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

1. Give reasons for the following observations:
   - (i) Noble gases possess positive values of electron gain enthalpy. *(1 Mark)*
   - (ii) The ionic radius of $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ($67.5\text{ pm}$) is significantly smaller than that of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($86\text{ pm}$), even though both ions are isoelectronic. *(1 Mark)*
   - (iii) The first ionization enthalpy of Gallium ($Z=31$, $\Delta_i H_1 = 579\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) is slightly higher than that of Aluminium ($Z=13$, $\Delta_i H_1 = 578\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$), deviating from the usual group trend. *(1 Mark)*
2. An element has atomic number $Z = 71$.
   - (i) Write its full electronic configuration. *(1 Mark)*
   - (ii) Identify its block, period, group, and IUPAC chemical name. *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Reasoning Sub-questions:**
   - **(i) Positive Electron Gain Enthalpy of Noble Gases:**  
     Noble gases have completely filled valence subshells ($n s^2 n p^6$), which provide high structural symmetry and stability. An additional electron must enter the next higher principal quantum shell ($n+1)s$, where it is screened by inner electrons and experiences minimal nuclear attraction. Work must be done against electrostatic repulsion to introduce this electron, making $\Delta_{eg}H$ **positive (endothermic)**. **[1 Mark]**
   - **(ii) Radius of $\text{Al}^{3+}$ vs. $\text{Mg}^{2+}$:**  
     Both $\text{Al}^{3+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ are isoelectronic with Neon ($10$ electrons, $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$). However, $\text{Al}^{3+}$ has $13$ protons ($Z=13$) while $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has only $12$ protons ($Z=12$). The effective nuclear charge ratio ($Z/e$) is higher in $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ($\frac{13}{10} = 1.3$) than in $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($\frac{12}{10} = 1.2$). The greater positive nuclear charge draws the electron cloud inward more strongly, making the ionic radius of $\text{Al}^{3+}$ smaller. **[1 Mark]**
   - **(iii) Ionization Enthalpy of $\text{Ga}$ vs. $\text{Al}$ (d-block contraction):**  
     Between $\text{Al}$ ($Z=13$) and $\text{Ga}$ ($Z=31$), the ten $3d$-electrons are filled ($3d^{10}$). The diffuse shapes of $d$-orbitals provide **poor shielding** to the outer $4s$ and $4p$ electrons against the increased nuclear charge ($+10$). Consequently, the outer electrons in Gallium experience an unexpectedly high effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$). This causes transition-metal contraction (d-block contraction), holding the valence electrons more tightly and making $\Delta_i H_1(\text{Ga})$ slightly higher than that of $\text{Al}$. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Element $Z = 71$ Analysis:**
   - **(i) Electronic Configuration:**
     Noble gas kernel is Xenon ($\text{Xe}$, $Z=54$).
     

$$
[\text{Xe}]_{54}\, 4f^{14}\, 5d^1\, 6s^2
$$

     or expanded: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^6 4d^{10} 5s^2 5p^6 4f^{14} 5d^1 6s^2$. **[1 Mark]**
   - **(ii) Periodic Location and Identification:**
     - **Block:** **$d$-block** (the last differentiating electron enters the $5d$ subshell, completing the $4f$ series). *(Note: Often classified with lanthanoids as an $f$-block congener, but formally placed in the $d$-block).*
     - **Period:** **6** ($n=6$).
     - **Group:** **3** (Group III-B).
     - **Element Name:** **Lutetium ($\text{Lu}$)**. **[1 Mark: ¼ mark each for Block, Period, Group, Name]**

#### Question 17
**[NCERT Exemplar Analytical Problem, 3 Marks]**  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

The first ($\Delta_i H_1$) and second ($\Delta_i H_2$) ionization enthalpies (in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) and the electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H$ in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) of four elements $W, X, Y,$ and $Z$ are given below:

| Element | $\Delta_i H_1$ | $\Delta_i H_2$ | $\Delta_{eg}H$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **W** | $419$ | $3051$ | $-48$ |
| **X** | $1681$ | $3374$ | $-328$ |
| **Y** | $2372$ | $5251$ | $+48$ |
| **Z** | $738$ | $1451$ | $-40$ |

Identify which of the above elements is likely to be:
1. The most reactive metal. *(1 Mark)*
2. The most reactive non-metal. *(1 Mark)*
3. The least reactive element (noble gas). *(1 Mark)*

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme

1. **Most Reactive Metal:**
   - **Element $W$**.
   - **Reasoning:** Element $W$ exhibits the lowest first ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1 = 419\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) along with a large jump to its second ionization enthalpy ($3051\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). This is characteristic of an alkali metal (such as Potassium, $\text{K}$). It readily loses its single valence electron to form a unipositive cation, making it the most electropositive and reactive metal. **[1 Mark]**

2. **Most Reactive Non-metal:**
   - **Element $X$**.
   - **Reasoning:** Element $X$ has a high first ionization enthalpy ($1681\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) and a very negative electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H = -328\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). This pattern is characteristic of a halogen (specifically Fluorine, $\text{F}$). It has a strong thermodynamic tendency to gain an electron to complete its octet, making it the most reactive non-metal. **[1 Mark]**

3. **Least Reactive Element (Noble Gas):**
   - **Element $Y$**.
   - **Reasoning:** Element $Y$ has the highest first ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1 = 2372\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) and a positive electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg}H = +48\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). This is characteristic of a noble gas with a stable, closed-shell electronic configuration (specifically Helium, $\text{He}$). It does not readily lose or gain electrons, making it chemically inert. **[1 Mark]**
