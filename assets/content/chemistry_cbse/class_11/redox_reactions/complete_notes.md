## Topic: Classical Concept of Redox Reactions and Electron Transfer Processes

***

## 1. Pedagogical Theory & Foundational Concepts

### 1.1 The Etymology and Core Premise of Redox Processes
The term **Redox** is a portmanteau of **Red**uction and **Ox**idation. Redox reactions constitute a fundamental class of chemical transformations involving stoichiometric, electrochemical, and electronic changes. These reactions govern diverse phenomena spanning biological respiration, metabolic energy transduction, industrial metallurgy, corrosion, combustion, and portable chemical energy sources (galvanic/electrolytic cells).

***

### 1.2 The Classical Concept (Oxygen and Hydrogen Transfer)
The classical framework developed historically from empirical observations of combustion, calcination, and smelting of ores.

#### Priority: ⭐⭐⭐ (Foundational Board & Historical Relevance)

#### (A) Oxidation (Classical Definitions)
1. **Addition of Oxygen:** Addition of oxygen or an electronegative element/radical to a substance.
2. **Removal of Hydrogen:** Removal of hydrogen or an electropositive element/radical from a substance.

#### (B) Reduction (Classical Definitions)
1. **Removal of Oxygen:** Removal of oxygen or an electronegative element/radical from a substance.
2. **Addition of Hydrogen:** Addition of hydrogen or an electropositive element/radical to a substance.

#### (C) Classical Classifications:
* **Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant):** A substance that supplies oxygen/electronegative element or removes hydrogen/electropositive element.
* **Reducing Agent (Reductant):** A substance that supplies hydrogen/electropositive element or removes oxygen/electronegative element.

***

### 1.3 Transition to Modern Electronic Theory: Electronegative & Electropositive Elements
Classical definitions failed to explain reactions occurring in non-aqueous, oxygen-free, and anhydrous environments (e.g., reaction between sodium and chlorine gas). The model was extended to include electronegative and electropositive species before evolving into the universal electron-transfer model.

* **Electronegative Element Addition = Oxidation:**
  An increase in the proportion of an electronegative element relative to the electropositive constituent.
* **Electropositive Element Addition = Reduction:**
  An increase in the proportion of an electropositive element relative to the electronegative constituent.

***

### 1.4 Electron Transfer Concept of Redox Reactions

#### Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Core JEE / NEET / CBSE Priority)

The electronic framework defines redox reactions strictly in terms of discrete or partial valence electron displacement between interacting atomic, ionic, or molecular species.

#### (A) Oxidation (De-electronation)
* Oxidation is defined as a process in which an atom, molecule, or ion **loses one or more electrons**.
* General representation:
  

$$
A \longrightarrow A^{n+} + n\,e^{-}
$$

* Oxidation leads to:
  * An increase in positive valence or oxidation state.
  * A decrease in negative charge.

#### (B) Reduction (Electronation)
* Reduction is defined as a process in which an atom, molecule, or ion **gains one or more electrons**.
* General representation:
  

$$
B + n\,e^{-} \longrightarrow B^{n-}
$$

* Reduction leads to:
  * A decrease in positive valence or oxidation state.
  * An increase in negative charge.

#### (C) Complementary Nature of Redox (Law of Conservation of Electric Charge)
Oxidation and reduction cannot occur in isolation. Electrons are subatomic particles with finite invariant rest mass ($m_e \approx 9.1093837 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$) and discrete elementary negative charge ($e \approx 1.60217663 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$). Because free isolated electrons cannot accumulate in a macroscopic stoichiometric chemical system:

$$
\sum n_{\text{lost}} = \sum n_{\text{gained}}
$$

Every redox transformation consists of two complementary halves termed **Redox Half-Reactions** or **Conjugate Redox Pairs**:

$$
\text{Oxidation Half-Reaction:} \quad \text{Reductant}_1 \longrightarrow \text{Conjugate Oxidant}_1 + n\,e^{-}
$$

$$
\text{Reduction Half-Reaction:} \quad \text{Oxidant}_2 + n\,e^{-} \longrightarrow \text{Conjugate Reductant}_2
$$

$$
\text{Net Overall Redox Reaction:} \quad \text{Reductant}_1 + \text{Oxidant}_2 \longrightarrow \text{Conjugate Oxidant}_1 + \text{Conjugate Reductant}_2
$$

#### (D) Definitions in Terms of Electron Transfer:
* **Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant / Electron Acceptor):** An entity that accepts electrons from another species. In doing so, the oxidant undergoes reduction.
* **Reducing Agent (Reductant / Electron Donor):** An entity that donates electrons to another species. In doing so, the reductant undergoes oxidation.

***

### 1.5 Competitive Electron Transfer & Relative Electron Releasing Tendency

#### Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Electrochemical Precursor)

When metallic zinc is immersed in an aqueous solution of copper(II) sulfate:

$$
\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

1. Zinc loses electrons to copper ions; metallic copper deposits as a spongy reddish-brown precipitate.
2. The blue color of the solution (attributed to hydrated $[\text{Cu}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6]^{2+}$ complexes) progressively fades.
3. The reaction is exothermic; $\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} < 0$.

Conversely, if a strip of metallic copper is placed into an aqueous solution of zinc sulfate:

$$
\text{Cu}(s) + \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{No Observable Reaction (Under Standard Conditions)}
$$

However, when metallic copper is placed in an aqueous silver nitrate ($\text{AgNO}_3$) solution:

$$
\text{Cu}(s) + 2\text{Ag}^{+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{Ag}(s)
$$

The solution turns blue due to the formation of $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)$, and lustrous dendritic silver crystals deposit on the copper strip.

**Deduction of Electron Release Tendency:**

$$
\text{Zn} > \text{Cu} > \text{Ag}
$$

This thermodynamic hierarchy of electron-donating power forms the foundational basis for the **Standard Electrochemical Series** ($E^\circ_{\text{reduction}}$ scale).

## 2. Chemical Equations, Derivations, and Step-by-Step Mechanisms

### 2.1 Classical Redox Systems: Step-by-Step Breakdown

#### Example 2.1.1: Removal of Hydrogen (Oxidation) & Addition of Hydrogen (Reduction)
Methane combustion in restricted vs. complete air streams:

$$
\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)
$$

* $\text{CH}_4 \to \text{CO}_2$: Removal of hydrogen from carbon and addition of electronegative oxygen $\implies$ **Carbon is oxidized**.
* $\text{O}_2 \to \text{H}_2\text{O}$: Addition of electropositive hydrogen to oxygen $\implies$ **Oxygen is reduced**.

#### Example 2.1.2: Reaction Involving Electronegative/Electropositive Elements (NCERT Classic)
Reaction of hydrogen sulfide with chlorine:

$$
\text{H}_2\text{S}(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{HCl}(g) + \text{S}(s)
$$

* **Oxidation:** $\text{H}_2\text{S} \to \text{S}$ (Removal of electropositive hydrogen from sulfur).
* **Reduction:** $\text{Cl}_2 \to 2\text{HCl}$ (Addition of electropositive hydrogen to chlorine).
* **Oxidizing Agent:** $\text{Cl}_2(g)$
* **Reducing Agent:** $\text{H}_2\text{S}(g)$

#### Example 2.1.3: Mercuric Chloride and Stannous Chloride Reaction
Reaction in aqueous acidic solution:

$$
2\text{HgCl}_2(aq) + \text{SnCl}_2(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Hg}_2\text{Cl}_2(s) \downarrow + \text{SnCl}_4(aq)
$$

Excess $\text{SnCl}_2$ further reduces calomel ($\text{Hg}_2\text{Cl}_2$) to elemental mercury:

$$
\text{Hg}_2\text{Cl}_2(s) + \text{SnCl}_2(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{Hg}(l) \downarrow + \text{SnCl}_4(aq)
$$

* **Classical Analysis:**
  * In $\text{SnCl}_2 \to \text{SnCl}_4$, the ratio of electronegative chlorine to tin increases from $2:1$ to $4:1$. Hence, $\text{SnCl}_2$ is **oxidized**.
  * In $2\text{HgCl}_2 \to \text{Hg}_2\text{Cl}_2$, the ratio of electropositive mercury to electronegative chlorine increases from $1:2$ ($0.5$) to $2:2$ ($1.0$). Hence, $\text{HgCl}_2$ is **reduced**.

***

### 2.2 Electronic De-electronation/Electronation Derivations

#### Example 2.2.1: Formation of Sodium Fluoride Lattice
Reaction:

$$
2\text{Na}(s) + \text{F}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaF}(s)
$$

* **Step 1: Sublimation of solid sodium:**
  

$$
\text{Na}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Na}(g) \quad [\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ > 0]
$$

* **Step 2: Ionization (Oxidation Half-Reaction):**
  

$$
\text{Na}(g) \longrightarrow \text{Na}^{+}(g) + e^{-} \quad [\Delta_i H^\circ = +495.8\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}]
$$

  *(Loss of electron $\implies$ Oxidation)*
* **Step 3: Dissociation of Fluorine:**
  

$$
\frac{1}{2}\text{F}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{F}(g) \quad \left[\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^\circ = +79.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\right]
$$

* **Step 4: Electron Gain (Reduction Half-Reaction):**
  

$$
\text{F}(g) + e^{-} \longrightarrow \text{F}^{-}(g) \quad [\Delta_{eg}H^\circ = -328.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}]
$$

  *(Gain of electron $\implies$ Reduction)*
* **Step 5: Electrostatic Lattice Condensation:**
  

$$
\text{Na}^{+}(g) + \text{F}^{-}(g) \longrightarrow \text{NaF}(s) \quad [\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\circ = -923.0\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}]
$$

**Overall Electronic Proof:**

$$
\boxed{2\text{Na} \longrightarrow 2\text{Na}^{+} + 2e^{-} \quad (\text{Oxidation Half-Reaction})}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{F}_2 + 2e^{-} \longrightarrow 2\text{F}^{-} \quad (\text{Reduction Half-Reaction})}
$$

Summing both equations cancels free electrons identically:

$$
\boxed{2\text{Na}(s) + \text{F}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{Na}^{+}\text{F}^{-}(s)}
$$

***

### 2.3 Partial Electron Transfer in Covalent Bond Formation
When reactants do not form distinct ionic lattices, oxidation and reduction are characterized by **partial electron displacement** driven by the electronegativity gradient ($\Delta \chi$).

#### Example: Synthesis of Hydrogen Chloride Gas

$$
\text{H}_2(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\text{HCl}(g)
$$

Paulings' Electronegativities:

$$
\chi(\text{H}) = 2.20, \quad \chi(\text{Cl}) = 3.16 \implies \Delta \chi = 0.96 \neq 0
$$

* In pure homonuclear diatomic $\text{H}_2$ and $\text{Cl}_2$, the shared electron pairs are positioned symmetrically between the two identical nuclei (formal oxidation state $= 0$).
* In polar covalent $\text{H}-\text{Cl}$, the bonded electron pair is pulled toward the more electronegative chlorine atom:
  

$$
\text{H}_2^0 \longrightarrow 2\text{H}^{\delta+} + 2e^{-}_{\text{partial displacement}}
$$

  

$$
\text{Cl}_2^0 + 2e^{-}_{\text{partial displacement}} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cl}^{\delta-}
$$

* **Conclusion:** Hydrogen experiences a net loss of electron density and is **oxidized**; chlorine experiences a net gain of electron density and is **reduced**.

## 3. High-Yield Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

### Problem 3.1 (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
**Question:** In the reaction:

$$
\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4(s) + 8\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow 3\text{PbCl}_2(s) + \text{Cl}_2(g) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)
$$

Identify which species is oxidized, which is reduced, and categorize the nature of the reaction using classical and modern concepts.

#### Step-by-Step Solution:
1. **Structural Deconstruction of $\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4$:**
   Red lead ($\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4$) is a mixed oxide:
   

$$
\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4 \equiv 2\text{PbO} \cdot \text{PbO}_2
$$

   Here, two lead atoms exist in the $+2$ oxidation state ($\text{Pb}^{\text{II}}$) and one lead atom exists in the $+4$ oxidation state ($\text{Pb}^{\text{IV}}$).

2. **Decoupling Acid-Base Neutralization vs. Redox:**
   * Reaction of basic oxide $\text{PbO}$ with $\text{HCl}$:
     

$$
2\text{PbO} + 4\text{HCl} \longrightarrow 2\text{PbCl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{Non-Redox Acid-Base Neutralization})
$$

   * Reaction of amphoteric/oxidizing oxide $\text{PbO}_2$ with $\text{HCl}$:
     

$$
\text{PbO}_2 + 4\text{HCl} \longrightarrow \text{PbCl}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{True Redox Process})
$$

3. **Electron Transfer Analysis:**
   * In $\text{PbO}_2$, lead is in the $\text{Pb}^{4+}$ state. It transitions to $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ in $\text{PbCl}_2$:
     

$$
\text{Pb}^{4+} + 2e^{-} \longrightarrow \text{Pb}^{2+} \quad (\text{Electronation} \implies \text{Reduction})
$$

   * In $\text{HCl}$, chloride ions exist as $\text{Cl}^-$. In $\text{Cl}_2(g)$, chlorine is in the elemental state ($0$):
     

$$
2\text{Cl}^{-} \longrightarrow \text{Cl}_2^0 + 2e^{-} \quad (\text{De-electronation} \implies \text{Oxidation})
$$

4. **Final Conclusion:**
   * **Substance Oxidized:** $\text{HCl}$ (specifically, chloride ion $\text{Cl}^-$)
   * **Substance Reduced:** $\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4$ (specifically, the lead(IV) constituent, $\text{PbO}_2$)
   * **Oxidizing Agent:** $\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4$
   * **Reducing Agent:** $\text{HCl}$

***

### Problem 3.2 (NCERT In-Text Problem)
**Question:** Justify whether the following reaction is a redox process based on the electron transfer concept:

$$
2\text{Na}(s) + \text{H}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{NaH}(s)
$$

#### Step-by-Step Solution:
1. **Analyze Reactants and Product:**
   Reactants: Pure metallic sodium $\text{Na}(s)$ and molecular hydrogen $\text{H}_2(g)$.
   Product: Sodium hydride $\text{NaH}(s)$, an ionic solid composed of sodium cations $\text{Na}^+$ and hydride anions $\text{H}^-$.
2. **Decompose into Half-Reactions:**
   * Oxidation Half-Reaction:
     

$$
2\text{Na}(s) \longrightarrow 2\text{Na}^{+} + 2e^{-}
$$

     Each sodium atom loses one electron. Sodium is oxidized; $\text{Na}$ acts as the **reductant**.
   * Reduction Half-Reaction:
     

$$
\text{H}_2(g) + 2e^{-} \longrightarrow 2\text{H}^{-}
$$

     Each hydrogen atom in the diatomic molecule gains one electron. Molecular hydrogen is reduced; $\text{H}_2$ acts as the **oxidant**.
3. **Synthesis:**
   Since electron loss and electron gain occur simultaneously in stoichiometric equivalence, the formation of sodium hydride is a **redox reaction**.

***

### Problem 3.3 (NCERT Exemplar Archetype - Competitive Electron Transfer)
**Question:** A strip of copper metal is immersed into an aqueous solution of silver nitrate ($\text{AgNO}_3$). After 30 minutes:
1. State the color change in the solution.
2. Formulate the ionic half-equations and net balanced molecular equation.
3. What would occur if a silver coin were immersed in aqueous copper(II) sulfate? Justify using electron transfer tendencies.

#### Step-by-Step Solution:
1. **Observations:**
   The solution gradually turns **sky-blue** due to the hydration of released $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions ($[\text{Cu}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6]^{2+}$). Shining dendritic silver precipitates out onto the copper strip.
2. **Formulation of Equations:**
   * Oxidation Half-Reaction:
     

$$
\text{Cu}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^{-}
$$

   * Reduction Half-Reaction:
     

$$
2\text{Ag}^{+}(aq) + 2e^{-} \longrightarrow 2\text{Ag}(s)
$$

   * Overall Ionic Equation:
     

$$
\text{Cu}(s) + 2\text{Ag}^{+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{Ag}(s)
$$

   * Balanced Molecular Equation:
     

$$
\text{Cu}(s) + 2\text{AgNO}_3(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2(aq) + 2\text{Ag}(s)
$$

3. **Silver Coin in Copper Sulfate:**
   * **Result:** No reaction occurs.
   * **Justification:** Copper has a higher electron-donating (oxidation) tendency than silver ($\text{Cu} > \text{Ag}$). Consequently, silver metal cannot reduce $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions to metallic copper under standard conditions.

## 4. Examiner Traps & Common Conceptual Mistakes

### Trap 1: Conflating "Addition of Hydrogen" with Acid-Base Proton Transfer
* **Error:** Students often confuse Brønsted-Lowry acid-base reactions with redox reactions.
  

$$
\text{NH}_3(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_4^{+}(aq) + \text{OH}^{-}(aq)
$$

* **Correction:** A proton ($\text{H}^{+}$) transfer is **not** an electron transfer.
  In this reaction, $\text{H}^{+}$ moves without carrying its electron. The oxidation state of nitrogen remains $-3$, hydrogen remains $+1$, and oxygen remains $-2$. This is an **acid-base reaction**, not a redox process.

### Trap 2: Incorrect Identification of the Oxidant/Reductant
* **Error:** Students often write individual ions (e.g., $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ or $\text{S}^{2-}$) when asked to identify the oxidizing or reducing agent in a molecular reaction.
* **Correction:** Unless the question specifies the *ionic species*, standard board guidelines require naming the entire **chemical compound/reagent** as present in the reaction equation (e.g., stating that $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ is the reducing agent, not just $\text{S}^{2-}$).

### Trap 3: Assuming Oxygen Always Acts as an Oxidizing Agent
* **Error:** Assuming that whenever oxygen combines with an element, oxygen is the oxidant and the other element is oxidized.
* **Counterexample:** Reaction with fluorine:
  

$$
\text{O}_2(g) + 2\text{F}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{OF}_2(g)
$$

  Fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen ($\chi(\text{F}) = 4.0$, $\chi(\text{O}) = 3.5$). Oxygen loses electron density to fluorine ($\text{O}^0 \to \text{O}^{2+}$) and is **oxidized**, while fluorine is **reduced**. Here, elemental oxygen serves as the **reducing agent**.

### Trap 4: Non-Redox Decomposition vs. Redox Decomposition
* **Non-Redox Decomposition:** Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate:
  

$$
\text{CaCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)
$$

  $(\text{Ca}: +2 \to +2; \quad \text{C}: +4 \to +4; \quad \text{O}: -2 \to -2)$ $\implies$ **No electron transfer occurs.**
* **Redox Decomposition:** Thermal decomposition of potassium chlorate:
  

$$
2\text{KClO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta, [\text{MnO}_2]} 2\text{KCl}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g)
$$

  $(\text{Cl}: +5 \to -1; \quad \text{O}: -2 \to 0)$ $\implies$ **True redox process.**

### 5.3 Golden Points for Instant Problem-Solving
1. **Spectator Ion Elimination Technique:**
   To identify electron transfer in aqueous solutions, eliminate non-reacting spectator ions (e.g., $\text{K}^{+}$, $\text{Na}^{+}$, $\text{NO}_3^{-}$, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$).
   

$$
\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \cancel{\text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq)} \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \cancel{\text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq)} + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

   This immediately reveals the active electron transfer pair:
   

$$
\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

2. **Equivalence of Loss and Gain:**
   In any balanced redox equation:
   

$$
\text{Moles of } e^{-} \text{ lost by reductant} \equiv \text{Moles of } e^{-} \text{ gained by oxidant}
$$

3. **Displacement Hierarchy:**
   A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its aqueous salt solution because it releases electrons more readily:
   

$$
\text{Reactivity/Electron release tendency: } \text{Zn} > \text{Fe} > \text{Ni} > \text{Sn} > \text{Pb} > [\text{H}] > \text{Cu} > \text{Ag} > \text{Au}
$$

## Master Lecture Notes: Oxidation Number, Determination Rules, and Fractional Oxidation States

***

# 1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Foundational Concepts

### Concept 1.1: Historical vs. Modern Electrochemical Framework
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐  
Historically, redox processes were viewed through classical phenomenological lenses:
1. **Oxidation:** Addition of oxygen/electronegative element, or removal of hydrogen/electropositive element.
2. **Reduction:** Addition of hydrogen/electropositive element, or removal of oxygen/electronegative element.

In modern electronic theory, oxidation is defined as the **de-electronation process** (loss of electrons) and reduction is the **electronation process** (gain of electrons):

$$
\text{Oxidation: } \text{M} \xrightarrow{} \text{M}^{n+} + n e^{-}
$$

$$
\text{Reduction: } \text{X} + m e^{-} \xrightarrow{} \text{X}^{m-}
$$

However, in predominantly covalent compounds with directional polar bonds (e.g., $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{SO}_2$), physical transfer of electrons does not take place. To track the redistribution of electron density along chemical bonds, IUPAC and NCERT formalize the concept of **Oxidation Number (O.N.)**.

***

### Concept 1.2: Rigorous NCERT Definition of Oxidation Number
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
> **Definition (NCERT):** Oxidation number denotes the oxidation state of an element in a compound ascertained according to a set of rules formulated on the basis that electron pairs in a covalent bond belong entirely to the more electronegative element.

It represents the **formal residual electric charge** an atom would possess if all heteroatomic bonds attached to it were cleaved heterolytically, allocating the bonding electron pair to the more electronegative partner.

#### Physical Meaning of the Cleavage Model:
For a diatomic bond $\text{A}-\text{B}$:
- If $\chi(\text{B}) > \chi(\text{A})$ (where $\chi$ denotes Pauling electronegativity):
  

$$
\text{A}-\text{B} \xrightarrow{\text{Heterolysis}} \text{A}^{+} + \text{B}^{-}
$$

  The contribution to O.N. is $+1$ on atom $\text{A}$ and $-1$ on atom $\text{B}$.
- For a double bond $\text{A}=\text{B}$ with $\chi(\text{B}) > \chi(\text{A})$:
  

$$
\text{A}=\text{B} \xrightarrow{\text{Heterolysis}} \text{A}^{2+} + \text{B}^{2-}
$$

  The contribution is $+2$ on $\text{A}$ and $-2$ on $\text{B}$.
- For a homonuclear bond $\text{A}-\text{A}$ ($\Delta\chi = 0$):
  

$$
\text{A}-\text{A} \xrightarrow{\text{Homolysis}} \text{A}^{\bullet} + \text{A}^{\bullet}
$$

  The contribution to O.N. from this bond is $0$ for both atoms.

***

### Concept 1.3: Fundamental Difference: Oxidation State vs. Valency
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  

| Parameter | Valency | Oxidation Number (Oxidation State) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Combining capacity of an element; number of bonds an atom can form. | Apparent/formal charge on an atom after assigning bonded electrons to more electronegative atoms. |
| **Mathematical Sign** | Pure dimensionless positive integer (No sign). | Always carries a formal sign ($+$, $-$, or $0$). |
| **Fractional Values** | **Never** fractional (always integer $1, 2, 3, \dots$). | Can be fractional (average oxidation state). |
| **Zero Value** | Zero only for noble gases in ground state. | Can be zero for elements in compounds (e.g., $\text{C}$ in $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$ or glucose $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$). |
| **Dependence** | Constant in most traditional hydrides/halides. | Varies widely depending on chemical environment (e.g., $\text{Mn}$ exhibits states from $-3$ to $+7$). |

***

# 2. Comprehensive Set of Standard NCERT Rules for O.N. Determination

**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

To assign oxidation numbers algebraically without drawing complete Lewis structures for every molecule, the following hierarchical rules are universally applied:

### Rule 1: Elementary / Free State Rule
In elemental form (free or uncombined state), each atom possesses an oxidation number of zero ($0$), irrespective of allotropic form or atomicity:

$$
\text{O.N. of atoms in } \text{H}_2, \text{O}_2, \text{O}_3, \text{N}_2, \text{P}_4, \text{S}_8, \text{C}_{\text{diamond}}, \text{C}_{\text{graphite}}, \text{Fe}(s), \text{Na}(s) = 0
$$

### Rule 2: Monoatomic Ions
For monoatomic ions, the oxidation number is identical to the net ionic charge:

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{Na}^+) = +1, \quad \text{O.N.}(\text{Mg}^{2+}) = +2, \quad \text{O.N.}(\text{Al}^{3+}) = +3
$$

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{Cl}^-) = -1, \quad \text{O.N.}(\text{O}^{2-}) = -2, \quad \text{O.N.}(\text{N}^{3-}) = -3
$$

### Rule 3: Group 1 and Group 2 Metals
In all their chemical compounds:
- **Alkali metals (Group 1: $\text{Li}, \text{Na}, \text{K}, \text{Rb}, \text{Cs}$):** Always $+1$.
- **Alkaline earth metals (Group 2: $\text{Be}, \text{Mg}, \text{Ca}, \text{Sr}, \text{Ba}$):** Always $+2$.
- **Aluminium (Group 13):** Always $+3$ in its stable compounds.

### Rule 4: Hydrogen
- In the majority of compounds (bonded to non-metals): $\text{O.N.}(\text{H}) = +1$ (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{NH}_3, \text{CH}_4, \text{HCl}$).
- In binary metal hydrides (bonded to highly electropositive Group 1 or Group 2 elements): $\text{O.N.}(\text{H}) = -1$ (Hydride ion $\text{H}^-$).
  

$$
\text{LiH}, \text{NaH}, \text{CaH}_2 \implies \text{O.N.}(\text{H}) = -1
$$

- In complex hydrides: $\text{NaBH}_4, \text{LiAlH}_4 \implies \text{O.N.}(\text{H}) = -1$.

### Rule 5: Oxygen
Oxygen exhibits variable oxidation numbers depending on bond type:
1. **Normal Oxides (Oxide ion, $\text{O}^{2-}$):** $\text{O.N.} = -2$ (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{MgO}, \text{CO}_2, \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
2. **Peroxides (Peroxy linkage, $-\text{O}-\text{O}-$, containing $\text{O}_2^{2-}$):** $\text{O.N.} = -1$ (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2, \text{Na}_2\text{O}_2, \text{BaO}_2$).
3. **Superoxides (Superoxide ion, $\text{O}_2^-$):** $\text{O.N.} = -\frac{1}{2}$ (e.g., $\text{KO}_2, \text{RbO}_2, \text{CsO}_2$).
4. **Fluorinated Oxygen Compounds (Fluorine is more electronegative than Oxygen):**
   - In Oxygen Difluoride ($\text{OF}_2$): $\text{O.N.}(\text{O}) = +2$
   - In Dioxygen Difluoride ($\text{O}_2\text{F}_2$): $\text{O.N.}(\text{O}) = +1$

### Rule 6: Halogens
- **Fluorine ($\text{F}$):** Most electronegative element ($\chi = 4.0$). It exhibits an invariant oxidation state of $-1$ in all its compounds.
- **Other Halogens ($\text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$):**
  - Exhibit an oxidation state of $-1$ when bonded to metals or less electronegative non-metals (e.g., $\text{NaCl}, \text{KBr}, \text{HI}$).
  - Exhibit positive oxidation states ($+1, +3, +5, +7$) when coordinated or bonded to more electronegative halogen atoms (interhalogens) or oxygen (oxoacids and oxides):
    

$$
\text{In } \text{KClO}_3: \quad (+1) + x + 3(-2) = 0 \implies x = +5
$$

    

$$
\text{In } \text{IF}_7: \quad x + 7(-1) = 0 \implies x = +7
$$

    

$$
\text{In } \text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7: \quad 2x + 7(-2) = 0 \implies x = +7
$$

### Rule 7: Principle of Electroneutrality (Summation Rules)
- For an electrically neutral polyatomic molecule: The algebraic sum of the oxidation numbers of all constituent atoms is zero:
  

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N} n_i \cdot \text{O.N.}_i = 0
$$

- For a polyatomic ionic species: The algebraic sum of the oxidation numbers equals the net charge $z$ of the ion:
  

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N} n_i \cdot \text{O.N.}_i = z
$$

# 3. Chemical Derivations & Structural Mechanisms of Coordinate/Polar Bonds

**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

### Mathematical Formulation: Chemical Cleavage Paradigm
Let a bond have multiplicity $m$ ($m=1$ for single, $2$ for double, $3$ for triple) between atom $\text{A}$ and atom $\text{B}$.
1. **Covalent Heteropolar Bond:**
   

$$
\Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{A}) = +m \quad \text{if } \chi(\text{B}) > \chi(\text{A})
$$

   

$$
\Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{B}) = -m \quad \text{if } \chi(\text{B}) > \chi(\text{A})
$$

2. **Covalent Homopolar Bond:**
   

$$
\Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{A}) = 0, \quad \Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{B}) = 0 \quad \text{if } \chi(\text{A}) = \chi(\text{B})
$$

### Coordinate (Dative) Covalent Bond Formalism: $\text{A} \rightarrow \text{B}$
When evaluating a coordinate covalent bond where donor $\text{A}$ provides both electrons to acceptor $\text{B}$:

$$
\text{Case 1: Donor atom is LESS electronegative than Acceptor: } \chi(\text{A}) \le \chi(\text{B})
$$

Heterolytic cleavage sends both shared electrons to the more electronegative acceptor atom $\text{B}$.

$$
\text{Contribution: } \Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{A}) = +2, \quad \Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{B}) = -2
$$

$$
\text{Case 2: Donor atom is MORE electronegative than Acceptor: } \chi(\text{A}) > \chi(\text{B})
$$

Since $\text{A}$ possesses higher electronegativity, its inherent pulling power retains the shared pair during hypothetical ionic cleavage. Thus, no net transfer of electrons is credited beyond its original share.

$$
\text{Contribution: } \Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{A}) = 0, \quad \Delta \text{O.N.}(\text{B}) = 0
$$

#### Exemplary Case: The Isocyanide ($\text{R}-\text{N}\equiv\text{C}$) Molecule
Consider Hydrogen Isocyanide ($\text{H}-\text{N}\substack{\rightarrow \\ =}\text{C}$):
- Electronegativities: $\chi(\text{N}) \approx 3.04$, $\chi(\text{C}) \approx 2.55$, $\chi(\text{H}) \approx 2.20$.
- Bond $\text{H}-\text{N}$: $\text{N}$ is more electronegative $\implies \text{H} = +1, \text{N} = -1$.
- Double covalent bond in $\text{N}=\text{C}$: $\text{N}$ takes $2$ electron pairs $\implies \text{N} = -2, \text{C} = +2$.
- Coordinate bond $\text{N} \rightarrow \text{C}$: Donor is $\text{N}$ ($\chi = 3.04$), Acceptor is $\text{C}$ ($\chi = 2.55$). Since donor is more electronegative: contribution $= 0$.
Summing up the partial oxidation states:

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{N}) = -1 + (-2) + 0 = -3
$$

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{C}) = +2 + 0 = +2
$$

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{H}) = +1
$$

$$
\text{Total Charge} = (+1) + (-3) + (+2) = 0
$$

# 4. The Fallacy of Average O.N. vs. Structural Oxidation State

**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
Direct algebraic calculation yields an **average oxidation number**, which can obscure structural reality or yield non-physical fractional states.

***

### Case A: Caro's Acid (Peroxomonosulphuric Acid, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_5$)

#### Algebraic Failure:

$$
2(+1) + x + 5(-2) = 0 \implies x - 8 = 0 \implies x = +8
$$

This is **chemically impossible** because Sulphur has only $6$ valence electrons ($[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2\,3p^4$). Its maximum possible oxidation state cannot exceed $+6$.

#### Structural Resolution:
Caro's acid contains one peroxy linkage ($-\text{O}-\text{O}-$). Its structural formula is $\text{H}-\text{O}-\text{SO}_2-\text{O}-\text{O}-\text{H}$.

```
        O (-2)
        ||
H - O - S - O - O - H
(-1)|   ||  (-1)(-1)(+1)
(+1)|   O (-2)
```

- $3$ normal oxide oxygens (one $-\text{OH}$ oxygen and two terminal $=0$ oxygens): each has O.N. $= -2$.
- $2$ peroxide oxygens (in the peroxy segment $-\text{O}-\text{O}-\text{H}$): each has O.N. $= -1$.
- $2$ protons ($\text{H}$): each has O.N. $= +1$.

Applying electroneutrality:

$$
2(+1) + x + 3(-2) + 2(-1) = 0
$$

$$
2 + x - 6 - 2 = 0 \implies x - 6 = 0
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{O.N. of Sulphur in } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_5 = +6}
$$

***

### Case B: Marshall's Acid (Peroxodisulphuric Acid, $\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8$)

#### Algebraic Failure:

$$
2(+1) + 2x + 8(-2) = 0 \implies 2x - 14 = 0 \implies x = +7 \quad (\text{Exceeds group valency } +6)
$$

#### Structural Resolution:
Marshall's acid has a central peroxy bridge: $\text{HO}_3\text{S}-\text{O}-\text{O}-\text{SO}_3\text{H}$.

```
     O        O
     ||       ||
HO - S - O-O- S - OH
     ||       ||
     O        O
```

- Number of non-peroxide oxygens $= 6$ (each $-2$).
- Number of peroxide oxygens $= 2$ (each $-1$).
- Number of hydrogens $= 2$ (each $+1$).

$$
2(+1) + 2x + 6(-2) + 2(-1) = 0
$$

$$
2 + 2x - 12 - 2 = 0 \implies 2x = 12 \implies \boxed{x = +6}
$$

Both sulphur atoms exist in a $+6$ oxidation state.

***

### Case C: Chromium Peroxide ($\text{CrO}_5$) - "Butterfly Structure"

#### Algebraic Failure:

$$
x + 5(-2) = 0 \implies x = +10 \quad (\text{Chromium has only } 6 \text{ valence electrons: } [\text{Ar}]\,3d^5\,4s^1)
$$

#### Structural Resolution:
Chromium pentoxide forms an adduct with a characteristic butterfly geometry containing two peroxo groups and one oxo group:

```
    O (-2)
    ||
O - Cr - O
 \ /  \ /    (Each peroxy oxygen = -1)
  O    O
```

- $1$ terminal oxo oxygen ($\text{Cr}=\text{O}$): contribution $= -2$.
- $4$ peroxide oxygens ($2$ bidentate peroxo ligands $[\text{O}_2]^{2-}$): contribution $= 4 \times (-1) = -4$.

$$
x + 1(-2) + 4(-1) = 0 \implies x - 6 = 0 \implies \boxed{x = +6}
$$

***

# 5. Fractional Oxidation States: Origin & Structural Reality

**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
> **Physical Law:** Electrons are indivisible elementary particles; an individual atom **cannot** gain or lose fractional parts of an electron. A fractional oxidation state reported for a compound is always an **arithmetic average** of structurally non-equivalent atoms of the same element possessing integer oxidation states.

***

### System 1: Carbon Suboxide ($\text{C}_3\text{O}_2$)
Formed by dehydrating malonic acid with $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$:

$$
\text{CH}_2(\text{COOH})_2 \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}} \text{O}=\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{O} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

#### 1. Algebraic Calculation:

$$
3x + 2(-2) = 0 \implies 3x = +4 \implies x = +\frac{4}{3}
$$

#### 2. Structural Heterolysis:
The molecule is linear:

$$
\overset{\text{O}}{\text{O}}=\overset{\text{C}_1}{\text{C}}=\overset{\text{C}_2}{\text{C}}=\overset{\text{C}_3}{\text{C}}=\overset{\text{O}}{\text{O}}
$$

1. **Terminal Carbon Atoms ($\text{C}_1$ and $\text{C}_3$):**
   - Each is double bonded to Oxygen ($\chi = 3.44$ vs $\chi = 2.55$). Cleavage of $\text{C}=\text{O}$ gives $+2$ to Carbon and $-2$ to Oxygen.
   - Each is double bonded to central Carbon ($\text{C}_2$). Homonuclear bond cleavage gives $0$.
   

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{C}_1) = +2, \quad \text{O.N.}(\text{C}_3) = +2
$$

2. **Central Carbon Atom ($\text{C}_2$):**
   - Bound solely to carbon atoms via two double bonds ($\text{C}_1=\text{C}_2=\text{C}_3$).
   - Heterolytic electronegativity difference $\Delta\chi = 0$.
   

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{C}_2) = 0
$$

#### 3. Average Value Derivation:

$$
\text{O.N.}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{O.N.}(\text{C}_1) + \text{O.N.}(\text{C}_2) + \text{O.N.}(\text{C}_3)}{3} = \frac{(+2) + 0 + (+2)}{3} = \boxed{+\frac{4}{3}}
$$

***

### System 2: Tribromo-octaoxide ($\text{Br}_3\text{O}_8$)

$$
\text{Preparation: } 3\text{Br}_2 + 8\text{O}_3 \xrightarrow{-78^\circ\text{C}} 2\text{Br}_3\text{O}_8
$$

#### 1. Algebraic Calculation:

$$
3x + 8(-2) = 0 \implies 3x = +16 \implies x = +\frac{16}{3}
$$

#### 2. Structural Heterolysis:
The framework consists of a bromine chain $\text{Br}-\text{Br}-\text{Br}$:
```
    O        O        O
    ||       ||       ||
O = Br   -   Br   -   Br = O
    ||       ||       ||
    O                 O
 (Terminal) (Central)(Terminal)
```

1. **Terminal Bromine Atoms ($\text{Br}_t$):**
   - Each terminal $\text{Br}$ forms three double bonds with oxygen ($\text{Br}=\text{O}$) and one single homonuclear bond to central bromine ($\text{Br}-\text{Br}$).
   - Cleavage of $3 \times (\text{Br}=\text{O})$ gives $3 \times (+2) = +6$.
   - Cleavage of homonuclear $\text{Br}-\text{Br}$ gives $0$.
   

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{Br}_t) = +6
$$

2. **Central Bromine Atom ($\text{Br}_c$):**
   - Forms two double bonds with oxygen atoms: $2 \times (+2) = +4$.
   - Forms two single homonuclear $\text{Br}-\text{Br}$ bonds: $2 \times (0) = 0$.
   

$$
\text{O.N.}(\text{Br}_c) = +4
$$

#### 3. Average Value Derivation:

$$
\text{O.N.}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2 \times (+6) + 1 \times (+4)}{3} = \frac{12 + 4}{3} = \boxed{+\frac{16}{3}}
$$

***

### System 3: Tetrathionate Ion ($\text{S}_4\text{O}_6^{2-}$)
Generated during the analytical iodometric titration of sodium thiosulphate:

$$
2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-} + \text{I}_2 \longrightarrow \text{S}_4\text{O}_6^{2-} + 2\text{I}^-
$$

#### 1. Algebraic Calculation:

$$
4x + 6(-2) = -2 \implies 4x - 12 = -2 \implies 4x = +10 \implies x = +2.5 \quad \left(+\frac{5}{2}\right)
$$

#### 2. Structural Heterolysis:

$$
\left[ \text{O}_3\overset{+5}{\text{S}} - \overset{0}{\text{S}} - \overset{0}{\text{S}} - \overset{+5}{\text{S}}\text{O}_3 \right]^{2-}
$$

```
    O(-)                   O(-)
    |                      |
O = S   -   S  -  S   -    S = O
    ||                     ||
    O                      O
 (Terminal)(Middle)(Middle)(Terminal)
```

1. **Two Terminal Sulphur Atoms:**
   - Each bonded to two oxo oxygens ($\text{S}=\text{O}$, contributing $+4$), one alkoxide-like oxygen ($\text{S}-\text{O}^-$, contributing $+1$), and one sulphur atom ($\text{S}-\text{S}$, contributing $0$).
   

$$
\text{O.N.} = +2 + 2 + 1 + 0 = +5
$$

2. **Two Central Sulphur Atoms:**
   - Bound exclusively to neighboring sulphur atoms via homonuclear single covalent bonds ($\text{S}-\text{S}-\text{S}$).
   

$$
\Delta\chi = 0 \implies \text{O.N.} = 0
$$

#### 3. Average Value Derivation:

$$
\text{O.N.}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{(+5) + 0 + 0 + (+5)}{4} = \frac{10}{4} = \boxed{+2.5}
$$

***

### System 4: Mixed Oxides ($\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4, \text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4, \text{Mn}_3\text{O}_4$)
These compounds exist as stoichiometric interstitial lattice combinations of two distinct oxides:

1. **Magnetite ($\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$):**
   - Empirical composition: $\text{FeO} \cdot \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$
   - Lattice contents: One $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ion (O.N. $= +2$) and two $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ions (O.N. $= +3$).
   

$$
\text{O.N.}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1(+2) + 2(+3)}{3} = \boxed{+\frac{8}{3}}
$$

2. **Red Lead / Sindoor ($\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4$):**
   - Empirical composition: $2\text{PbO} \cdot \text{PbO}_2$
   - Lattice contents: Two $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ ions and one $\text{Pb}^{4+}$ ion.
   

$$
\text{O.N.}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2(+2) + 1(+4)}{3} = \boxed{+\frac{8}{3}}
$$

# 6. High-Yield Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

### Example 1: Oxidation State of Sulphur in Sodium Thiosulphate ($\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3$)
**Problem:** Calculate the average and structural oxidation states of sulphur in $\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3$.  
**Solution:**
- **Average Method:**
  

$$
2(+1) + 2x + 3(-2) = 0 \implies 2x - 4 = 0 \implies x = +2
$$

- **Structural Method:**  
  Thiosulphate is structurally derived by replacing one oxygen of sulphate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$) with sulphur: $[\text{S}-\text{SO}_3]^{2-}$.
  The terminal sulphur behaves as a sulphide-like ligand coordinated to the central sulphur:
  

$$
\text{Central S bonded to: } 2 \times (-\text{O}^-) \implies +2
$$

  

$$
1 \times (=\text{O}) \implies +2
$$

  

$$
1 \times (=\text{S}) \implies +2 \quad (\chi(\text{S}) \approx \chi(\text{S}), \text{ but terminal acts as acceptor/electronegative equivalent})
$$

  More rigorously via coordinate representation:
  

$$
\text{Terminal S} = -2, \quad \text{Central S} = +6
$$

  

$$
\text{Average} = \frac{+6 + (-2)}{2} = \boxed{+2}
$$

***

### Example 2: Oxidation State of Nitrogen in Ammonium Nitrate ($\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$)
**Problem:** Determine the individual oxidation states of both nitrogen atoms in $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$.  
**Solution:**  
$\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$ is an ionic compound composed of the cation $\text{NH}_4^+$ and the anion $\text{NO}_3^-$. Applying the algebraic rule to each ion independently:
1. **For Cation ($\text{NH}_4^+$):**
   

$$
x + 4(+1) = +1 \implies x = +1 - 4 \implies \boxed{\text{O.N.} = -3}
$$

2. **For Anion ($\text{NO}_3^-$):**
   

$$
y + 3(-2) = -1 \implies y - 6 = -1 \implies \boxed{\text{O.N.} = +5}
$$

*(Note: Calculating the average via formula $\text{N}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_3$ gives $2z + 4 - 6 = 0 \implies z = +1$, which completely hides the true chemical behavior).*

***

### Example 3: Oxidation State of Iron in the "Brown Ring" Complex
**Problem:** Find the oxidation state of $\text{Fe}$ in $[\text{Fe}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_5(\text{NO})]\text{SO}_4$.  
**Solution:**  
- Outer sphere counter-ion is $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, so the coordination sphere has charge $+2$:
  

$$
[\text{Fe}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_5(\text{NO})]^{2+}
$$

- Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is a neutral ligand: $\text{O.N.} = 0$.
- Magnetic moment measurements ($\mu \approx 3.87\text{ B.M.}$, corresponding to 3 unpaired electrons) prove electron transfer occurs from $\text{NO}$ to $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, yielding a nitrosyl cation ($\text{NO}^+$):
  

$$
x + 5(0) + (+1) = +2 \implies x + 1 = +2 \implies \boxed{x = +1}
$$

Iron exists in an unusual $+1$ oxidation state ($3d^7$).

***

### Example 4: Oxidation State of Carbon in Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) and Acetic Acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$)
**Problem:** Evaluate the oxidation numbers of carbon in glucose and both carbon atoms in acetic acid.  
**Solution:**
- **Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$):**
  

$$
6x + 12(+1) + 6(-2) = 0 \implies 6x + 12 - 12 = 0 \implies 6x = 0 \implies \boxed{x = 0}
$$

  *(Individual carbons range from $-1$ in $-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ to $+1$ in $-\text{CH}=\text{O}$, averaging zero).*
- **Acetic Acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$):**
  - Average: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_2 \implies 2x + 4(+1) + 2(-2) = 0 \implies 2x = 0 \implies x = 0$.
  - Structural:
    - Methyl carbon ($\text{C}-2$ in $\text{CH}_3-$): Bonded to $3\text{H}$ and $1\text{C} \implies x + 3(+1) + 0 = 0 \implies \mathbf{-3}$.
    - Carboxyl carbon ($\text{C}-1$ in $-\text{COOH}$): Bonded to $1\text{C}$, $1(=\text{O})$, and $1(-\text{OH})$:
      

$$
y + 0 + (-2) + (-1) = 0 \implies \mathbf{+3}
$$

  

$$
\text{Average} = \frac{-3 + (+3)}{2} = \boxed{0}
$$

# 7. Examiner Traps & Common Student Errors

### 1. The Maximum Oxidation Number Limit Trap
> **The Rule:** The maximum possible oxidation number of any representative $p$-block element can never exceed its **group valence number** (Total number of valence electrons, or $\text{Group Number} - 10$). For transition metals ($d$-block), it cannot exceed $(n-1)d + ns$ valence electrons.

- **Phosphorus (Group 15):** Max O.N. $= +5$. If you compute $+7$ for $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_5$, suspect a peroxy bond:
  

$$
\text{H}_3\text{PO}_5 \implies \text{H}_3\text{PO}_3(\text{O}_2) \implies \text{O.N.}(\text{P}) = +5
$$

- **Sulphur (Group 16):** Max O.N. $= +6$. If an equation yields $+7$ or $+8$, identify peroxo groups ($-\text{O}-\text{O}-$).
- **Chromium (Group 6):** Max O.N. $= +6$. Any calculation giving $+10$ ($\text{CrO}_5$) ignores peroxy linkages.

### 2. The Hydride Sign Confusion Trap
- Students routinely assign $\text{H} = +1$ out of habit.
- **Trap:** In saline/alkali hydrides ($\text{NaH}, \text{CaH}_2, \text{BaH}_2$), metals have vastly lower electronegativity than Hydrogen ($\chi(\text{Na}) \approx 0.9$, $\chi(\text{H}) \approx 2.2$). Thus, $\text{H}$ exists as hydride ($\text{H}^-$) with $\text{O.N.} = -1$.
- **Error in balancing:** In the reaction:
  

$$
\text{CaH}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2 + 2\text{H}_2
$$

  Hydrogen in $\text{CaH}_2$ ($-1$) is oxidized to $\text{H}_2$ ($0$), while Hydrogen in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($+1$) is reduced to $\text{H}_2$ ($0$). This is a **comproportionation reaction**.

### 3. Missing the Sign on Zero or Positive States
- Unlike valency, oxidation numbers **must explicitly carry a sign**. Writing "$1$" or "$2$" instead of "$+1$" or "$+2$" is penalized in CBSE marking schemes.
- An oxidation state of zero must be explicitly stated as **$0$** (not left blank or labeled "neutral").

### 4. Direct Formula Application to Coordinate Adducts
- When finding the oxidation state of Carbon in $\text{HCN}$ vs $\text{HNC}$:
  - In $\text{H}-\text{C}\equiv\text{N}$: $\text{H} = +1, \text{N} = -3 \implies \text{C} = +2$.
  - In $\text{H}-\text{N}\substack{\rightarrow \\ =}\text{C}$: $\text{H} = +1, \text{N} = -3 \implies \text{C} = +2$.
- In nickel tetracarbonyl $[\text{Ni}(\text{CO})_4]$, carbon monoxide is a neutral ligand:
  

$$
x + 4(0) = 0 \implies \text{O.N.}(\text{Ni}) = 0
$$

```
Fluorine (F)                      -1                         NONE (Always -1)
Alkali Metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) +1                         NONE (In all compounds)
Alkaline Earth (Be, Mg, Ca, Ba)   +2                         NONE (In all compounds)
Aluminium (Al)                    +3                         NONE (In all compounds)
Hydrogen (H)                      +1                         -1 in Metal Hydrides (NaH, CaH2)
Oxygen (O)                        -2                         -1 in Peroxides (H2O2)
                                                             -1/2 in Superoxides (KO2)
                                                             +2 in OF2, +1 in O2F2
Halogens (Cl, Br, I)              -1                         Positive (+1 to +7) with O & F
```

### Golden Anchor 2: The Range Rule Formula
For any element belonging to group $n$ (IUPAC groups 13 to 17):

$$
\text{Minimum Possible O.N.} = n - 18
$$

$$
\text{Maximum Possible O.N.} = n - 10
$$

*Example for Chlorine (Group 17):*
- $\text{Minimum O.N.} = 17 - 18 = \mathbf{-1}$ (e.g., $\text{HCl}$)
- $\text{Maximum O.N.} = 17 - 10 = \mathbf{+7}$ (e.g., $\text{HClO}_4$)
*Redox deduction:*
- A compound at its maximum oxidation state (e.g., $\text{HNO}_3$ with $\text{N} = +5$, $\text{KMnO}_4$ with $\text{Mn} = +7$) can **only act as an oxidizing agent** (it can only decrease its O.N.).
- A compound at its minimum oxidation state (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ with $\text{S} = -2$, $\text{NH}_3$ with $\text{N} = -3$) can **only act as a reducing agent**.
- A compound in an intermediate oxidation state (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ with $\text{O} = -1$, $\text{HNO}_2$ with $\text{N} = +3$, $\text{SO}_2$ with $\text{S} = +4$) can act as **both an oxidizing and a reducing agent**, and can undergo **disproportionation**.

### Golden Anchor 3: Shortcut for Finding the Number of Peroxy Bonds
If the calculated apparent oxidation number ($x_{\text{calc}}$) exceeds the maximum allowed group valence ($x_{\text{max}}$):

$$
\text{Number of Peroxy Linkages } (-\text{O}-\text{O}-) = \frac{x_{\text{calc}} - x_{\text{max}}}{2 \times 1} = \frac{x_{\text{calc}} - x_{\text{max}}}{1} \text{ per metal atom? No:}
$$

Use the exact peroxy oxygen count formula:

$$
\text{Number of Peroxy Oxygen Atoms } (N_{\text{peroxy}}) = x_{\text{calc}} - x_{\text{max}}
$$

Each peroxy linkage $(-\text{O}-\text{O}-)$ contains two peroxy oxygen atoms:

$$
\text{Number of Peroxy Linkages} = \frac{x_{\text{calc}} - x_{\text{max}}}{2}
$$

*(Where $x_{\text{calc}}$ is calculated assuming all oxygens are $-2$)*

*Verification with $\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8$:*
- $x_{\text{calc}} = +7$ per Sulphur, so total calculated valence above max $= 2 \times (7 - 6) = 2$.
- $N_{\text{peroxy}} = 2$ oxygen atoms.
- Number of peroxy linkages $= \frac{2}{2} = \mathbf{1}$.

*Verification with $\text{CrO}_5$:*
- $x_{\text{calc}} = +10$; $x_{\text{max}} = +6$.
- $N_{\text{peroxy}} = 10 - 6 = 4$ oxygen atoms ($2$ peroxy pairs).
- Remaining oxo oxygens $= 5 - 4 = 1$ ($\text{Cr}=\text{O}$).
- Structural formula: $\text{CrO}(\text{O}_2)_2 \implies \text{O.N.} = +6$.

# Types of Redox Reactions: Combination, Decomposition, Displacement, and Disproportionation

# Chapter: Redox Reactions (Class 11 Chemistry)

## Topic: Types of Redox Reactions — Combination, Decomposition, Displacement, and Disproportionation

***

### Master Pedagogical Overview & Star Priority Matrix

| Subtopic / Reaction Archetype | NCERT / Board Priority | Conceptual Core | Key Exam Angle |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Combination Redox Reactions** | ⭐⭐⭐ | $A + B \rightarrow C$; at least one reactant in elemental form | Distinguishing redox combination from non-redox Lewis acid-base adduct formation |
| **2. Decomposition Redox Reactions** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | $AB \rightarrow A + B$; breakdown leading to oxidation state changes | Intra-molecular electron transfer; non-redox thermal decompositions (e.g., $\text{CaCO}_3$) |
| **3. Displacement Reactions (Metal)** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | $X + YZ \rightarrow XZ + Y$; Standard Reduction Potential ($E^\circ$) driven | Electrochemical series ranking, competitive electron transfer |
| **4. Displacement Reactions (Non-Metal)** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Halogen-halogen and proton-reducing metal displacements | Electronegativity / standard reduction potential gradients of halogens |
| **5. Disproportionation Reactions** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Single element in intermediate oxidation state undergoes simultaneous oxidation & reduction | Variable oxidation state rules, halogen disproportionation in alkaline media, $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, phosphorus, sulfur |
| **6. Comproportionation (Synproportionation)** | ⭐⭐⭐ | Reverse of disproportionation; two different oxidation states collapse into an intermediate state | Identifying the non-disproportionation pathway |

***

## 1. Fundamental Conceptual Framework

A redox process involves electron transfer between reacting species, resulting in simultaneous changes in oxidation numbers. A reaction is classified as a redox reaction **if and only if** at least one element undergoes an increase in oxidation number (oxidation) and another element undergoes a decrease in oxidation number (reduction).

$$
\Delta \text{O.N.}_{\text{system}} = \sum (\Delta \text{O.N.}_{\text{oxidized species}}) + \sum (\Delta \text{O.N.}_{\text{reduced species}}) = 0
$$

NCERT categorizes redox processes into four primary mechanistic classes based on structural rearrangements of atoms and oxidation number shifts:
1. **Combination Reactions**
2. **Decomposition Reactions**
3. **Displacement Reactions** (Metal and Non-Metal)
4. **Disproportionation Reactions** (a specialized class of intra-elemental electron transfer)

***

## 2. Combination Redox Reactions

### 2.1 Theoretical Foundations & Definition
> **NCERT Definition:** A combination reaction involves the union of two chemical entities to synthesize a single final compound:
> 

$$
A + B \longrightarrow C
$$

> For a combination reaction to be categorized as a **redox reaction**, **at least one of the reactants ($A$ or $B$) must be in the elemental state (oxidation number = $0$)**.

If both reactants are compounds already existing in their respective stable oxidation states and no transfer of electrons occurs, the combination is **non-redox**.

### 2.2 Pedagogical Derivation & Oxidation Number Tracking

#### Case 1: Combustion of Carbon (Both Reactants Elemental)

$$
\overset{0}{\text{C}}(\text{s}) + \overset{0}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \overset{+4}{\text{C}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g})
$$

- Oxidation step: $\overset{0}{\text{C}} \longrightarrow \overset{+4}{\text{C}} + 4\text{e}^-$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = +4$; Carbon acts as the **reducing agent**).
- Reduction step: $\overset{0}{\text{O}}_2 + 4\text{e}^- \longrightarrow 2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = -2 \text{ per oxygen atom}$; Dioxygen acts as the **oxidizing agent**).
- Conclusion: Genuine Combination Redox Reaction.

#### Case 2: Reaction with One Elemental Reactant and One Compound

$$
\overset{+2}{\text{C}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{g}) + \frac{1}{2}\overset{0}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \overset{+4}{\text{C}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g})
$$

- Carbon changes from $+2$ to $+4$ (Oxidation).
- Molecular oxygen changes from $0$ to $-2$ (Reduction).
- Oxygen in $\text{CO}$ remains spectator in terms of oxidation state ($-2 \rightarrow -2$).

#### Case 3: Non-Redox Combination (Examiner Trap)

$$
\overset{+2}{\text{Ca}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{s}) + \overset{+4}{\text{S}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \overset{+2}{\text{Ca}}\overset{+4}{\text{S}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3(\text{s})
$$

- Check oxidation numbers:
  - $\text{Ca}: +2 \longrightarrow +2$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = 0$)
  - $\text{S}: +4 \longrightarrow +4$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = 0$)
  - $\text{O}: -2 \longrightarrow -2$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = 0$)
- Conclusion: This is an acid-base (Lewis/Lux-Flood) combination, **NOT a redox reaction**.

***

## 3. Decomposition Redox Reactions

### 3.1 Theoretical Foundations & Definition
> **NCERT Definition:** A decomposition reaction involves the breakdown of a single compound into two or more components:
> 

$$
C \longrightarrow A + B
$$

> For a decomposition reaction to be a **redox reaction**, **at least one of the products must be in the elemental state**.

Not all decomposition reactions are redox reactions. Thermal dissociation can occur with conservation of oxidation numbers across all atomic centers.

### 3.2 Mechanisms & Exemplar Reactions

#### Case 1: Decomposition Yielding Two Elemental Products

$$
\overset{+1}{2\text{H}_2}\overset{-1}{\text{O}_2}(\text{l}) \xrightarrow{h\nu \text{ or } \text{MnO}_2} \overset{+1}{2\text{H}_2}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{l}) + \overset{0}{\text{O}_2}(\text{g})
$$

*(Note: As shown later, this is also a disproportionation reaction).*

Thermal breakdown of alkali metal hydrides:

$$
\overset{+1}{2\text{Na}}\overset{-1}{\text{H}}(\text{s}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \overset{0}{2\text{Na}}(\text{s}) + \overset{0}{\text{H}_2}(\text{g})
$$

- $\text{Na}^+ + \text{e}^- \longrightarrow \text{Na}^0$ (Reduction: $+1 \rightarrow 0$)
- $2\text{H}^- \longrightarrow \text{H}_2^0 + 2\text{e}^-$ (Oxidation: $-1 \rightarrow 0$)

#### Case 2: Classical Laboratory Preparation of Dioxygen

$$
\overset{+1}{2\text{K}}\overset{+5}{\text{Cl}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3(\text{s}) \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{MnO}_2} \overset{+1}{2\text{K}}\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}(\text{s}) + 3\overset{0}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g})
$$

- Potassium ($\text{K}$): $+1 \longrightarrow +1$ (Spectator)
- Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$): $+5 \longrightarrow -1$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = -6$, Reduction: Gain of $6\text{e}^-$ per $\text{Cl}$ atom)
- Oxygen ($\text{O}$): $-2 \longrightarrow 0$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = +2$, Oxidation: Loss of $2\text{e}^-$ per $\text{O}$ atom; across $6$ atoms = $12\text{e}^-$ transferred)

$$
\boxed{\text{Total Electrons Transferred, } n = 12 \text{ per formula unit reaction (2 moles of } \text{KClO}_3\text{)}}
$$

#### Case 3: Non-Redox Decomposition (High-Frequency Examiner Trap)

$$
\overset{+2}{\text{Ca}}\overset{+4}{\text{C}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3(\text{s}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \overset{+2}{\text{Ca}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{s}) + \overset{+4}{\text{C}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g})
$$

- $\text{Ca}: +2 \longrightarrow +2$
- $\text{C}: +4 \longrightarrow +4$
- $\text{O}: -2 \longrightarrow -2$
- **Result:** No electron transfer occurs. This is purely a thermal, non-redox decomposition.

***

## 4. Displacement Redox Reactions

A displacement reaction is represented as:

$$
X + YZ \longrightarrow XZ + Y
$$

where an atom or ion in a compound is replaced by an atom or ion of another element. These divide strictly into **Metal Displacement** and **Non-Metal Displacement**.

```
                           Displacement Redox Reactions
                                        |
                 +----------------------+----------------------+
                 |                                             |
       Metal Displacement                            Non-Metal Displacement
  (Driven by standard reduction                (Driven by relative oxidizing
   potential differences: E° values)            power / halogen electronegativity)
```

### 4.1 Metal Displacement Reactions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A metal in elemental form will displace another metal from its aqueous salt solution **if and only if** the displacing metal is thermodynamically a stronger reducing agent (more negative standard reduction potential, $E^\circ_{\text{red}}$) than the metal being displaced.

$$
\text{Thermodynamic Feasibility Condition: } \Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}} < 0 \implies E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0
$$

#### The Canonical $\text{Zn}-\text{Cu}^{2+}$ System

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Zn}}(\text{s}) + \overset{+2}{\text{Cu}}\overset{+6}{\text{S}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_4(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \overset{+2}{\text{Zn}}\overset{+6}{\text{S}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_4(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{Cu}}(\text{s})
$$

- Net ionic equation:
  

$$
\text{Zn}(\text{s}) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(\text{aq}) + \text{Cu}(\text{s})
$$

- Half-cell potentials at $298\text{ K}$:
  

$$
\text{Zn}^{2+}(\text{aq}) + 2\text{e}^- \longrightarrow \text{Zn}(\text{s}) \quad E^\circ = -0.76\text{ V}
$$

  

$$
\text{Cu}^{2+}(\text{aq}) + 2\text{e}^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(\text{s}) \quad E^\circ = +0.34\text{ V}
$$

- Cell electromotive force:
  

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = (+0.34\text{ V}) - (-0.76\text{ V}) = +1.10\text{ V} > 0
$$

- $\text{Zn}$ reduces $\text{Cu}^{2+}$: Zinc is the reducing agent; $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is the oxidizing agent.

#### Pyrometallurgical Application (Thermite Process)

$$
\overset{+3}{\text{Cr}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3(\text{s}) + 2\overset{0}{\text{Al}}(\text{s}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \overset{+3}{\text{Al}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3(\text{s}) + 2\overset{0}{\text{Cr}}(\text{s})
$$

$$
\overset{+3}{\text{Fe}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3(\text{s}) + 2\overset{0}{\text{Al}}(\text{s}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \overset{+3}{\text{Al}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3(\text{s}) + 2\overset{0}{\text{Fe}}(\text{l})
$$

- Aluminum reduces oxides of less electropositive metals due to the high thermodynamic stability ($\Delta_{\text{f}}G^\circ \ll 0$) of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$.

***

### 4.2 Non-Metal Displacement Reactions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Non-metal displacement reactions almost always involve **hydrogen displacement** or **halogen displacement**.

#### 4.2.1 Hydrogen Displacement
1. **Group 1 and Group 2 metals (very active, highly negative $E^\circ$) displace $\text{H}_2$ from cold water:**
   

$$
2\overset{0}{\text{Na}}(\text{s}) + 2\overset{+1}{\text{H}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{l}) \longrightarrow 2\overset{+1}{\text{Na}}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}\overset{+1}{\text{H}}(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{H}}_2(\text{g})
$$

   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Ca}}(\text{s}) + 2\overset{+1}{\text{H}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{l}) \longrightarrow \overset{+2}{\text{Ca}}(\overset{-2}{\text{O}}\overset{+1}{\text{H}})_2(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{H}}_2(\text{g})
$$

2. **Less active metals (e.g., $\text{Mg}, \text{Fe}$) require steam:**
   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Mg}}(\text{s}) + 2\overset{+1}{\text{H}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{g, steam}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \overset{+2}{\text{Mg}}(\overset{-2}{\text{O}}\overset{+1}{\text{H}})_2(\text{s}) + \overset{0}{\text{H}}_2(\text{g})
$$

   

$$
3\overset{0}{\text{Fe}}(\text{s}) + 4\overset{+1}{\text{H}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{g, steam}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \overset{+8/3}{\text{Fe}_3}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_4(\text{s}) + 4\overset{0}{\text{H}}_2(\text{g})
$$

3. **Metals with $E^\circ < 0\text{ V}$ displace $\text{H}_2$ from dilute non-oxidizing acids ($\text{HCl}, \text{dil. } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$):**
   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Zn}}(\text{s}) + 2\overset{+1}{\text{H}}\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \overset{+2}{\text{Zn}}\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}_2(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{H}}_2(\text{g})
$$

   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Fe}}(\text{s}) + 2\overset{+1}{\text{H}}\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \overset{+2}{\text{Fe}}\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}_2(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{H}}_2(\text{g})
$$

> **Critical NCERT Note:** Metals like $\text{Cu}, \text{Ag}, \text{Au}, \text{Pt}$ have positive standard reduction potentials ($E^\circ_{\text{M}^{n+}/\text{M}} > 0\text{ V}$) and **cannot** displace hydrogen from dilute non-oxidizing acids.

#### 4.2.2 Halogen Displacement Series
The oxidizing power of Group 17 elements decreases down the group:

$$
\boxed{\text{F}_2 > \text{Cl}_2 > \text{Br}_2 > \text{I}_2}
$$

Therefore, a halogen with higher standard reduction potential displaces a halide ion of a halogen lower down in the group from its aqueous solution:

1. **Chlorine displacing Bromide and Iodide:**
   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Cl}}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{K}\overset{-1}{\text{Br}}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{K}\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{Br}}_2(\text{aq})
$$

   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Cl}}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{K}\overset{-1}{\text{I}}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{K}\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{I}}_2(\text{s})
$$

2. **Bromine displacing Iodide:**
   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Br}}_2(\text{aq}) + 2\text{K}\overset{-1}{\text{I}}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{K}\overset{-1}{\text{Br}}(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{I}}_2(\text{s})
$$

3. **The Unique Case of Fluorine ($\text{F}_2$):**
   Fluorine is such a powerful oxidizing agent ($E^\circ_{\text{F}_2/\text{F}^-} = +2.87\text{ V}$) that it oxidizes the solvent water itself into oxygen gas, precluding straightforward halide displacement in aqueous solution:
   

$$
2\overset{0}{\text{F}}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{H}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{l}) \longrightarrow 4\text{H}\overset{-1}{\text{F}}(\text{aq}) + \overset{0}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g})
$$

***

## 5. Disproportionation Reactions ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

### 5.1 Definition & Fundamental Requirement
> **NCERT Definition:** A reaction in which an element in one particular oxidation state is simultaneously oxidized and reduced is called a **disproportionation reaction**.
>
> **Necessary Condition:** The reacting species must contain an element capable of existing in **at least three distinct oxidation states**:
> 1. An intermediate oxidation state (possessed by the reactant).
> 2. A higher oxidation state (formed in the oxidized product).
> 3. A lower oxidation state (formed in the reduced product).

```
                      Higher Oxidation State (+Y)  [Oxidation Product]
                     ^
                    /  (Loss of e-)
  Intermediate State (+N)
                    \  (Gain of e-)
                     v
                      Lower Oxidation State (-Z or 0) [Reduction Product]
```

### 5.2 Classic Exemplar Reactions & Oxidation State Derivations

#### 1. Hydrogen Peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$)

$$
\overset{+1}{2\text{H}_2}\overset{-1}{\text{O}}_2(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \overset{+1}{2\text{H}_2}\overset{-2}{\text{O}}(\text{l}) + \overset{0}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g})
$$

- Oxygen in $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ is in the **$-1$** oxidation state.
- Reduced to $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($\text{O.N.} = -2$, gain of $1\text{e}^-$ per atom).
- Oxidized to $\text{O}_2$ ($\text{O.N.} = 0$, loss of $1\text{e}^-$ per atom).

#### 2. Phosphorus ($\text{P}_4$) in Alkaline Medium

$$
\overset{0}{\text{P}}_4(\text{s}) + 3\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \longrightarrow \overset{-3}{\text{P}}\text{H}_3(\text{g}) + 3\text{H}_2\overset{+1}{\text{P}}\text{O}_2^-(\text{aq})
$$

- Reactant: White phosphorus ($\text{P}_4$), $\text{O.N.} = 0$.
- Reduced product: Phosphine ($\text{PH}_3$), $\text{O.N.} = -3$ ($\Delta\text{O.N.} = -3$).
- Oxidized product: Hypophosphite ion ($\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$):
  

$$
2(+1) + x + 2(-2) = -1 \implies x = +1 \quad (\Delta\text{O.N.} = +1)
$$

- Hence, $\text{P}$ disproportionates from $0$ to $-3$ and $+1$.

#### 3. Sulfur ($\text{S}_8$) in Alkaline Medium

$$
\overset{0}{\text{S}}_8(\text{s}) + 12\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 4\overset{-2}{\text{S}}^{2-}(\text{aq}) + 2\overset{+2}{\text{S}}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}}_3^{2-}(\text{aq}) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

- Reactant: Octaatomic sulfur ($\text{S}_8$), $\text{O.N.} = 0$.
- Reduced product: Sulfide ($\text{S}^{2-}$), $\text{O.N.} = -2$.
- Oxidized product: Thiosulfate ($\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-}$):
  

$$
2x + 3(-2) = -2 \implies 2x = +4 \implies x = +2
$$

- Hence, $\text{S}$ disproportionates from $0$ to $-2$ and $+2$.

#### 4. Halogens ($\text{Cl}_2, \text{Br}_2, \text{I}_2$) in Alkaline Medium
The outcome depends strictly on temperature and hydroxide concentration:

* **Cold and Dilute Alkali ($288 - 298\text{ K}$):**
  

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Cl}}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}^-(\text{aq}) + \overset{+1}{\text{Cl}}\text{O}^-(\text{aq}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

  - Chlorine ($0$) disproportionates to Chloride ($-1$) and Hypochlorite ($+1$).

* **Hot and Concentrated Alkali ($\sim 373\text{ K}$):**
  

$$
3\overset{0}{\text{Cl}}_2(\text{g}) + 6\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 5\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}^-(\text{aq}) + \overset{+5}{\text{Cl}}\text{O}_3^-(\text{aq}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

  - Chlorine ($0$) disproportionates to Chloride ($-1$) and Chlorate ($+5$).

### 5.3 The Fluorine Exception (Crucial NCERT Key Point)
Fluorine **cannot** undergo disproportionation reactions.

$$
\text{Reason: Fluorine is the most electronegative element and exhibits only } 0 \text{ and } -1 \text{ oxidation states.}
$$

Since it lacks accessible $d$-orbitals and has the highest electronegativity of all elements, it cannot exhibit positive oxidation states ($+1, +3, +5, +7$). When $\text{F}_2$ reacts with alkali, it behaves as an ordinary oxidizing agent:

$$
2\overset{0}{\text{F}}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\overset{-1}{\text{F}}^-(\text{aq}) + \overset{+2}{\text{O}}\text{F}_2(\text{g}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \quad (\text{Cold, dilute})
$$

$$
\overset{0}{\text{F}}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\overset{-1}{\text{F}}^-(\text{aq}) + \frac{1}{2}\overset{0}{\text{O}}_2(\text{g}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \quad (\text{Hot, concentrated})
$$

In both cases, Fluorine is exclusively **reduced** from $0$ to $-1$. It is oxygen that is oxidized (from $-2$ in $\text{OH}^-$ to $+2$ in $\text{OF}_2$ or $0$ in $\text{O}_2$).

***

## 6. Comproportionation (Synproportionation) ⭐⭐⭐

> **Definition:** The exact reverse of disproportionation. Two species containing the same element in **different** oxidation states react to form a product in which the element is in a **single intermediate** oxidation state.

$$
\overset{\text{State } A}{X^{a+}} + \overset{\text{State } B}{X^{b+}} \longrightarrow \overset{\text{Intermediate}}{X^{c+}} \quad (\text{where } a > c > b \text{ or } a < c < b)
$$

### Exemplar Comproportionation Reactions:
1. **Volcanic gas reaction between $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ and $\text{SO}_2$:**
   

$$
2\text{H}_2\overset{-2}{\text{S}}(\text{g}) + \overset{+4}{\text{S}}\text{O}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow 3\overset{0}{\text{S}}(\text{s}) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

   - $\text{S}^{-2}$ is oxidized to $\text{S}^0$.
   - $\text{S}^{+4}$ is reduced to $\text{S}^0$.

2. **Iodate-Iodide reaction in acidic solution:**
   

$$
\overset{+5}{\text{I}}\text{O}_3^-(\text{aq}) + 5\overset{-1}{\text{I}}^-(\text{aq}) + 6\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 3\overset{0}{\text{I}}_2(\text{s}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

   - $\text{I}(+5)$ and $\text{I}(-1)$ collapse into elemental $\text{I}(0)$.

3. **Manganese synproportionation:**
   

$$
\overset{0}{\text{Mn}}(\text{s}) + \overset{+4}{\text{Mn}}\text{O}_2(\text{s}) + 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\overset{+2}{\text{Mn}}\text{SO}_4(\text{aq}) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

***

## 7. High-Yield Solved Exemplars & Exam Archetypes

### Problem 1 (NCERT Exemplar Class 11)
**Identify the type of redox reaction for each of the following transformations:**
1. $2\text{CuSO}_4(\text{aq}) + 4\text{KI}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{CuI}(\text{s}) + \text{I}_2(\text{s}) + 2\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4(\text{aq})$
2. $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_2(\text{s}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{N}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})$
3. $\text{P}_4(\text{s}) + 3\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \longrightarrow \text{PH}_3(\text{g}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-(\text{aq})$
4. $2\text{Pb}(\text{NO}_3)_2(\text{s}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{PbO}(\text{s}) + 4\text{NO}_2(\text{g}) + \text{O}_2(\text{g})$

#### Solution Step-by-Step:
1. **$2\text{CuSO}_4 + 4\text{KI} \longrightarrow 2\text{CuI} + \text{I}_2 + 2\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$**
   - Copper: $\text{In }\text{CuSO}_4$, $\text{O.N.} = +2$; in $\text{CuI}$, $\text{O.N.} = +1$ (Reduction).
   - Iodine: $\text{In }\text{KI}$, $\text{O.N.} = -1$; in $\text{I}_2$, $\text{O.N.} = 0$ (Oxidation); in $\text{CuI}$, $\text{O.N.} = -1$ (Spectator).
   - *Classification:* **Redox reaction involving non-metal oxidation and metal reduction** (often classified under general redox / displacement-elimination).

2. **$\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{N}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$**
   - In ammonium nitrite, nitrogen exists in two distinct ions:
     - In $\text{NH}_4^+$: $x + 4(+1) = +1 \implies x = -3$
     - In $\text{NO}_2^-$: $y + 2(-2) = -1 \implies y = +3$
   - Product: $\text{N}_2$ ($\text{O.N.} = 0$).
   - $\text{N}(-3) \longrightarrow \text{N}(0)$ (Oxidation)
   - $\text{N}(+3) \longrightarrow \text{N}(0)$ (Reduction)
   - *Classification:* **Intramolecular Comproportionation / Decomposition Redox Reaction**.

3. **$\text{P}_4 + 3\text{OH}^- + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{PH}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$**
   - $\text{P}$ goes from $0 \longrightarrow -3$ in $\text{PH}_3$ (Reduction) and $0 \longrightarrow +1$ in $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$ (Oxidation).
   - *Classification:* **Disproportionation Reaction**.

4. **$2\text{Pb}(\text{NO}_3)_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{PbO} + 4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2$**
   - $\text{Pb}: +2 \longrightarrow +2$ (Spectator)
   - $\text{N}: +5 \ (\text{in }\text{NO}_3^-) \longrightarrow +4 \ (\text{in }\text{NO}_2)$ (Reduction)
   - $\text{O}: -2 \ (\text{in nitrate}) \longrightarrow 0 \ (\text{in }\text{O}_2)$ (Oxidation)
   - *Classification:* **Decomposition Redox Reaction**.

***

### Problem 2 (JEE/NEET Competitive Trap Archetype)
**Which of the following compounds CANNOT undergo disproportionation? Explain why.**

$$
\text{ClO}^-, \ \text{ClO}_2^-, \ \text{ClO}_3^-, \ \text{ClO}_4^-
$$

#### Solution Step-by-Step:
1. Determine the oxidation state of Chlorine in each oxoanion:
   - $\text{ClO}^-$: $x - 2 = -1 \implies x = +1$
   - $\text{ClO}_2^-$: $x - 4 = -1 \implies x = +3$
   - $\text{ClO}_3^-$: $x - 6 = -1 \implies x = +5$
   - $\text{ClO}_4^-$: $x - 8 = -1 \implies x = +7$

2. Recall that chlorine belongs to Group 17 (valence configuration $3s^2 3p^5$). The range of permissible oxidation numbers for chlorine is:
   

$$
\text{Minimum O.N.} = -1, \quad \text{Maximum O.N.} = +7
$$

3. For a species to disproportionate, the element must be able to:
   - **Increase** its oxidation number (must not be at maximum O.N.).
   - **Decrease** its oxidation number (must not be at minimum O.N.).

4. In $\text{ClO}_4^-$, Chlorine is at its absolute maximum oxidation state ($+7$). It cannot lose any more electrons to reach a higher state. Therefore, it cannot be oxidized further.

$$
\boxed{\text{ClO}_4^- \text{ cannot undergo disproportionation.}}
$$

## 8. Examiner Traps & Common Pedagogical Pitfalls

### Trap 1: Confusing Non-Redox Decomposition with Redox Decomposition
* **Trap:** Calling $\text{CaCO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2$ a redox reaction because it involves heat and decomposition.
* **Fix:** Always calculate oxidation states before labeling. If all atoms retain their oxidation state, it is non-redox.

### Trap 2: Fluorine in Basic Solutions
* **Trap:** Expecting $\text{F}_2 + 2\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{NaF} + \text{NaOF} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ analogously to $\text{Cl}_2$.
* **Fix:** $\text{F}$ cannot have positive oxidation states (no $\text{OF}^-$ hypofluorite containing $\text{F}^+$). The reaction yields oxygen difluoride ($\text{OF}_2$), where oxygen is $+2$ and fluorine is $-1$. This is **not** disproportionation.

### Trap 3: Ammonium Salt Decompositions
* **$\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{N}_2\text{O} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$**: Nitrogen is in $-3$ and $+5$ states, transforming into $+1$ in $\text{N}_2\text{O}$. This is **comproportionation**, not disproportionation.
* **$\text{NH}_4\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{NH}_3 + \text{HCl}$**: Nitrogen remains $-3$, Hydrogen $+1$, Chlorine $-1$. This is **non-redox thermal dissociation**.

### Trap 4: Fractional Oxidation States in Displacement/Decomposition
* In reactions such as $3\text{Fe} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 4\text{H}_2$, students write $\text{Fe}$ as $+8/3$ and get confused about electron bookkeeping.
* Remember: $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$ is a mixed oxide ($\text{FeO} \cdot \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) consisting of one $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ion and two $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ions.

### 1. The "Intermediate State" Check for Disproportionation
```
Can it disproportionate?
-----------------------------------------------------------
Oxidation State = Maximum?  --> NO (Can only reduce)
Oxidation State = Minimum?  --> NO (Can only oxidize)
Oxidation State = Intermediate? --> YES (Eligible candidate)
```

  - Nitrogen: Min $-3$, Max $+5$ ($\text{HNO}_3$ cannot disproportionate; $\text{NO}_2$ ($+4$) can).

  - Manganese: Min $0$, Max $+7$ ($\text{MnO}_4^-$ cannot; $\text{MnO}_4^{2-}$ ($+6$) can).

# Balancing Redox Reactions: Oxidation Number Method and Half-Reaction (Ion-Electron) Method

# Class 11 Chemistry — Lecture Note: Redox Reactions

## 1. Pedagogical Theory & Foundational Concepts

### 1.1 Fundamental Definitions and Physical Laws
A redox (reduction-oxidation) reaction involves the transfer of electrons from an electron donor (reducing agent) to an electron acceptor (oxidizing agent).

* **Oxidation Number (O.N.)**: The residual electrical charge that an atom appears to have when all bonded electrons are counted as belonging completely to the more electronegative atom in each bond.
* **Oxidation**: An increase in the oxidation number of an element corresponding to a loss of electrons.
* **Reduction**: A decrease in the oxidation number of an element corresponding to a gain of electrons.
* **Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant)**: A substance containing an element whose oxidation number decreases (it gains electrons and undergoes reduction).
* **Reducing Agent (Reductant)**: A substance containing an element whose oxidation number increases (it loses electrons and undergoes oxidation).
* **Disproportionation Reaction**: A redox reaction in which atoms of a single element in an intermediate oxidation state are simultaneously oxidized and reduced.
* **Law of Conservation of Mass**: In any closed chemical system, the mass of reactants must equal the mass of products. The total number of atoms of each element must remain constant throughout the reaction.
* **Law of Conservation of Charge**: The algebraic sum of formal charges on the reactant side must equal the algebraic sum of formal charges on the product side.

## 2. Core Methodologies: Detailed Step-by-Step Mechanisms

***

### 2.1 The Oxidation Number Method

#### Algorithmic Protocol

1. **Step 1: Write the Skeletal Equation**  
   Formulate the unbalanced chemical equation containing all participating species (reactants and products).

2. **Step 2: Assign Oxidation Numbers**  
   Assign the oxidation state to every atom in the equation using IUPAC/NCERT assignment rules. Identify which elements undergo a change in oxidation state.

3. **Step 3: Calculate the Net Increase and Decrease in Oxidation Number**  
   * Determine the change in oxidation number **per atom**.
   * Multiply this change by the total number of such atoms present in the formula unit undergoing the change.
   * Total Increase in $\text{O.N.} = \Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{ox}} \times n_1$
   * Total Decrease in $\text{O.N.} = |\Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{red}}| \times n_2$

4. **Step 4: Equalize Total Increase and Total Decrease in Oxidation Number**  
   Determine the lowest common multiple (LCM) of the net increase and net decrease. Multiply the respective oxidizing and reducing formula units by suitable integers so that:
   

$$
\sum \Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{increase}} = \sum |\Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{decrease}}|
$$

5. **Step 5: Balance All Atoms Other than Hydrogen and Oxygen**  
   Apply standard stoichiometric inspection to balance remaining elements (e.g., metals, halogens, sulfur, nitrogen).

6. **Step 6: Balance Oxygen and Hydrogen Atoms**  
   * **In Acidic Medium**:
     * Balance $\text{O}$ atoms by adding $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules to the oxygen-deficient side:
       

$$
\text{Deficient side} + n\,\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

     * Balance $\text{H}$ atoms by adding $\text{H}^+$ ions to the hydrogen-deficient side:
       

$$
\text{Deficient side} + m\,\text{H}^+
$$

   * **In Basic Medium**:
     * Balance charge first, or balance as in acidic medium and neutralize:
     * Add equal numbers of $\text{OH}^-$ ions to **both sides** corresponding to the number of $\text{H}^+$ ions present.
     * Combine $\text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$, then simplify water molecules appearing on both sides.

7. **Step 7: Final Charge and Mass Audit**  
   Verify that atom counts for every element and total electrical charges on both sides are identical.

***

### 2.2 The Half-Reaction (Ion-Electron) Method

#### Algorithmic Protocol

1. **Step 1: Convert to Net Ionic Form**  
   Eliminate all spectator ions (ions whose oxidation states do not change and that do not participate in electron transfer).

2. **Step 2: Separate into Oxidation and Reduction Half-Reactions**  
   Identify the oxidant and reductant; decouple into two separate half-cells:
   

$$
\text{Oxidation Half-Reaction: } \text{Red}_1 \rightarrow \text{Ox}_1 + n_1\,e^-
$$

   

$$
\text{Reduction Half-Reaction: } \text{Ox}_2 + n_2\,e^- \rightarrow \text{Red}_2
$$

3. **Step 3: Balance Elements Undergoing Oxidation/Reduction**  
   Balance all atoms *other* than $\text{O}$ and $\text{H}$ using stoichiometric coefficients.

4. **Step 4: Balance Oxygen Atoms**  
   Add one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecule for each deficit of an oxygen atom:
   

$$
\text{Side deficient in O} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

5. **Step 5: Balance Hydrogen Atoms**  
   * **In Acidic Medium**:
     Add one $\text{H}^+$ ion for each deficit of a hydrogen atom:
     

$$
\text{Side deficient in H} + \text{H}^+
$$

   * **In Basic Medium**:
     * Method A (Direct): For each hydrogen deficit, add one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecule to the deficient side and an equal number of $\text{OH}^-$ ions to the opposite side.
     * Method B (Acidic-to-Basic Conversion): Balance initially with $\text{H}^+$, then add identical stoichiometric amounts of $\text{OH}^-$ to **both sides** to neutralize all $\text{H}^+$ into $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

6. **Step 6: Balance Charge Using Electrons ($e^-$)**  
   Compute the net charge on both sides of each half-reaction. Add electrons ($e^-$) to the more positive side to equalize the net electrical charge:
   

$$
Q_{\text{left}} + z_e(-1) = Q_{\text{right}}
$$

7. **Step 7: Equalize and Cancel Electrons**  
   Multiply the oxidation half-reaction by integer $k_1$ and the reduction half-reaction by integer $k_2$ such that:
   

$$
k_1 \times n_1 = k_2 \times n_2 = \text{LCM}(n_1, n_2)
$$

8. **Step 8: Sum Half-Reactions and Cancel Redundant Species**  
   Add the two balanced half-reactions. The electrons must cancel out completely. Simplify common species ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{H}^+$, $\text{OH}^-$) on both sides.

9. **Step 9: Final Verification**  
   Audit both mass (individual atomic counts) and total charge:
   

$$
\sum Z_{\text{reactants}} = \sum Z_{\text{products}}
$$

## 3. High-Yield Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

***

### Example 1: Acidic Medium — Dichromate and Iron(II) (NCERT Textbook Core Archetype)
**Problem**: Balance the following reaction in an acidic aqueous medium via the **Half-Reaction (Ion-Electron) Method**:

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(\text{aq}) + \text{Fe}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \rightarrow \text{Cr}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + \text{Fe}^{3+}(\text{aq})
$$

#### Step-by-Step Derivation:

* **Step 1: Identify Oxidation States & Decouple into Half-Reactions**
  * In $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$: $2x + 7(-2) = -2 \implies 2x = +12 \implies x = +6$ (Chromium is $+6$)
  * In $\text{Cr}^{3+}$: Chromium is $+3$
  * In $\text{Fe}^{2+}$: Iron is $+2$; in $\text{Fe}^{3+}$: Iron is $+3$
  * **Oxidation Half-Reaction**:
    

$$
\text{Fe}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}(\text{aq})
$$

  * **Reduction Half-Reaction**:
    

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(\text{aq}) \rightarrow \text{Cr}^{3+}(\text{aq})
$$

* **Step 2: Balance Atoms Other Than $\text{O}$ and $\text{H}$**
  * Oxidation: $\text{Fe}$ is balanced (1 atom on each side).
  * Reduction: 2 Chromium atoms on left; multiply $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ by 2:
    

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(\text{aq}) \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}(\text{aq})
$$

* **Step 3: Balance Oxygen Atoms with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$**
  * Oxidation: No oxygen involved.
  * Reduction: Left has 7 oxygen atoms; add $7\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to the right:
    

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(\text{aq}) \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

* **Step 4: Balance Hydrogen Atoms with $\text{H}^+$**
  * Reduction: Right side has $14\text{H}$; add $14\text{H}^+$ to the left side:
    

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(\text{aq}) + 14\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})
$$

* **Step 5: Balance Charge with Electrons ($e^-$)**
  * Oxidation Half:
    

$$
\text{Left Charge} = +2, \quad \text{Right Charge} = +3
$$

    Add $1e^-$ to the right:
    

$$
\text{Fe}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + e^- \quad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}
$$

  * Reduction Half:
    

$$
\text{Left Charge} = (-2) + 14(+1) = +12
$$

    

$$
\text{Right Charge} = 2(+3) + 7(0) = +6
$$

    Deficit on right $= +6 - (+12) = -6$; add $6e^-$ to the left:
    

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(\text{aq}) + 14\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + 6e^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \quad \text{--- (Eq. 2)}
$$

* **Step 6: Equalize Electron Transfer and Add**
  Multiply $(\text{Eq. 1})$ by 6 and add to $(\text{Eq. 2})$:
  

$$
6 \times [\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + e^-]
$$

  

$$
\implies 6\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow 6\text{Fe}^{3+} + 6e^-
$$

  Summing both equations:
  

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6e^- + 6\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O} + 6\text{Fe}^{3+} + 6e^-
$$

* **Final Balanced Net Ionic Equation**:
  

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(\text{aq}) + 6\text{Fe}^{2+}(\text{aq}) + 14\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + 6\text{Fe}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l})}
$$

* **Mass and Charge Check**:
  * Atoms: $\text{Cr}: 2 = 2$; $\text{Fe}: 6 = 6$; $\text{O}: 7 = 7$; $\text{H}: 14 = 14$.
  * Charge: Left $= (-2) + 6(+2) + 14(+1) = +24$; Right $= 2(+3) + 6(+3) + 0 = +24$. (Balanced)

***

### Example 2: Basic Medium — Permanganate and Iodide (NCERT Core Archetype)
**Problem**: Balance the reaction of aqueous potassium permanganate with potassium iodide in basic medium by the **Oxidation Number Method**:

$$
\text{MnO}_4^-(\text{aq}) + \text{I}^-(\text{aq}) \rightarrow \text{MnO}_2(\text{s}) + \text{IO}_3^-(\text{aq}) \quad [\text{Basic Medium}]
$$

#### Step-by-Step Derivation:

* **Step 1: Assign Oxidation Numbers to Identify Changes**
  * $\text{Mn}$ in $\text{MnO}_4^-$: $x + 4(-2) = -1 \implies x = +7$
  * $\text{Mn}$ in $\text{MnO}_2$: $x + 2(-2) = 0 \implies x = +4$
    

$$
\Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{Mn}} = +4 - (+7) = -3 \quad (\text{Reduction, Gain of } 3e^-)
$$

  * $\text{I}$ in $\text{I}^-$: $-1$
  * $\text{I}$ in $\text{IO}_3^-$: $y + 3(-2) = -1 \implies y = +5$
    

$$
\Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{I}} = +5 - (-1) = +6 \quad (\text{Oxidation, Loss of } 6e^-)
$$

* **Step 2: Equalize the Oxidation Number Changes**
  * Total decrease per $\text{MnO}_4^-$ unit $= 3$
  * Total increase per $\text{I}^-$ unit $= 6$
  * $\text{LCM}(3, 6) = 6$
  * Multiply $\text{MnO}_4^-$ and $\text{MnO}_2$ by 2, and $\text{I}^-$ and $\text{IO}_3^-$ by 1:
    

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^-(\text{aq}) + \text{I}^-(\text{aq}) \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2(\text{s}) + \text{IO}_3^-(\text{aq})
$$

* **Step 3: Balance Charges in Basic Medium**
  * Compute charge on each side:
    

$$
\text{Left side: } 2(-1) + 1(-1) = -3
$$

    

$$
\text{Right side: } 2(0) + 1(-1) = -1
$$

  * To equalize charge in **basic medium**, add $\text{OH}^-$ to the more positive side.
  * Right side is $-1$, left side is $-3$. Add $2\text{OH}^-$ to the right side:
    

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^-(\text{aq}) + \text{I}^-(\text{aq}) \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2(\text{s}) + \text{IO}_3^-(\text{aq}) + 2\text{OH}^-(\text{aq})
$$

  * Audit charge: Left $= -3$, Right $= (-1) + 2(-1) = -3$. (Balanced)

* **Step 4: Balance Oxygen and Hydrogen Atoms with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$**
  * Left side Oxygen $= 2 \times 4 = 8$
  * Right side Oxygen $= (2 \times 2) + 3 + 2 = 9$
  * Left side lacks 1 Oxygen; add $1\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to the left side:
    

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^-(\text{aq}) + \text{I}^-(\text{aq}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2(\text{s}) + \text{IO}_3^-(\text{aq}) + 2\text{OH}^-(\text{aq})
$$

  * Audit Hydrogen: Left $= 2$; Right $= 2$.

* **Final Balanced Net Ionic Equation**:
  

$$
\boxed{2\text{MnO}_4^-(\text{aq}) + \text{I}^-(\text{aq}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2(\text{s}) + \text{IO}_3^-(\text{aq}) + 2\text{OH}^-(\text{aq})}
$$

***

### Example 3: Disproportionation Reaction in Basic Medium (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
**Problem**: Balance the alkaline disproportionation of elemental phosphorus ($\text{P}_4$) into phosphine ($\text{PH}_3$) and hypophosphite ($\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$) using the **Half-Reaction Method**:

$$
\text{P}_4(\text{s}) \rightarrow \text{PH}_3(\text{g}) + \text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-(\text{aq}) \quad [\text{Basic Medium}]
$$

#### Step-by-Step Derivation:

* **Step 1: Assign Oxidation States & Separate Half-Reactions**
  * Phosphorus in $\text{P}_4$: $0$
  * Phosphorus in $\text{PH}_3$: $x + 3(+1) = 0 \implies x = -3$ (Reduction: $0 \rightarrow -3$)
  * Phosphorus in $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$: $2(+1) + x + 2(-2) = -1 \implies x = +1$ (Oxidation: $0 \rightarrow +1$)
  * **Reduction Half**: $\text{P}_4 \rightarrow \text{PH}_3$
  * **Oxidation Half**: $\text{P}_4 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$

* **Step 2: Balance Reduction Half-Reaction ($\text{P}_4 \rightarrow \text{PH}_3$)**
  1. Balance $\text{P}$ atoms:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 \rightarrow 4\text{PH}_3
$$

  2. Balance $\text{H}$ atoms:
     Right side has $12\text{H}$. Since medium is basic, add $12\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to the left and $12\text{OH}^-$ to the right:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 + 12\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 4\text{PH}_3 + 12\text{OH}^-
$$

  3. Balance electrical charge:
     

$$
\text{Left Charge} = 0, \quad \text{Right Charge} = 12(-1) = -12
$$

     Add $12e^-$ to the left side:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 + 12\text{H}_2\text{O} + 12e^- \rightarrow 4\text{PH}_3 + 12\text{OH}^- \quad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}
$$

* **Step 3: Balance Oxidation Half-Reaction ($\text{P}_4 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$)**
  1. Balance $\text{P}$ atoms:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-
$$

  2. Balance $\text{O}$ atoms:
     Right side has $4 \times 2 = 8$ oxygen atoms. Add $8\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to the left side:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-
$$

  3. Balance $\text{H}$ atoms:
     Left has $16\text{H}$, Right has $8\text{H}$ (deficit of $8\text{H}$ on right).
     In basic medium, add $8\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to the deficient (right) side and $8\text{OH}^-$ to the opposite (left) side:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O} + 8\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^- + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

     Cancel $8\text{H}_2\text{O}$ from both sides:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 + 8\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-
$$

  4. Balance electrical charge:
     

$$
\text{Left Charge} = 8(-1) = -8, \quad \text{Right Charge} = 4(-1) = -4
$$

     Add $4e^-$ to the right side:
     

$$
\text{P}_4 + 8\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 4\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^- + 4e^- \quad \text{--- (Eq. 2)}
$$

* **Step 4: Equalize Electrons and Combine**
  * $(\text{Eq. 1})$ requires $12e^-$.
  * $(\text{Eq. 2})$ produces $4e^-$.
  * Multiply $(\text{Eq. 2})$ by 3:
    

$$
3\text{P}_4 + 24\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 12\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^- + 12e^-
$$

  * Add this to $(\text{Eq. 1})$:
    

$$
(\text{P}_4 + 12\text{H}_2\text{O} + 12e^-) + (3\text{P}_4 + 24\text{OH}^-) \rightarrow (4\text{PH}_3 + 12\text{OH}^-) + (12\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^- + 12e^-)
$$

  * Combine like species:
    

$$
4\text{P}_4 + 12\text{H}_2\text{O} + 24\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 4\text{PH}_3 + 12\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^- + 12\text{OH}^-
$$

  * Simplify $\text{OH}^-$ ($24 - 12 = 12\text{OH}^-$ on left):
    

$$
4\text{P}_4(\text{s}) + 12\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) + 12\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \rightarrow 4\text{PH}_3(\text{g}) + 12\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-(\text{aq})
$$

  * Divide the entire equation by the common divisor 4:

* **Final Balanced Net Ionic Equation**:
  

$$
\boxed{\text{P}_4(\text{s}) + 3\text{OH}^-(\text{aq}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \rightarrow \text{PH}_3(\text{g}) + 3\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-(\text{aq})}
$$

* **Final Verification**:
  * Atoms: $\text{P}: 4 = (1 + 3) = 4$; $\text{O}: 3 + 3 = 6$ vs $3 \times 2 = 6$; $\text{H}: 3 + 6 = 9$ vs $3 + 6 = 9$.
  * Charge: Left $= 3(-1) = -3$; Right $= 3(-1) = -3$. (Balanced)

***

### Example 4: Full Molecular Balancing with Spectator Ions (Advanced Board Archetype)
**Problem**: Balance the reaction of potassium permanganate with oxalic acid in the presence of dilute sulfuric acid by the **Oxidation Number Method**:

$$
\text{KMnO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{MnSO}_4 + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

#### Step-by-Step Derivation:

* **Step 1: Identify Key Redox Couples**
  * $\text{Mn}$ changes from $+7$ (in $\text{KMnO}_4$) to $+2$ (in $\text{MnSO}_4$):
    

$$
\Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{Mn}} = -5 \quad (\text{Decrease of 5 per Mn atom})
$$

  * $\text{C}$ changes from $+3$ (in $\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4$) to $+4$ (in $\text{CO}_2$):
    

$$
\Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{C per atom}} = +1
$$

    

$$
\text{Total change per molecule of } \text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 = 2 \times (+1) = +2 \quad (\text{Increase of 2 per formula unit})
$$

* **Step 2: Equalize Net Increase and Decrease in Oxidation Number**
  * Cross-multiply coefficients: Multiply $\text{KMnO}_4$ by 2, and $\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4$ by 5:
    

$$
2\text{KMnO}_4 + 5\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{MnSO}_4 + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Step 3: Balance Redox Product Species**
  * $2\text{KMnO}_4$ produces $2\text{MnSO}_4$ and $1\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$.
  * $5\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4$ (containing $10\text{C}$) produces $10\text{CO}_2$:
    

$$
2\text{KMnO}_4 + 5\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{MnSO}_4 + 10\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Step 4: Balance Sulfate Ions ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$) from the Acid**
  * Right side contains:
    

$$
1\text{ mole of } \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 \ (1\text{ SO}_4^{2-}) + 2\text{ moles of } \text{MnSO}_4 \ (2\text{ SO}_4^{2-}) = 3\text{ moles of } \text{SO}_4^{2-}
$$

  * Therefore, set coefficient of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ to 3:
    

$$
2\text{KMnO}_4 + 5\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + 3\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{MnSO}_4 + 10\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Step 5: Balance Hydrogen and Oxygen Atoms**
  * Count $\text{H}$ on left side:
    

$$
\text{From } 5\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 = 10\text{H}
$$

    

$$
\text{From } 3\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 = 6\text{H}
$$

    

$$
\text{Total H on left} = 16\text{H}
$$

  * Therefore, coefficient of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ on right side must be $\frac{16}{2} = 8$:
    

$$
2\text{KMnO}_4 + 5\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + 3\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{MnSO}_4 + 10\text{CO}_2 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Step 6: Verify Oxygen Count**
  * Left side $\text{O}$: $(2 \times 4) + (5 \times 4) + (3 \times 4) = 8 + 20 + 12 = 40$
  * Right side $\text{O}$: $(1 \times 4) + (2 \times 4) + (10 \times 2) + (8 \times 1) = 4 + 8 + 20 + 8 = 40$ (Matched)

* **Final Balanced Molecular Equation**:
  

$$
\boxed{2\text{KMnO}_4 + 5\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 + 3\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{K}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{MnSO}_4 + 10\text{CO}_2 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

## 4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

```
               ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
               │    COMMON REDOX BALANCING MISTAKES CHECK     │
               └──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                                      │
        ┌─────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                             ▼                             ▼
┌──────────────┐              ┌──────────────┐              ┌──────────────┐
│  Per-Atom vs │              │ Basic Medium │              │ Stoichiometry│
│  Per-Molecule│              │ Neutralizing │              │ Cancellation │
│  Error       │              │ Misstep      │              │ Omission     │
└───────┬──────┘              └───────┬──────┘              └───────┬──────┘
        │                             │                             │
   O.N. Change                   Adding OH- to                 Leaving non-
  forgot to mult.                one side only;               reduced common
  by subscript                   leaves net H+                multipliers
  (e.g., Cr2, C2)               in basic sol.                 (e.g., 2:4:2)
```

1. **The Subscript Multiplier Omission (Per-Atom vs. Per-Molecule Trap)**
   * *Trap*: In $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}$, the oxidation state changes from $+6$ to $+3$. Students often write $\Delta = 3e^-$.
   * *Correction*: There are **two** chromium atoms per formula unit. The total change per ion is:
     

$$
\Delta(\text{O.N.})_{\text{total}} = 2 \times (+6 - +3) = 6e^-
$$

   * Always balance the element whose oxidation number changes **before** calculating electron transfer.

2. **The "Basic Medium Neutralization" Slip**
   * *Trap*: Leaving $\text{H}^+$ in the final reaction equation when the problem explicitly specifies a **basic or alkaline medium**.
   * *Correction*: If balanced using the acidic pathway, add an equal number of $\text{OH}^-$ ions to **both sides** of the equation to convert all $\text{H}^+$ into $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, then cancel the water molecules appearing on both sides.

3. **Peroxide and Superoxide Oxidation State Hallucination**
   * *Trap*: Assigning oxygen an oxidation state of $-2$ unconditionally.
   * *Correction*: In hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) and peroxides ($\text{Na}_2\text{O}_2$, $\text{BaO}_2$), oxygen is **$-1$**. In superoxides ($\text{KO}_2$), oxygen is **$-\frac{1}{2}$**. In oxygen difluoride ($\text{OF}_2$), oxygen is **$+2$**.

4. **Charge Neglect in Mass Balancing**
   * *Trap*: Having equal numbers of atoms on both sides while failing to equalize the algebraic sum of electrical charges.
   * *Correction*: In net ionic equations, the algebraic sum of charges on the reactant side **must equal** that on the product side. It does not need to equal zero; it only needs to be identical on both sides.

5. **Failure to Reduce to Simplest Whole-Number Ratio**
   * *Trap*: Submitting an equation with coefficients such as $2\text{A} + 4\text{B} \rightarrow 2\text{C} + 6\text{D}$.
   * *Correction*: Stoichiometric coefficients must be expressed in the lowest coprime integer ratio: divide throughout by 2 to yield $1\text{A} + 2\text{B} \rightarrow 1\text{C} + 3\text{D}$.

### 5.2 The "$n$-factor" Quick Calculation Table
The $n$-factor (valence factor) represents the total number of moles of electrons lost or gained per mole of the reactant:

| Reagent / System | Transition | Change in Oxidation State | $n$-factor |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :---: |
| $\text{KMnO}_4$ (Acidic Medium) | $\text{Mn}^{+7} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{+2}$ | $+7 \rightarrow +2$ ($\Delta = 5$) | **$5$** |
| $\text{KMnO}_4$ (Neutral/Faintly Basic) | $\text{Mn}^{+7} \rightarrow \text{MnO}_2\,(+4)$ | $+7 \rightarrow +4$ ($\Delta = 3$) | **$3$** |
| $\text{KMnO}_4$ (Strongly Alkaline Medium)| $\text{Mn}^{+7} \rightarrow \text{MnO}_4^{2-}\,(+6)$ | $+7 \rightarrow +6$ ($\Delta = 1$) | **$1$** |
| $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ (Acidic Medium) | $2\text{Cr}^{+6} \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{+3}$ | $2 \times (+6 \rightarrow +3)$ | **$6$** |
| $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$ (Oxalate ion) | $\text{C}_2^{+3} \rightarrow 2\text{C}^{+4}$ | $2 \times (+3 \rightarrow +4)$ | **$2$** |
| $\text{FeSO}_4$ | $\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}$ | $+2 \rightarrow +3$ | **$1$** |
| $\text{FeC}_2\text{O}_4$ (Ferrous Oxalate) | $\text{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}$ and $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2$ | $1 + 2 = 3$ | **$3$** |

> **Memory Hook for $\text{KMnO}_4$**: **BAN 153**  
> * **B**asic medium $\rightarrow n = 1$  
> * **A**cidic medium $\rightarrow n = 5$  
> * **N**eutral medium $\rightarrow n = 3$

### 5.3 Quick Formula for Disproportionation $n$-factor
When a single species disproportionates into two products where the oxidation step has $n$-factor $n_1$ and the reduction step has $n$-factor $n_2$, the net equivalent weight $E$ and net $n$-factor are given by:

$$
\frac{1}{n_{\text{net}}} = \frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2} \implies n_{\text{net}} = \frac{n_1 \times n_2}{n_1 + n_2}
$$

$$
\text{Equivalent Weight } E = \frac{M}{n_{\text{net}}} = \frac{M}{n_1} + \frac{M}{n_2}
$$

*Example: For the disproportionation of phosphorus:*

$$
\text{P}_4 \rightarrow \text{PH}_3 \ (n_1 = 12e^-/\text{mol P}_4), \quad \text{P}_4 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^- \ (n_2 = 4e^-/\text{mol P}_4)
$$

$$
n_{\text{net}} = \frac{12 \times 4}{12 + 4} = \frac{48}{16} = 3 \implies E = \frac{M}{3}
$$

***

### 5.4 Cross-Multiplication Balancing Shortcut (The 15-Second Test Technique)
To balance net ionic reactions rapidly during objective exams (JEE/NEET):

1. Compute $n$-factor of the oxidant ($n_{\text{ox}}$) and reducing agent ($n_{\text{red}}$).
2. Cross-multiply the two reactant species directly by each other's $n$-factors:
   

$$
n_{\text{red}}\,\text{Oxidant} + n_{\text{ox}}\,\text{Reductant}
$$

3. Balance oxidized and reduced product species immediately by matching central atoms.
4. Complete the balance of oxygen using $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, then balance hydrogen with $\text{H}^+$ (or $\text{OH}^-$).

*Demonstration on $\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{Mn}^{2+} + \text{CO}_2$:*
* $n_{\text{MnO}_4^-} = 5$
* $n_{\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}} = 2$
* Cross-multiply reactants immediately:
  

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}
$$

* Balance $\text{Mn}$ and $\text{C}$ on product side:
  

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 10\text{CO}_2
$$

* Balance oxygen ($8\text{O}$ on left from permanganate, oxalates match $\text{CO}_2$):
  Add $8\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to the right side.
* Balance hydrogen:
  Add $16\text{H}^+$ to the left side.
* Net Result:
  

$$
\boxed{2\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} + 16\text{H}^+ \rightarrow 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 10\text{CO}_2 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

# Master Teaching Note: Redox Reactions and Electrode Processes

**Class 11 Chemistry | Unit: Redox Reactions**  
**Core Topic:** Redox Reactions & Electrode Processes: Electrochemical Cells and Standard Electrode Potential ($E^\circ$)

***

## 1. Pedagogical Theory & Foundational Concepts

### 1.1 Direct vs. Indirect Redox Reactions
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐*

* **Direct Redox Reactions:** Oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously in the **same vessel**. 
  * *Example:* A strip of zinc metal immersed in an aqueous solution of copper sulfate ($\text{CuSO}_4$).
  * *Observations:*
    1. Zinc rod loses mass as metallic zinc dissolves:
       

$$
\text{Zn}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-
$$

    2. Deep blue color of $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)$ fades as copper ions are reduced:
       

$$
\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(s)
$$

    3. Reddish-brown spongy copper metal precipitates at the bottom and on the zinc strip.
    4. Reaction is **exothermic**; chemical energy dissipates irreversibly into the surroundings as **thermal energy** (heat, $q < 0$).
    5. No external electrical work ($w_{\text{elec}}$) can be harnessed.
* **Indirect Redox Reactions:** Oxidation and reduction half-reactions are physically segregated into **two separate compartments** (half-cells).
  * Electrons do not transfer directly through collision between $\text{Zn}$ atoms and $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions.
  * Electron transfer is forced across an **external metallic conductor** (connecting wire).
  * Chemical free energy ($\Delta G$) is converted directly into **electrical work** ($w_{\text{elec}} = -\Delta G$).

***

### 1.2 The Galvanic / Voltaic Cell (The Daniell Cell Archetype)
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐*

A **Galvanic cell** is an electrochemical device that transforms the decrease in Gibbs free energy of a spontaneous chemical redox reaction into electrical energy.

```
       e- Flow  ------>  (External Circuit)
   [Zn Anode (-)]                           [Cu Cathode (+)]
         |                                         |
  +--------------+                         +---------------+
  |  ZnSO4 (aq)  | <====== Salt Bridge ====> |   CuSO4 (aq)  |
  +--------------+                         +---------------+
       <------ Conventional Current Flow
```

#### Half-Cell Architecture & Phase Boundaries
1. **Oxidation Half-Cell (Anode):**
   * A zinc metal plate dipped in an aqueous $\text{ZnSO}_4$ solution.
   * Zinc atoms undergo oxidation, shedding electrons to the electrode matrix and entering the solution as hydrated cations:
     

$$
\text{Zn}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \quad (\text{Oxidation Half-Reaction})
$$

   * Because electrons accumulate on the zinc electrode surface before passing through the external circuit, the **anode in a galvanic cell has a negative polarity ($-$)**.
2. **Reduction Half-Cell (Cathode):**
   * A copper metal plate dipped in an aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$ solution.
   * Hydrated $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions migrate to the electrode-solution interface, capture electrons arriving from the external circuit, and deposit as metallic copper:
     

$$
\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(s) \quad (\text{Reduction Half-Reaction})
$$

   * Because electrons are consumed from this terminal, it has a higher potential relative to the anode; the **cathode in a galvanic cell has a positive polarity ($+$)**.
3. **Net Cell Reaction:**
   

$$
\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

***

### 1.3 The Salt Bridge: Physico-Chemical Role & Dynamics
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐*

A **Salt Bridge** is an inverted U-shaped glass tube packed with a semi-solid gel matrix (agar-agar or gelatin) saturated with a concentrated aqueous solution of an **inert electrolyte** (e.g., $\text{KCl}$, $\text{KNO}_3$, $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$).

#### Primary Functions:
1. **Completion of the Internal Circuit:** It permits the migration of ions between the two half-cells without mechanical mixing of bulk solutions.
2. **Maintenance of Electrical Neutrality:**
   * In the anode compartment, continuous generation of $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ causes an excess positive charge layer around the zinc rod, which would retard further electron ejection. Anions from the salt bridge (e.g., $\text{Cl}^-$ or $\text{NO}_3^-$) migrate into the anolyte to neutralize the excess $\text{Zn}^{2+}$.
   * In the cathode compartment, continuous removal of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ leaves an excess of $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ counterions, repelling incoming electrons. Cations from the salt bridge (e.g., $\text{K}^+$ or $\text{NH}_4^+$) migrate into the catholyte to neutralize the excess $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$.
3. **Elimination of Liquid Junction Potential ($E_{LJ}$):** When two different electrolyte solutions meet directly, differing migration velocities of cations and anions generate a potential gradient across the liquid-liquid boundary. The salt bridge eliminates this junction potential by selecting ions of nearly identical transport numbers.

#### Essential Criteria for Selecting an Inert Salt:
* **Equi-transference / Equal Ionic Mobility:** The transport numbers ($t_+$ and $t_-$) and ionic mobilities ($u_+$ and $u_-$) of the cation and anion must be nearly identical:
  

$$
u_{\text{K}^+} \approx u_{\text{Cl}^-}, \quad u_{\text{K}^+} \approx u_{\text{NO}_3^-}
$$

* **Chemical Inertness:** The electrolyte must not react chemically or form insoluble precipitates with the ions present in the half-cells.
  * *Critical Prohibition:* **$\text{KCl}$ cannot be used** if the half-cell contains $\text{Ag}^+$, $\text{Pb}^{2+}$, $\text{Hg}_2^{2+}$, or $\text{Tl}^+$ ions, because sparingly soluble halides precipitate (e.g., $\text{Ag}^+ + \text{Cl}^- \longrightarrow \text{AgCl}(s)\downarrow$), destroying cell reversibility. In such cases, $\text{KNO}_3$ or $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$ is mandatory.

***

### 1.4 Origin of Electrode Potential & Electrical Double Layer (EDL)
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐*

When a metal strip $M$ is placed into an aqueous solution containing its own ions $M^{n+}(aq)$, two opposing tendencies develop at the solid-liquid phase boundary:

```
               [ Metal M ]  |  [ Solution M^(n+) ]
      De-electronation      |
   M(s) --------------> M^(n+)(aq) + n e-  (Solution Pressure, P_s)
                            |
       Electronation        |
   M^(n+)(aq) + n e- ---> M(s)             (Osmotic Pressure, P_o)
```

1. **Solution Pressure ($P_s$ / De-electronation Tendency):**
   * Metal atoms tend to lose electrons to the electrode and pass into solution as cations ($M \longrightarrow M^{n+} + ne^-$).
   * Drives the metal to become negatively charged relative to the solution.
2. **Osmotic Pressure of Ions ($P_o$ / Electronation Tendency):**
   * Hydrated cations $M^{n+}(aq)$ collide with the metal surface, withdraw electrons, and deposit as neutral metal atoms ($M^{n+} + ne^- \longrightarrow M$).
   * Drives the metal to become positively charged relative to the solution.

At dynamic equilibrium, the rate of de-electronation equals the rate of electronation:

$$
M(s) \rightleftharpoons M^{n+}(aq) + n e^-
$$

A charge separation is established across the phase boundary, forming the **Helmholtz Electrical Double Layer**. 

> **Electrode Potential ($E$):** The electrical potential difference set up between the metal electrode and the electrolyte solution containing its ions at dynamic equilibrium.

***

### 1.5 IUPAC Conventions for Electrode Potential
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐*

1. **Reduction Potential Convention:**
   * According to IUPAC rules, the term **"Electrode Potential" without qualification ALWAYS denotes the Standard Reduction Potential ($E^\circ_{\text{red}}$)**.
   * Oxidation potential is simply the negative of the reduction potential:
     

$$
E^\circ_{\text{ox}} = - E^\circ_{\text{red}}
$$

2. **Standard State Specifications:**
   * Standard Electrode Potential ($E^\circ$) is measured when:
     * Activity (effective concentration) of all dissolved ionic species: $C = 1.00\text{ M} \ (\text{mol}\cdot\text{L}^{-1})$.
     * Pressure of all gaseous species involved: $P = 1.00\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa}$ (or traditionally $1\text{ atm} = 1.01325\text{ bar}$).
     * Standard reference temperature: $T = 298.15\text{ K} \ (25^\circ\text{C})$.
     * Pure solid or liquid phases are in their thermodynamic standard states (activity $a = 1$).

***

### 1.6 The Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE / NHE)
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐*

Because an isolated half-cell cannot undergo a redox process on its own, **absolute electrode potential cannot be measured experimentally**. The potential of a single electrode can only be determined by coupling it with a standard reference electrode whose potential is defined by convention.

The **Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)** is the universal primary reference electrode.

```
                  H2 gas (1 bar, 298 K)
                         |
                         v
                    +----------+
                    |  Glass   |
                    |  Jacket  |
                    |          |
      Pt Wire ----->|    ||    |
                    |    ||    |
                    |    ||    |
  1.0 M H+(aq) ===> |  ======  | <--- Platinized Pt foil (Pt(s)|H2(g)|H+(aq))
                    +----------+
```

* **Construction:**
  * A glass tube containing a platinum wire sealed into it, terminating in a small rectangular foil of **platinized platinum** (platinum coated with finely divided platinum black to dramatically increase the electroactive surface area).
  * Dipped into an aqueous solution of a monoprotic strong acid containing $\text{H}^+$ ions at unit molar concentration ($[\text{H}^+] = 1.0\text{ M}$; strictly, activity $a_{\text{H}^+} = 1$).
  * Pure, dry hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) is continuously bubbled over the platinum foil at a controlled pressure of $1.0\text{ bar}$ at $298.15\text{ K}$.
* **Assigned Conventional Potential:**
  

$$
E^\circ_{\text{H}^+/\text{H}_2} = 0.000\text{ V} \quad (\text{at all temperatures by definition})
$$

* **Half-Reaction Equilbria:**
  * As Anode:
    

$$
\text{H}_2(g, 1\text{ bar}) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}^+(aq, 1\text{ M}) + 2e^- \quad (E^\circ_{\text{ox}} = 0.00\text{ V})
$$

  * As Cathode:
    

$$
2\text{H}^+(aq, 1\text{ M}) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2(g, 1\text{ bar}) \quad (E^\circ_{\text{red}} = 0.00\text{ V})
$$

* **Standard Cell Representation:**
  

$$
\text{Pt}(s) \mid \text{H}_2(g, 1\text{ bar}) \mid \text{H}^+(aq, 1\text{ M})
$$

## 2. Reactions, Derivations & Quantitative Formulations

### 2.1 Standard Cell Notation (IUPAC Cell Diagram Rules)
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐*

To represent any galvanic cell without sketching laboratory hardware, the IUPAC convention dictates:

1. **Left-Hand Side:** Anode (Oxidation half-cell).
2. **Right-Hand Side:** Cathode (Reduction half-cell).
3. **Phase Boundaries:** Denoted by a single vertical line ($\mid$).
4. **Liquid-Liquid Junction / Salt Bridge:** Denoted by a double vertical line ($\parallel$).
5. **Phase Continuity:** Multiple species in the same phase are separated by commas (e.g., $\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq), \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq)$).
6. **Inert Current Collectors:** Listed at the outermost positions (e.g., $\text{Pt}(s)$ or $\text{C}(\text{graphite})$).

$$
\text{Anode Metal} \mid \text{Anolyte Cation } (C_1) \parallel \text{Catholyte Cation } (C_2) \mid \text{Cathode Metal}
$$

For the classic Daniell Cell:

$$
\text{Zn}(s) \mid \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq, 1\text{ M}) \parallel \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq, 1\text{ M}) \mid \text{Cu}(s)
$$

***

### 2.2 Derivation of Electromotive Force ($E_{\text{cell}}$)
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐*

The **Electromotive Force (EMF)** or **Cell Potential** ($E_{\text{cell}}$) is the maximum potential difference between the two electrodes of a galvanic cell when **zero current flows** through an external potentiometer:

$$
E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}
$$

#### Step-by-Step Derivation from Electrode Potentials:
Let $V_{\text{cathode}}$ and $V_{\text{anode}}$ be the electrical potentials of the cathode and anode relative to the solution.

The driving force for the complete cell is the sum of the oxidation potential at the anode and the reduction potential at the cathode:

$$
E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{ox (anode)}} + E_{\text{red (cathode)}}
$$

By IUPAC definition:

$$
E_{\text{ox (anode)}} = - E_{\text{red (anode)}}
$$

Substituting into the EMF expression gives the standard operational formula:

$$
E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{red (cathode)}} - E_{\text{red (anode)}}
$$

Under standard state conditions ($1\text{ M}$, $1\text{ bar}$, $298\text{ K}$):

$$
\boxed{E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = E^\circ_{\text{right}} - E^\circ_{\text{left}}}
$$

$$
\text{Units: Volts } (\text{V}) \equiv \text{Joules per Coulomb } (\text{J}\cdot\text{C}^{-1})
$$

***

### 2.3 Thermodynamic Derivation: Relation Between $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$, $\Delta_r G^\circ$, and $K_c$
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐*

#### 1. Relation Between $\Delta_r G$ and $E_{\text{cell}}$:
From classical thermodynamics, the reversible non-expansion work done by a closed system at constant temperature and pressure equals the decrease in its Gibbs free energy:

$$
-\Delta_r G = w_{\text{electrical, max}}
$$

Electrical work is the product of total electric charge transferred ($Q$) and the potential difference through which it moves ($E_{\text{cell}}$):

$$
w_{\text{electrical, max}} = Q \cdot E_{\text{cell}}
$$

For $n$ moles of electrons transferred per mole of reaction:

$$
Q = n \cdot F
$$

Where:
* $n$ = stoichiometric number of electrons exchanged in the balanced redox equation (dimensionless).
* $F$ = Faraday's constant $\approx 96485.33\text{ C}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (charge carried by 1 mole of electrons; $e \cdot N_A = 1.60217663 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C} \times 6.02214 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}$).

Equating both relations:

$$
\Delta_r G = -nFE_{\text{cell}}
$$

Under standard state conditions:

$$
\boxed{\Delta_r G^\circ = -n F E^\circ_{\text{cell}}}
$$

$$
\text{Units: } \Delta_r G^\circ \text{ in }\text{J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \text{ or }\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

#### Thermodynamic Criteria for Spontaneity:
* $\Delta_r G^\circ < 0 \iff E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0 \implies \text{Spontaneous forward reaction (Galvanic operation)}$
* $\Delta_r G^\circ > 0 \iff E^\circ_{\text{cell}} < 0 \implies \text{Non-spontaneous forward reaction (Electrolytic operation required)}$
* $\Delta_r G^\circ = 0 \iff E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0 \implies \text{Dynamic chemical equilibrium}$

#### 2. Relation Between $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ and Equilibrium Constant ($K_c$):
From chemical thermodynamics:

$$
\Delta_r G^\circ = -RT \ln K_c
$$

Substitute $\Delta_r G^\circ = -n F E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$:

$$
-n F E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = -RT \ln K_c
$$

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = \frac{RT}{nF} \ln K_c = \frac{2.303 R T}{n F} \log_{10} K_c
$$

Substituting standard constants at $T = 298.15\text{ K}$:
* $R = 8.31446\text{ J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
* $F = 96485\text{ C}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
* $\frac{2.303 \times 8.31446 \times 298.15}{96485} \approx 0.05916\text{ V}$

$$
\boxed{E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = \frac{0.0591}{n} \log_{10} K_c \quad (\text{at } 298\text{ K})}
$$

Or equivalently for the equilibrium constant:

$$
\boxed{\log_{10} K_c = \frac{n E^\circ_{\text{cell}}}{0.0591}}
$$

| $\text{Cl}_2(g) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{Cl}^-$ | $+1.36$ | |
| $\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5e^- \rightleftharpoons \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | $+1.51$ | |
| $\text{F}_2(g) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{F}^-$ | $+2.87$ | **Strongest Oxidizing Agent** ($\text{F}_2$) |

#### Quantitative Chemical Principles Derived from the Series:
1. **Oxidizing Power:** Higher (more positive) $E^\circ \implies$ greater electron affinity $\implies$ stronger oxidizing agent (top right of reduction reactions, e.g., $\text{F}_2$).
2. **Reducing Power:** Lower (more negative) $E^\circ \implies$ greater ease of releasing electrons $\implies$ stronger reducing agent (bottom left of oxidation reactions, e.g., metallic $\text{Li}$).
3. **Spontaneous Displacement of Hydrogen:** Any metal with an $E^\circ_{\text{red}} < 0.00\text{ V}$ (situated above $\text{H}_2$ in typical negative-to-positive series) reduces $\text{H}^+(aq)$ from dilute non-oxidizing mineral acids ($\text{HCl}$, dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) to evolve $\text{H}_2(g)$:
   

$$
M(s) + n\text{H}^+(aq) \longrightarrow M^{n+}(aq) + \frac{n}{2}\text{H}_2(g) \quad (E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0 - E^\circ_M = -E^\circ_M > 0)
$$

   *Metals with $E^\circ > 0.00\text{ V}$ (e.g., $\text{Cu}, \text{Ag}, \text{Au}$) cannot liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas from non-oxidizing acids.*
4. **Spontaneous Metal Displacement:** A metal with a more negative $E^\circ$ displaces a metal with a less negative / more positive $E^\circ$ from its aqueous salt solution:
   

$$
\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s) \quad (\Delta E^\circ = +0.34 - (-0.76) = +1.10\text{ V} > 0)
$$

## 3. High-Yield Solved Examples & Exemplar Problems

### Example 1: Predicting Reactivity and Constructing Cell Notation
*Problem:* Predict whether a spontaneous redox reaction occurs between $\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq)$ and $\text{I}^-(aq)$ under standard conditions at $298\text{ K}$. If so, write the balanced chemical equation, formulate the complete IUPAC cell notation, and calculate $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$.  
*Given:* $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}} = +0.77\text{ V}$, $E^\circ_{\text{I}_2/\text{I}^-} = +0.54\text{ V}$.

#### Solution:
* **Step 1: Identify Potential Half-Reactions:**
  For a reaction between $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ and $\text{I}^-$, the only possible processes are:
  

$$
\text{Reduction: } \text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) \quad (E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} = +0.77\text{ V})
$$

  

$$
\text{Oxidation: } 2\text{I}^-(aq) \longrightarrow \text{I}_2(s) + 2e^- \quad (E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = +0.54\text{ V})
$$

* **Step 2: Balance the Electron Transfer ($n$):**
  Multiply the reduction half-reaction by 2 and add to the oxidation half-reaction:
  

$$
2\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + 2\text{I}^-(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{I}_2(s) \quad (n = 2)
$$

* **Step 3: Calculate Standard Cell Potential ($E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$):**
  

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = +0.77\text{ V} - (+0.54\text{ V}) = \mathbf{+0.23\text{ V}}
$$

* **Step 4: Assess Thermodynamic Spontaneity:**
  Because $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +0.23\text{ V} > 0$, the reaction is **thermodynamically feasible and spontaneous**.
* **Step 5: Write the IUPAC Cell Notation:**
  Both half-cells involve soluble species or non-metallic solids; thus, chemically inert $\text{Pt}(s)$ electrodes must serve as physical conductors:
  

$$
\boxed{\text{Pt}(s) \mid \text{I}^-(aq, 1\text{ M}) \mid \text{I}_2(s) \parallel \text{Fe}^{3+}(aq, 1\text{ M}), \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq, 1\text{ M}) \mid \text{Pt}(s)} \quad \blacksquare
$$

***

### Example 2: Non-Additivity of Standard Reduction Potentials (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
*Problem:* Given the standard potentials:
1. $\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + 3e^- \longrightarrow \text{Fe}(s) \quad E^\circ_1 = -0.036\text{ V}$
2. $\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Fe}(s) \quad E^\circ_2 = -0.440\text{ V}$

Calculate the standard electrode potential for:

$$
\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) \quad E^\circ_3 = ?
$$

#### Solution:
* **Step 1: Check Additivity Rule:**
  $E^\circ$ is an **intensive property** and is **NOT directly additive** when combining half-reactions to produce a new half-reaction! We must convert each step into its extensive thermodynamic counterpart: $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$.
* **Step 2: Write Thermodynamic Cycles:**
  * Reaction 1: $\text{Fe}^{3+} + 3e^- \longrightarrow \text{Fe} \implies n_1 = 3$
    

$$
\Delta G^\circ_1 = -n_1 F E^\circ_1 = -3 F (-0.036\text{ V}) = +0.108 F
$$

  * Reaction 2: $\text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Fe} \implies n_2 = 2$
    

$$
\Delta G^\circ_2 = -n_2 F E^\circ_2 = -2 F (-0.440\text{ V}) = +0.880 F
$$

* **Step 3: Relate the Reactions:**
  Target Reaction (3) = Reaction (1) $-$ Reaction (2):
  

$$
\text{Fe}^{3+} + 3e^- - (\text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^-) \longrightarrow \text{Fe} - \text{Fe} \implies \text{Fe}^{3+} + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}
$$

  Therefore, by Hess's Law:
  

$$
\Delta G^\circ_3 = \Delta G^\circ_1 - \Delta G^\circ_2
$$

* **Step 4: Substitute $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$:**
  

$$
-n_3 F E^\circ_3 = \Delta G^\circ_1 - \Delta G^\circ_2
$$

  Here $n_3 = 1$:
  

$$
-(1) F E^\circ_3 = (+0.108 F) - (+0.880 F)
$$

  

$$
-E^\circ_3 = -0.772\text{ V} \implies \boxed{E^\circ_3 = +0.772\text{ V}} \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Example 3: Calculation of $\Delta_r G^\circ$ and Equilibrium Constant ($K_c$)
*Problem:* For the Daniell cell operating at $298\text{ K}$:

$$
\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

Given $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.763\text{ V}$ and $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} = +0.340\text{ V}$. Calculate:
1. Standard cell potential ($E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$).
2. Standard Gibbs energy change of the reaction ($\Delta_r G^\circ$) in $\text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
3. Equilibrium constant ($K_c$) at $298\text{ K}$.

#### Solution:
* **Step 1: Calculate $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$:**
  

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} - E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = +0.340\text{ V} - (-0.763\text{ V}) = \mathbf{+1.103\text{ V}}
$$

* **Step 2: Calculate $\Delta_r G^\circ$:**
  Here, $n = 2$ moles of electrons are transferred per mole of reaction:
  

$$
\Delta_r G^\circ = -n F E^\circ_{\text{cell}}
$$

  

$$
\Delta_r G^\circ = -(2\text{ mol}) \times (96485\text{ C}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (1.103\text{ V})
$$

  

$$
\Delta_r G^\circ = -212845\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = \boxed{\mathbf{-212.85\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}}
$$

* **Step 3: Calculate $K_c$:**
  

$$
\log_{10} K_c = \frac{n E^\circ_{\text{cell}}}{0.0591} = \frac{2 \times 1.103}{0.0591} = \frac{2.206}{0.0591} \approx 37.326
$$

  

$$
K_c = 10^{37.326} = 10^{0.326} \times 10^{37} \approx \mathbf{2.12 \times 10^{37}} \quad \blacksquare
$$

  *(The large $K_c$ indicates the displacement reaction goes to virtual completion).*

### Example 4: Influence of Opposing External Counter-Potential ($E_{\text{ext}}$) on Daniell Cell
*Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Core NCERT Conceptual Question)*

Consider a Daniell cell connected in opposition to an adjustable external DC voltage source ($E_{\text{ext}}$):

```
       +------------------- (E_ext) -------------------+
       |                                                |
   [Zn Anode]                                     [Cu Cathode]
       |                                                |
   ZnSO4(aq) <============ Salt Bridge ============> CuSO4(aq)
```

| Applied Potential Condition | Electron Flow Direction | Current Flow Direction | Chemical Reaction Direction | Operating Regime |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Case 1:** $E_{\text{ext}} < 1.10\text{ V}$ | Zinc $\longrightarrow$ Copper (External circuit) | Copper $\longrightarrow$ Zinc | Forward: $\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \longrightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$ | **Galvanic Cell** (Discharging) |
| **Case 2:** $E_{\text{ext}} = 1.10\text{ V}$ | **No net electron flow** | **Zero current ($I = 0$)** | **No net chemical reaction** (Dynamic equilibrium) | **Cell Inactive** |
| **Case 3:** $E_{\text{ext}} > 1.10\text{ V}$ | Copper $\longrightarrow$ Zinc (Reversed) | Zinc $\longrightarrow$ Copper | Reverse: $\text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu} \longrightarrow \text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+}$ | **Electrolytic Cell** (Charging) |

*Key Takeaway for Exams:* When $E_{\text{ext}} > E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$, the cell reverses its role: Zinc is deposited on the zinc rod, and copper dissolves from the copper plate.

## 4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

```
   TRAP: Multiplying E° by Stoichiometric Coefficients
   ----------------------------------------------------
   Ag+(aq) + e- ---> Ag(s)         E° = +0.80 V
   2Ag+(aq) + 2e- ---> 2Ag(s)      E° = +0.80 V  (NOT +1.60 V!)
   [Reason: Potential is an INTENSIVE variable (Joules per Coulomb)]
```

* **Pitfall 1: Multiplying $E^\circ$ Values by Reaction Coefficients.**
  * *Error:* When doubling a half-reaction to balance electrons (e.g., $2 \times [\text{Ag}^+ + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Ag}]$), students double the potential: $E^\circ = 2 \times 0.80\text{ V} = 1.60\text{ V}$.
  * *Correction:* $E^\circ$ is an **intensive property** (energy per unit charge, $\text{J}\cdot\text{C}^{-1}$), independent of the quantity of matter. It **never** scales with stoichiometry. Only extensive properties like $\Delta G^\circ$ multiply with stoichiometric factors.

* **Pitfall 2: Double-Subtracting Negative Reduction Potentials.**
  * *Error:* For $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$, where $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76\text{ V}$, writing $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.34 - (+0.76) = -0.42\text{ V}$.
  * *Correction:* Always substitute the value with its sign:
    

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = (+0.34\text{ V}) - (-0.76\text{ V}) = +0.34\text{ V} + 0.76\text{ V} = +1.10\text{ V}
$$

* **Pitfall 3: Inverting Signs Manually When Using the Standard Formula.**
  * *Error:* Changing the sign of the anode potential to an oxidation potential, and *then* subtracting it in $E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$.
  * *Rule:* Pick **one** formal convention and stay consistent:
    * *Standard IUPAC Formula:* $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{red(cathode)}} - E^\circ_{\text{red(anode)}}$
    * *Additive Form:* $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{red(cathode)}} + E^\circ_{\text{ox(anode)}}$

* **Pitfall 4: Misidentifying Electrodes in Half-Cell Coupling.**
  * *Exam Trap:* "Can an aqueous solution of $\text{FeSO}_4$ be stirred with a copper spoon?"
  * *Rule:* The spoon acts as the reactant metal. If a reaction occurs:
    

$$
\text{Cu}(s) + \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Fe}(s)
$$

    

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}} - E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} = -0.44\text{ V} - (+0.34\text{ V}) = -0.78\text{ V}
$$

    Because $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} < 0$, the reaction is non-spontaneous. Yes, an $\text{FeSO}_4$ solution **can be safely stirred** with a copper spoon.

* **Pitfall 5: Neglecting Inert Electrodes in Cell Notation.**
  * Half-reactions with two solution-phase species (e.g., $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$ or $\text{MnO}_4^-/\text{Mn}^{2+}$) or gaseous species (e.g., $\text{Cl}_2/\text{Cl}^-$) have no solid metal to conduct electrons. Forgetting to write $\text{Pt}(s)$ or $\text{C}(s)$ on the outer flanks costs marks in board exams.

  * **R**eduction **I**s **G**ain of electrons

### 5.2 The Diagonal Rule for Spontaneity
On a standard reduction table arranged with most negative $E^\circ$ on top and most positive $E^\circ$ on the bottom:

```
   Species Upper-Left (Reduced Form, e.g., Zn)
                 \
                  \   SPONTANEOUS REACTION
                   \
                    v
          Species Lower-Right (Oxidized Form, e.g., Cu²⁺)
```

$$
\text{Species Upper-Left (Metal)} + \text{Species Lower-Right (Cation)} \longrightarrow \text{Spontaneous Process}
$$

\log_{10} K_c \approx \frac{n \times E^\circ_{\text{cell}}}{0.06} \quad (\text{Approximating } 0.0591 \approx 0.06)

   Since $F \approx 96500\text{ C}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$:
   

$$
\Delta_r G^\circ (\text{in kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) \approx -96.5 \times n \times E^\circ_{\text{cell}}

### CASE-BASED STUDY 1: The Chemical Metallurgy of Gold & Cyanide Leaching

**Tag:** `[CBSE Competency Case-Based, 4 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[Must-Know]`

#### Context / Stimulus:
In hydrometallurgical extraction, native gold ($\text{Au}$) is separated from low-grade ore via the MacArthur-Forrest process. Finely ground ore is treated with a dilute aerated aqueous solution of sodium cyanide ($\text{NaCN}$). Dissolved atmospheric oxygen oxidizes metallic gold to the aurous state ($+1$), forming a stable, water-soluble coordination complex, sodium dicyanoaurate(I):

$$
4\text{Au}(s) + 8\text{CN}^-(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow 4[\text{Au}(\text{CN})_2]^-(aq) + 4\text{OH}^-(aq)
$$

The pregnant leach solution is filtered, and gold is recovered by cementation using powdered zinc dust:

$$
\text{Zn}(s) + 2[\text{Au}(\text{CN})_2]^-(aq) \longrightarrow [\text{Zn}(\text{CN})_4]^{2-}(aq) + 2\text{Au}(s)
$$

#### Questions:
1. Identify the oxidizing agent and reducing agent in the dissolution (leaching) step. Support your choice using oxidation states. **[1 Mark]**
2. Calculate the equivalent mass of oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$) in the leaching reaction if its molar mass is $M = 32.00\text{ g mol}^{-1}$. **[1 Mark]**
3. (a) Is the cementation of gold by zinc thermodynamically spontaneous under standard conditions given $E^\circ_{[\text{Zn}(\text{CN})_4]^{2-}/\text{Zn}} = -1.26\text{ V}$ and $E^\circ_{[\text{Au}(\text{CN})_2]^-/\text{Au}} = -0.60\text{ V}$? Calculate $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$. **[1 Mark]**  
   **OR**  
   (b) State the dual role of dissolved $\text{O}_2$ in this process and explain what happens if deoxygenated water is used. **[1 Mark]**
4. What is the coordinate geometry and oxidation state of zinc in the product complex ion $[\text{Zn}(\text{CN})_4]^{2-}$? **[1 Mark]**

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

1. **Oxidation State Analysis:**
   - Gold ($\text{Au}$): Changes from $0$ in $\text{Au}(s)$ to $+1$ in $[\text{Au}(\text{CN})_2]^-$ (loss of electrons $\implies$ oxidation; $\text{Au}$ is the **reducing agent**). `[½ Mark]`
   - Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$): Changes from $0$ in $\text{O}_2(g)$ to $-2$ in $\text{OH}^-(aq)$ (gain of electrons $\implies$ reduction; $\text{O}_2$ is the **oxidizing agent**). `[½ Mark]`

2. **Equivalent Mass of $\text{O}_2$:**
   - In the reaction: $\text{O}_2^0 + 4e^- + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$.
   - Change in oxidation number per mole of $\text{O}_2 = 4 \times (0 - (-2)) = 4$ electrons ($n\text{-factor} = 4$). `[½ Mark]`
   - Equivalent Mass:
     

$$
E = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{n\text{-factor}} = \frac{32.00\text{ g mol}^{-1}}{4} = 8.00\text{ g eq}^{-1}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`

3. **(a)**
   - Anode (Oxidation): $\text{Zn} \longrightarrow [\text{Zn}(\text{CN})_4]^{2-} + 2e^- \quad (E^\circ_{\text{ox}} = +1.26\text{ V})$
   - Cathode (Reduction): $[\text{Au}(\text{CN})_2]^- + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Au} + 2\text{CN}^- \quad (E^\circ_{\text{red}} = -0.60\text{ V})$
   - Cell Potential:
     

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = -0.60\text{ V} - (-1.26\text{ V}) = +0.66\text{ V}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`
   - Since $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0$ ($\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}} < 0$), the displacement is **thermodynamically spontaneous**. `[½ Mark]`  
   **OR**  
   **(b)**
   - Oxygen acts as the electron acceptor (oxidant) driving the oxidation of insoluble $\text{Au}^0$ to $\text{Au}^+$. `[½ Mark]`
   - Without dissolved $\text{O}_2$, the redox couple lacks an oxidizing agent with a sufficiently positive reduction potential; gold will not dissolve, rendering leaching non-functional. `[½ Mark]`

4. **Complex Characterization:**
   - Oxidation state: $x + 4(-1) = -2 \implies x = +2$ ($\text{Zn}^{2+}$, $d^{10}$ system). `[½ Mark]`
   - Geometry: $sp^3$ hybridized, **Tetrahedral**. `[½ Mark]`

### CASE-BASED STUDY 2: Wastewater Treatment & Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)

**Tag:** `[CBSE Competency Case-Based, 4 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[High-Yield]`

#### Context / Stimulus:
Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) quantifies organic pollutants in industrial effluents by measuring the mass of oxygen consumed during complete oxidation by boiling acidified potassium dichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$):

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(aq) + 14\text{H}^+(aq) + 6e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}(aq) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)
$$

In an analytical laboratory test, a $50.0\text{ mL}$ sample of textile effluent was digested with $25.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.020\text{ M } \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ in $50\%\ \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ in the presence of $\text{Ag}_2\text{SO}_4$ (catalyst) and $\text{HgSO}_4$ (to mask chloride interference). The unreacted residual $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ required $15.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.050\text{ M}$ ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS, $\text{Fe}^{2+}$) solution to reach the ferroin end-point:

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 6\text{Fe}^{2+} + 14\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 6\text{Fe}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

#### Questions:
1. Calculate the initial millimoles of $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ added and the millimoles consumed by the unreacted back-titration. **[1 Mark]**
2. Determine the millimoles of $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ consumed purely by the organic pollutants in the wastewater. **[1 Mark]**
3. Calculate the Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) of the effluent in $\text{mg of }\text{O}_2\text{ per litre of sample}$ ($\text{ppm}$). **[2 Marks]**

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

1. **Initial & Excess Dichromate Calculations:**
   - Initial mmols of $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} = V \times M = 25.0\text{ mL} \times 0.020\text{ mmol mL}^{-1} = 0.50\text{ mmol}$. `[½ Mark]`
   - Mmols of $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ used = $15.0\text{ mL} \times 0.050\text{ M} = 0.75\text{ mmol}$.
   - From stoichiometry, $1\text{ mmol }\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \equiv 6\text{ mmol }\text{Fe}^{2+}$.
   - Excess (unreacted) $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} = \frac{0.75}{6} = 0.125\text{ mmol}$. `[½ Mark]`

2. **Dichromate Consumed by Organics:**
   

$$
\text{mmol}(\text{consumed}) = \text{mmol}(\text{initial}) - \text{mmol}(\text{excess}) = 0.50 - 0.125 = 0.375\text{ mmol}
$$

 `[1 Mark]`

3. **COD Computation:**
   - Equivalence Relation:
     

$$
1\text{ mol }\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \equiv 6\text{ equivalents of electrons} \equiv 1.5\text{ mol }\text{O}_2 \quad (\because \text{O}_2 + 4e^- \to 2\text{O}^{2-})
$$

 `[½ Mark]`
   - Moles of $\text{O}_2$ equivalent consumed:
     

$$
n(\text{O}_2) = 0.375\text{ mmol }\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \times 1.5 = 0.5625\text{ mmol }\text{O}_2
$$

 `[½ Mark]`
   - Mass of $\text{O}_2$:
     

$$
\text{Mass} = 0.5625\text{ mmol} \times 32\text{ mg mmol}^{-1} = 18.0\text{ mg}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`
   - Volume of sample = $50.0\text{ mL} = 0.050\text{ L}$.
   - $\text{COD}$:
     

$$
\text{COD} = \frac{18.0\text{ mg}}{0.050\text{ L}} = 360\text{ mg L}^{-1} = 360\text{ ppm}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`

**Reason ($\text{R}$):** Nitrogen in $\text{HNO}_3$ is in its maximum possible oxidation state of $+5$, whereas in $\text{HNO}_2$ it is in an intermediate oxidation state of $+3$.

**Assertion ($\text{A}$):** The oxidation number of carbon in glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) is zero, but all six carbon atoms do not have an oxidation state of zero.  
**Reason ($\text{R}$):** The oxidation number calculated from the stoichiometric molecular formula represents the formal average of the individual oxidation numbers of all constituent atoms.

- **Correct Option:** **(A)** `[½ Mark]`
- **Model Explanation:** In $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$, the formal oxidation state is:
  

$$
6x + 12(+1) + 6(-2) = 0 \implies 6x = 0 \implies x = 0
$$

  In the open-chain structure of D-glucose:
  - $\text{C}-1$ ($\text{CHO}$): $x + 1 - 2 = 0 \implies x = +1$
  - $\text{C}-2, \text{C}-3, \text{C}-4, \text{C}-5$ ($-\text{CHOH}-$): $x + 1 - 1 = 0 \implies x = 0$
  - $\text{C}-6$ ($-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$): $x + 2 - 1 = 0 \implies x = -1$
  Average:
  

$$
\frac{(+1) + 4(0) + (-1)}{6} = 0
$$

  Thus, the net zero value is a mathematical average across structurally distinct carbon atoms. `[½ Mark]`

#### Problem 1: Disproportionation vs. Comproportionation Mechanics
**Tag:** `[CBSE HOTS Multi-Concept, 3 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[Must-Know]`

**(a)** Phosphorous acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$) on heating disproportionates, whereas phosphoric acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$) does not. Justify this behavior with a balanced chemical equation and calculate the equivalent weight of $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$ during disproportionation in terms of its molecular weight $M$. **[2 Marks]**  
**(b)** Differentiate between disproportionation and comproportionation with an illustrative example of the latter. **[1 Mark]**

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

**(a)**
1. **Oxidation State Argument:**
   - In $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$, oxidation state of $\text{P} = +3$. Since phosphorus exhibits oxidation states ranging from $-3$ to $+5$, $+3$ is an intermediate state, allowing it to undergo simultaneous oxidation and reduction. `[½ Mark]`
   - In $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$, $\text{P}$ is at its maximum oxidation state of $+5$ and cannot be oxidized further; it cannot disproportionate. `[½ Mark]`
2. **Balanced Reaction:**
   

$$
4\overset{+3}{\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3} \xrightarrow{\quad\Delta\quad} 3\overset{+5}{\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4} + \overset{-3}{\text{PH}_3}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`
3. **Equivalent Weight of $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$:**
   - Oxidation half-reaction: $\text{P}(+3) \longrightarrow \text{P}(+5) + 2e^- \implies n_1 = 2$
   - Reduction half-reaction: $\text{P}(+3) + 6e^- \longrightarrow \text{P}(-3) \implies n_2 = 6$
   - Combined $n\text{-factor}$ for a disproportionating species:
     

$$
n = \frac{n_1 \times n_2}{n_1 + n_2} = \frac{2 \times 6}{2 + 6} = \frac{12}{8} = 1.5 = \frac{3}{2}
$$

     Alternatively, from the balanced reaction: $4\text{ moles of }\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$ exchange $6\text{ moles of electrons}$.
     

$$
\text{Electrons per mole} = \frac{6}{4} = 1.5
$$

   - Equivalent Mass:
     

$$
E = \frac{M}{1.5} = \frac{2M}{3}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`

**(b)**
- **Disproportionation:** A reaction in which an element in a single intermediate oxidation state is simultaneously oxidized and reduced into two different products. `[½ Mark]`
- **Comproportionation:** The reverse of disproportionation; two reactants containing the same element in different oxidation states react to form a single product with an intermediate oxidation state. `[½ Mark]`
  - *Example:*
    

$$
\overset{-2}{\text{H}_2\text{S}}(aq) + 2\overset{+4}{\text{SO}_2}(g) \longrightarrow 3\overset{0}{\text{S}}(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)
$$

    $\text{S}^{2-}$ and $\text{S}^{4+}$ converge to elemental sulfur ($\text{S}^0$).

 `[½ Mark]`

### NCERT Exercise Problem 8.8
**Tag:** `[NCERT Exercise Q8.8, 3 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[Must-Know]`

While sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) and hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) can act as both oxidizing and reducing agents in their chemical reactions, ozone ($\text{O}_3$) and nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) act only as oxidants. Explain this observation by evaluating the oxidation numbers of the central atoms.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

1. **Analysis of $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$:**
   - In $\text{SO}_2$, the oxidation state of Sulfur is $+4$. Sulfur belongs to Group 16 and exhibits oxidation states from $-2$ to $+6$. Because $+4$ is an intermediate oxidation state:
     - It can be oxidized to $+6$ (e.g., $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$), acting as a **reducing agent**.
     - It can be reduced to $0$ (elemental sulfur) or $-2$ ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$), acting as an **oxidizing agent**. `[1 Mark]`
   - In $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, the oxidation state of Oxygen is $-1$ (peroxide linkage). The stable oxidation limits of oxygen are $0$ and $-2$.
     - Oxidation: $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{O}_2^0 + 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-$ (acts as a **reducing agent**).
     - Reduction: $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}^{-2}$ (acts as an **oxidizing agent**). `[1 Mark]`

2. **Analysis of $\text{O}_3$ and $\text{HNO}_3$:**
   - In $\text{O}_3$ (ozone), the oxidation number of oxygen is $0$. Oxygen is the second most electronegative element; it cannot exhibit positive oxidation states with elements other than fluorine. Because it cannot lose electrons to increase its oxidation state above zero, it can only decrease its oxidation state to $-2$ ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or $\text{OH}^-$). Hence, $\text{O}_3$ acts exclusively as an **oxidizing agent**.
   - In $\text{HNO}_3$, Nitrogen has an oxidation state of $+5$, which corresponds to its maximum valence limit ($2s^2 2p^3 \implies 5$). Nitrogen cannot be oxidized further, so it can only undergo reduction to lower oxidation states ($+4, +2, 0$, etc.). Consequently, $\text{HNO}_3$ acts solely as an **oxidizing agent**. `[1 Mark]`

### NCERT Exercise Problem 8.18
**Tag:** `[NCERT Exercise Q8.18, 5 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[Must-Know]`

Balance the following redox equations by the **Ion-Electron (Half-Reaction) Method**:

**(a)** In acidic medium:

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(aq) + \text{SO}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{Cr}^{3+}(aq) + \text{HSO}_4^-(aq)

**(b)** In basic medium:

\text{MnO}_4^-(aq) + \text{I}^-(aq) \longrightarrow \text{MnO}_2(s) + \text{IO}_3^-(aq)

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

#### Part (a): Acidic Medium

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(aq) + \text{SO}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{Cr}^{3+}(aq) + \text{HSO}_4^-(aq)
$$

1. **Separate into Half-Reactions:**
   - Reduction: $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \longrightarrow \text{Cr}^{3+}$
   - Oxidation: $\text{SO}_2 \longrightarrow \text{HSO}_4^-$ `[½ Mark]`

2. **Balance the Reduction Half-Reaction:**
   - Balance chromium atoms:
     

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}
$$

   - Balance oxygen atoms using $\text{H}_2\text{O}$:
     

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}

\text{Left: } (-2) + 14(+1) = +12; \quad \text{Right: } 2(+3) = +6

\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}
$$

 `[1 Mark]`

   - Sulfur is balanced ($1:1$).
   - Balance oxygen using $\text{H}_2\text{O}$:

\text{SO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{HSO}_4^-

\text{SO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{HSO}_4^- + 3\text{H}^+

   - Balance charge:
     

$$
\text{Left: } 0; \quad \text{Right: } -1 + 3(+1) = +2
$$

     

$$
\text{SO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{HSO}_4^- + 3\text{H}^+ + 2e^- \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}
$$

 `[1 Mark]`

3\text{SO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 3\text{HSO}_4^- + 9\text{H}^+ + 6e^-
$$

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6e^- + 3\text{SO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O} + 3\text{HSO}_4^- + 9\text{H}^+ + 6e^-

 `[½ Mark]`

$$

 `[½ Mark]`
   - In $\text{CsAuF}_6$: Cesium has an oxidation state of $+1$ (alkali metal), and fluorine is $-1$.
     

$$
(+1) + x + 6(-1) = 0 \implies x = +5 \quad (\text{Gold is in the }\text{Au}^{5+}\text{ state})

### EXEMPLAR PROBLEM 1: Paradoxical Fractional Oxidation States & Structural Elucidation
**Tag:** `[NCERT Exemplar Subjective, 3 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[Must-Know]`

Conventional stoichiometry yields fractional oxidation states for:
**(a)** Carbon in Carbon suboxide ($\text{C}_3\text{O}_2$)  
**(b)** Bromine in Tribromooctoxide ($\text{Br}_3\text{O}_8$)  
**(c)** Sulfur in Sodium tetrathionate ($\text{Na}_2\text{S}_4\text{O}_6$)

Draw the Lewis structures for these three compounds, deduce the true individual oxidation state of each atom, and resolve the fractional oxidation state paradox.

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

#### (a) Carbon Suboxide ($\text{C}_3\text{O}_2$)
- **Lewis Structure:**
  

$$
\overset{-2}{\text{O}} = \overset{\text{C}_{(1)}}{\text{C}} = \overset{\text{C}_{(2)}}{\text{C}} = \overset{\text{C}_{(3)}}{\text{C}} = \overset{-2}{\text{O}}
$$

  Linear arrangement: $\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{O}$. `[½ Mark]`
- **Atom-by-Atom Oxidation States:**
  - $\text{C}_{(1)}$ and $\text{C}_{(3)}$ are each bonded via a double bond to electronegative oxygen and a double bond to neutral carbon:
    

$$
\text{Oxidation state of }\text{C}_{(1)} = +2; \quad \text{C}_{(3)} = +2
$$

  - $\text{C}_{(2)}$ (central carbon) is bonded only to adjacent carbon atoms (homonuclear non-polar $\text{C}=\text{C}$ bonds):
    

$$
\text{Oxidation state of }\text{C}_{(2)} = 0
$$

- **Paradox Resolution:**
  

$$
\text{Average} = \frac{(+2) + 0 + (+2)}{3} = +\frac{4}{3}
$$

  The fractional state $+\frac{4}{3}$ is the arithmetic average of two $+2$ carbons and one $0$ carbon. `[½ Mark]`

***

#### (b) Tribromooctoxide ($\text{Br}_3\text{O}_8$)
- **Lewis Structure:**
  

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
  \text{O} & \text{O} & \text{O} \\
  \parallel & \parallel & \parallel \\
  \text{O}=\overset{\text{Br}_{(1)}}{\text{Br}}-\!\! & \!\!-\overset{\text{Br}_{(2)}}{\text{Br}}-\!\! & \!\!-\overset{\text{Br}_{(3)}}{\text{Br}}=\text{O} \\
  \parallel & & \parallel \\
  \text{O} & & \text{O}
  \end{array}
$$

  Terminal bromines are bonded to three oxygens; central bromine is bonded to two oxygens. `[½ Mark]`
- **Atom-by-Atom Oxidation States:**
  - Terminal atoms ($\text{Br}_{(1)}$ and $\text{Br}_{(3)}$): Each has three coordinate/double bonds to oxygen atoms ($\text{O} = -2$) and one $\text{Br}-\text{Br}$ bond:
    

$$
\text{Oxidation state} = 3 \times (+2) + 0 = +6
$$

  - Central atom ($\text{Br}_{(2)}$): Bonded to two terminal oxygens ($\text{O} = -2$) and two $\text{Br}-\text{Br}$ bonds ($0$ contribution):
    

$$
\text{Oxidation state} = 2 \times (+2) + 0 = +4
$$

- **Paradox Resolution:**
  

$$
\text{Average} = \frac{(+6) + (+4) + (+6)}{3} = +\frac{16}{3}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`

***

#### (c) Sodium Tetrathionate ($\text{Na}_2\text{S}_4\text{O}_6$)
- **Lewis Structure of Tetrathionate Anion ($[\text{S}_4\text{O}_6]^{2-}$):**
  

$$
[\text{O}_3\overset{\text{S}_{(1)}}{\text{S}}-\overset{\text{S}_{(2)}}{\text{S}}-\overset{\text{S}_{(3)}}{\text{S}}-\overset{\text{S}_{(4)}}{\text{S}}\text{O}_3]^{2-}
$$

  Contains an unbranched chain of four sulfur atoms: $-\text{S}-\text{S}-\text{S}-\text{S}-$. `[½ Mark]`
- **Atom-by-Atom Oxidation States:**
  - Terminal sulfurs ($\text{S}_{(1)}$ and $\text{S}_{(4)}$): Bonded to three oxygen atoms (one single bond to $\text{O}^-$, two double bonds to $\text{O}$) and one $\text{S}-\text{S}$ single bond:
    

$$
\text{Oxidation state} = +5
$$

  - Central sulfurs ($\text{S}_{(2)}$ and $\text{S}_{(3)}$): Bonded only to adjacent sulfur atoms through homonuclear $\text{S}-\text{S}$ bonds:
    

$$
\text{Oxidation state} = 0
$$

- **Paradox Resolution:**
  

$$
\text{Average} = \frac{(+5) + 0 + 0 + (+5)}{4} = +\frac{10}{4} = +2.5
$$

 `[½ Mark]`

### EXEMPLAR PROBLEM 2: The Peroxo Compounds ($\text{CrO}_5$ and $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_5$)
**Tag:** `[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 3 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[Must-Know]`

1. Calculating the oxidation number of $\text{Cr}$ in chromium pentoxide ($\text{CrO}_5$) and $\text{S}$ in peroxomonosulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_5$, Caro's Acid) using formal valence rules gives $+10$ and $+8$, respectively. Explain why both results are chemically impossible. **[1 Mark]**
2. Draw the structures of both compounds (including the "butterfly" structure of $\text{CrO}_5$) and calculate the correct oxidation states based on chemical bonding. **[2 Marks]**

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

1. **Why $+10$ and $+8$ are Impossible:**
   - The maximum oxidation state of any transition metal or representative element cannot exceed the total number of its valence shell electrons (Group valence).
   - Chromium ($\text{Cr}: [\text{Ar}] 3d^5 4s^1$) has $6$ valence electrons; its maximum possible oxidation state is $+6$.
   - Sulfur ($\text{S}: [\text{Ne}] 3s^2 3p^4$) has $6$ valence electrons; its maximum possible oxidation state is $+6$.
   - Any calculated value above $+6$ indicates the presence of **peroxo linkages** ($-\text{O}-\text{O}-$, where each oxygen has an oxidation state of $-1$) rather than oxide ions ($\text{O}^{2-}$). `[1 Mark]`

2. **Structural Derivation:**

   **(a) Chromium Pentoxide ($\text{CrO}_5$ - Butterfly Structure):**
   ```text
        O
        ||
        Cr
      / | \ \
     O--O  O--O
   ```
   - $\text{CrO}_5$ contains:
     - One oxo group: $\text{Cr}=\text{O}$ (oxidation number of $\text{O} = -2$) `[½ Mark]`
     - Two peroxo groups: two $(-\text{O}-\text{O}-)$ rings (four peroxo oxygen atoms, each with an oxidation number of $-1$)
   - Oxidation state balance:
     

$$
x + 1(-2) + 4(-1) = 0 \implies x - 6 = 0 \implies x = +6
$$

 `[½ Mark]`

   **(b) Peroxomonosulfuric Acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_5$ - Caro's Acid):**
   ```text
         O
         ||
   H-O - S - O - O - H
         ||
         O
   ```
   - Bonding arrangement around central sulfur:
     - Two double-bonded oxo oxygens ($=\text{O}$): $2 \times (-2) = -4$
     - One hydroxyl oxygen ($-\text{OH}$): $1 \times (-2) = -2$
     - One peroxo group ($-\text{O}-\text{O}-\text{H}$): The inner oxygen bonded to $\text{S}$ has an oxidation state of $-1$.
   - Formal calculation:
     

$$
2(+1) + x + 3(-2) + 2(-1) = 0
$$

     

$$
+2 + x - 6 - 2 = 0 \implies x - 6 = 0 \implies x = +6
$$

 `[1 Mark]`

### EXEMPLAR PROBLEM 3: Analytical Electrochemical Competition
**Tag:** `[NCERT Exemplar MCQ Analytical, 2 Marks]`  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ `[High-Yield]`

You are provided with the following standard reduction potentials:
- $E^\circ_{\text{Ce}^{4+}/\text{Ce}^{3+}} = +1.74\text{ V}$
- $E^\circ_{\text{Cl}_2/\text{Cl}^-} = +1.36\text{ V}$
- $E^\circ_{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}/\text{Cr}^{3+}} = +1.33\text{ V}$
- $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}} = +0.77\text{ V}$
- $E^\circ_{\text{Sn}^{4+}/\text{Sn}^{2+}} = +0.15\text{ V}$

#### Questions:
1. Which of the following species can oxidize $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ to $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ without oxidizing $\text{Cl}^-$ to $\text{Cl}_2$?
   - **(A)** $\text{Ce}^{4+}$
   - **(B)** $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$
   - **(C)** $\text{Sn}^{4+}$
   - **(D)** $\text{Cl}_2$
2. Justify why hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$) cannot be used to acidify potassium permanganate ($\text{KMnO}_4$, $E^\circ = +1.51\text{ V}$) in redox titrations involving $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, but can be safely used with potassium dichromate ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$).

***

#### Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

1. **Selection:**
   - **Correct Option:** **(B)** $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ `[½ Mark]`
   - **Thermodynamic Basis:**
     - To oxidize $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ($E^\circ = +0.77\text{ V}$), the oxidant must have $E^\circ > +0.77\text{ V}$.
     - To leave $\text{Cl}^-$ unoxidized ($E^\circ_{\text{Cl}_2/\text{Cl}^-} = +1.36\text{ V}$), the oxidant must have $E^\circ < +1.36\text{ V}$.
     - The only species satisfying $0.77\text{ V} < E^\circ < 1.36\text{ V}$ is the dichromate ion:
       

$$
E^\circ_{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}/\text{Cr}^{3+}} = +1.33\text{ V}
$$

 `[½ Mark]`

2. **Analytical Justification:**
   - For $\text{KMnO}_4$:
     

$$
E^\circ_{\text{MnO}_4^-/\text{Mn}^{2+}} = +1.51\text{ V} > E^\circ_{\text{Cl}_2/\text{Cl}^-} (+1.36\text{ V})
$$

     $\text{MnO}_4^-$ oxidizes the chloride ions ($\text{Cl}^-$) from $\text{HCl}$ into toxic chlorine gas ($\text{Cl}_2$), consuming part of the titrant and yielding an incorrectly high titer value. `[½ Mark]`
   - For $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$:
     

$$
E^\circ_{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}/\text{Cr}^{3+}} = +1.33\text{ V} < E^\circ_{\text{Cl}_2/\text{Cl}^-} (+1.36\text{ V})
$$

     Dichromate is thermodynamically unable to oxidize $\text{Cl}^-$ to $\text{Cl}_2$ under standard conditions, so it selectively oxidizes only $\text{Fe}^{2+}$. Thus, $\text{HCl}$ does not interfere with dichromate titrations. `[½ Mark]`

## 1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

### 1.1 Lightning Balancing: The Cross-Multiplication Inspection Hack
Avoid writing full half-reactions on rough sheets during multiple-choice questions or initial step-planning. Use the **Net Change Cross-Multiplication Method**:

1. **Calculate Total $\Delta \text{O.N.}$ per formula unit**:
   - For $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \to 2\text{Cr}^{3+}$: Change per $\text{Cr}$ is $6 - 3 = 3$. With $2$ Cr atoms, net decrease $= 2 \times 3 = 6$.
   - For $\text{Fe}^{2+} \to \text{Fe}^{3+}$: Change per $\text{Fe} = 1$. Net increase $= 1$.
2. **Cross-multiply directly**:
   

$$
\mathbf{1}\,\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + \mathbf{6}\,\text{Fe}^{2+} \to 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 6\text{Fe}^{3+}
$$

3. **Balance Oxygens with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, then Hydrogens with $\text{H}^+$**:
   - Left has $7\text{ O} \implies \text{Add } 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to right.
   - Right has $14\text{ H} \implies \text{Add } 14\text{H}^+$ to left.
   - **Result**: $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 6\text{Fe}^{2+} + 14\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 6\text{Fe}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Solved in $< 20$ seconds).

***

### 1.2 Basic Medium Shortcut: The "Fake Acidic First" Trick
Balancing with $\text{OH}^-$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ simultaneously in basic medium is where $>60\%$ of students commit sign and stoichiometric errors.
1. Balance the entire skeletal equation using the standard acidic medium method ($\text{H}^+$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
2. Add $\text{OH}^-$ ions **equal to the number of $\text{H}^+$ ions** to **both sides** of the equation.
3. Combine $\text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^- \to \text{H}_2\text{O}$ on one side, then cancel common $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules across the arrow.

***

### 1.3 Rapid $n$-Factor Formula Engine
Equivalence calculations require rapid identification of the $n$-factor ($z$-factor):

$$
\text{Equivalent Mass } (E) = \frac{\text{Molar Mass } (M)}{n\text{-factor}}
$$

| Species & Condition | Redox Change | $n$-factor | Fast Formula |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\text{KMnO}_4$ in **Acidic** Medium | $\text{Mn}^{+7} \to \text{Mn}^{+2}$ | **5** | $E = M/5$ |
| $\text{KMnO}_4$ in **Neutral / Faintly Alkaline** | $\text{Mn}^{+7} \to \text{MnO}_2\,(\text{Mn}^{+4})$ | **3** | $E = M/3$ |
| $\text{KMnO}_4$ in **Strongly Alkaline** | $\text{Mn}^{+7} \to \text{MnO}_4^{2-}\,(\text{Mn}^{+6})$ | **1** | $E = M/1$ |
| $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ in **Acidic** Medium | $\text{Cr}_2^{+6} \to 2\text{Cr}^{+3}$ | **6** | $E = M/6$ |

| $\text{FeC}_2\text{O}_4$ (Ferrous Oxalate) oxidized to $\text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{CO}_2$ | $\text{Fe}^{2+} \to \text{Fe}^{3+}$ ($1e^-$) 
 $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \to 2\text{CO}_2$ ($2e^-$) | **3** ($1 + 2$) | $E = M/3$ |

| $\text{Fe}_2(\text{C}_2\text{O}_4)_3$ oxidized to $\text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{CO}_2$ | Only $\text{C}$ oxidizes: $3 \times 2 = 6e^-$ | **6** | $E = M/6$ |

### 1.4 Disproportionation vs. Comproportionation Diagnostic
- **Disproportionation**: Same element in a single intermediate oxidation state simultaneously undergoes oxidation and reduction.
  

$$
\text{Condition: } \text{O.N.}_{\text{lowest}} < \text{O.N.}_{\text{reactant}} < \text{O.N.}_{\text{highest}}
$$

  *Example*: $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2^{(-1)} \to 2\text{H}_2\text{O}^{(-2)} + \text{O}_2^{(0)}$
- **Comproportionation (Reverse Disproportionation)**: Two species containing the same element in different oxidation states react to yield a single intermediate oxidation state.
  *Example*: $\text{Ag}^{2+} + \text{Ag}^{(0)} \to 2\text{Ag}^{+}$ or $\text{IO}_3^- + 5\text{I}^- + 6\text{H}^+ \to 3\text{I}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$

***

### 1.5 Fractional Oxidation State Resolution
When formal calculation yields a fractional oxidation number, it represents the **average** of non-equivalent atoms in the structural formula:
- **$\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$** (O.N. $= +8/3$): Stoichiometric mixture of $\text{FeO} \cdot \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \implies \text{Fe(II)}$ and $2\text{ Fe(III)}$.
- **$\text{Mn}_3\text{O}_4$** (O.N. $= +8/3$): Stoichiometric mixture of $2\text{MnO} \cdot \text{MnO}_2 \implies 2\text{ Mn(II)}$ and $1\text{ Mn(IV)}$.
- **$\text{Pb}_3\text{O}_4$** (Red Lead, O.N. $= +8/3$): $2\text{PbO} \cdot \text{PbO}_2 \implies 2\text{ Pb(II)}$ and $1\text{ Pb(IV)}$.
- **$\text{C}_3\text{O}_2$** (Carbon suboxide, O.N. $= +4/3$): Structure $\text{O}=\overset{+2}{\text{C}}=\overset{0}{\text{C}}=\overset{+2}{\text{C}}=\text{O}$.
- **$\text{Br}_3\text{O}_8$** (Tribromooctaoxide, O.N. $= +16/3$): Structure $\text{O}_3\overset{+6}{\text{Br}}-\overset{+4}{\text{Br}}(\text{O}_2)-\overset{+6}{\text{Br}}\text{O}_3$.
- **$\text{S}_4\text{O}_6^{2-}$** (Tetrathionate ion, O.N. $= +2.5$): Structure $^-\text{O}_3\overset{+5}{\text{S}}-\overset{0}{\text{S}}-\overset{0}{\text{S}}-\overset{+5}{\text{S}}\text{O}_3^-$. Two central sulfur atoms are $0$, terminal sulfurs are $+5$.

## 2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

### 2.1 The CBSE Marking Scheme Hierarchy
CBSE evaluates long-form balancing questions (3 Marks) using strict step-marking:
1. **Step 1 (½ Mark)**: Correctly splitting the skeletal equation into oxidation and reduction half-reactions with explicit oxidation numbers indicated.
2. **Step 2 (1 Mark)**: Correct balancing of atoms other than $\text{O}$ and $\text{H}$, followed by balancing electrons transferred.
3. **Step 3 (1 Mark)**: Correct addition of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{H}^+$ (or $\text{OH}^-$) according to the specified reaction medium.
4. **Step 4 (½ Mark)**: Final net balanced equation with proper state symbols ($\text{aq}$, $\text{l}$, $\text{s}$, $\text{g}$) and total charges verified on both sides.

> [!WARNING]
> **Zero Mark Trigger**: If a student balances a redox equation purely by algebraic trial-and-error without showing the **Ion-Electron Method** or **Oxidation Number Method** steps when explicitly asked in the prompt, examiners are instructed to award **0 out of 3**.

***

### 2.2 Top 7 Board Deduction Pitfalls

#### Pitfall 1: Oxidation State Representation vs. Ionic Charge
- **Oxidation State/Number**: Sign precedes the numeral $\to \mathbf{+2, -1, +6}$.
- **Electrovalent/Ionic Charge**: Numeral precedes the sign $\to \mathbf{2+, 1-, 6+}$.
- *CBSE Deduction*: Writing "the oxidation state of $\text{Mn}$ in $\text{MnO}_4^-$ is $7+$" can trigger a ½ mark deduction in precise evaluation centers. Always write **$+7$**.

#### Pitfall 2: Stock Notation Violations
- When asked to represent a compound using Stock notation, write the oxidation state in **Roman numerals enclosed in parentheses directly after the metal symbol** without any space:
  - Correct: $\text{AuCl}_3 \to \text{Au(III)Cl}_3$; $\text{SnCl}_4 \to \text{Sn(IV)Cl}_4$; $\text{MnO}_2 \to \text{Mn(IV)O}_2$; $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \to \text{Fe}_2(\text{III})\text{O}_3$ or $\text{Fe(III)}_2\text{O}_3$.
  - Wrong: $\text{Au(3)Cl}_3$, $\text{Au}^{+3}\text{Cl}_3$, or writing Stock notation for non-metals (e.g., $\text{CO}_2 \to \text{C(IV)O}_2$ is prohibited by NCERT conventions for non-metals).

#### Pitfall 3: Forgetting to Multiply Atoms Before Electron Count
- In $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \to \text{Cr}^{3+}$, students write:
  

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 3e^- \to \text{Cr}^{3+} \quad \mathbf{[FATAL ERROR]}
$$

- You **must** balance Cr atoms first:
  

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 6e^- \to 2\text{Cr}^{3+}
$$

#### Pitfall 4: Misidentifying the Disproportionation Limit
- Students often label $\text{HNO}_3$ or $\text{KMnO}_4$ as capable of disproportionation.
- **Rule**: An element in its **highest** or **lowest** oxidation state **CANNOT** disproportionate.
  - $\text{N}$ in $\text{HNO}_3$ ($+5$, max group state) $\implies$ acts strictly as an oxidizing agent.
  - $\text{N}$ in $\text{HNO}_2$ ($+3$, intermediate) $\implies$ readily disproportionates:
    

$$
3\text{HNO}_2 \to \text{HNO}_3 + 2\text{NO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

#### Pitfall 5: Omitting State Symbols and Medium Identification
- Writing $\text{H}^+$ in a reaction specified in **basic medium** yields an immediate 1-mark penalty. All final equations in basic medium must be stripped of $\text{H}^+$ and display $\text{OH}^-$.

#### Pitfall 6: Galvanic Cell Representation Inversion
- Cell notation rule: **Anode | Anolyte || Catholyte | Cathode** (Mnemonic: **LOAN** $\to$ **L**eft, **O**xidation, **A**node, **N**egative).
- For Daniell Cell:
  - Correct: $\text{Zn(s)} \mid \text{Zn}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \parallel \text{Cu}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \mid \text{Cu(s)}$
  - Inverted / Wrong: $\text{Cu(s)} \mid \text{Cu}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \parallel \text{Zn}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \mid \text{Zn(s)}$ (Examiner will deduct 1 full mark).
  - For inert electrodes (e.g., $\text{Pt}$ or graphite): Must be included when gas or ions are involved:
    

$$
\text{Pt(s)} \mid \text{H}_2(\text{g}, 1\,\text{bar}) \mid \text{H}^+(\text{aq}, 1\,\text{M}) \parallel \text{Fe}^{3+}(\text{aq}), \text{Fe}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \mid \text{Pt(s)}
$$

#### Pitfall 7: Thermodynamic Spontaneity Sign Confusion
- $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$
- For a spontaneous redox reaction:
  - $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0$ (**positive**)
  - $\Delta G^\circ < 0$ (**negative**)
- Students frequently write "$\Delta G^\circ$ must be positive for feasibility." This causes a direct 1-mark deduction in reasoning questions.

## 3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT In-Text Lines & High-Yield Truths)

### 3.1 NCERT Structural Anomalies (Peroxy Linkages)
Conventional chemical calculation fails whenever peroxy linkages ($\text{O}-\text{O}$) are present because each peroxy oxygen possesses an oxidation number of $\mathbf{-1}$, not $-2$.

```
1. Caro's Acid (Peroxomonosulphuric acid, H2SO4):
   Formula: H2SO5
   Algebraic Trap: 2(+1) + x + 5(-2) = 0 => x = +8 (IMPOSSIBLE: S max is +6)
   Structure: H-O-S(=O)2-O-O-H
   Calculation: 2(H) + 1(S) + 3(oxide O) + 2(peroxide O) = 0
                2(+1) + x + 3(-2) + 2(-1) = 0 => x = +6

2. Marshall's Acid (Peroxodisulphuric acid, H2S2O8):
   Algebraic Trap: 2(+1) + 2x + 8(-2) = 0 => 2x = 14 => x = +7 (IMPOSSIBLE)
   Structure: HO-S(=O)2-O-O-S(=O)2-OH
   Calculation: 2(+1) + 2x + 6(-2) + 2(-1) = 0 => x = +6

3. Chromium Pentoxide (CrO5, Butterfly Structure):
   Algebraic Trap: x + 5(-2) = 0 => x = +10 (IMPOSSIBLE: Cr max is +6)
   Structure: Four oxygens form two peroxo rings coordinated to Cr(=O)
   Calculation: x + 1(-2) + 4(-1) = 0 => x = +6 (Deep blue in ether)
```

***

### 3.2 Halogen Redox Hierarchy (NCERT Core Rules)
1. **Fluorine ($\text{F}$)**:
   - Most electronegative element; shows **only $-1$** oxidation state in all its compounds.
   - **Never exhibits positive oxidation states**. It cannot form oxoacids like $\text{HOClO}_3$. It forms only one oxoacid: $\text{HOF}$ (Fluoric(I) acid), where oxidation state of $\text{F}$ is **$-1$** and $\text{O}$ is **$0$**.
   - Strongest oxidizing agent: $\text{F}_2 + 2e^- \to 2\text{F}^-$ ($E^\circ = +2.87\,\text{V}$). It oxidizes water to oxygen/ozone:
     

$$
2\text{F}_2(\text{g}) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \to 4\text{HF}(\text{aq}) + \text{O}_2(\text{g})
$$

2. **Other Halogens ($\text{Cl, Br, I}$)**:
   - Exhibit positive oxidation states ($+1, +3, +5, +7$) due to presence of vacant $d$-orbitals (forming oxoacids and interhalogens).
   - Down the group, oxidizing ability decreases: $\text{F}_2 > \text{Cl}_2 > \text{Br}_2 > \text{I}_2$.
   - $\text{Cl}_2$ oxidizes $\text{Br}^-$ to $\text{Br}_2$ and $\text{I}^-$ to $\text{I}_2$; $\text{I}_2$ cannot oxidize $\text{Cl}^-$ or $\text{Br}^-$.

***

### 3.3 The Electrochemical Series: High-Yield NCERT Facts
- Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE): Potential arbitrarily taken as **$0.00\,\text{V}$ at all temperatures**.
- **Negative $E^\circ$**: Element is a stronger reducing agent than $\text{H}_2$ gas.
  - $\text{Li}^+/\text{Li} = -3.05\,\text{V}$ (Most powerful reducing agent in aqueous medium due to its exceptionally high hydration enthalpy, compensating for its high ionization enthalpy).
- **Positive $E^\circ$**: Element is a weaker reducing agent than $\text{H}_2$ (or its oxidized form is a stronger oxidizing agent).
  - $\text{F}_2/\text{F}^- = +2.87\,\text{V}$ (Most powerful oxidizing agent).
- **Displacement Rules**:
  - Metals with lower reduction potentials (more negative $E^\circ$) displace metals with higher reduction potentials from their salt solutions.
  - Any metal having negative $E^\circ$ reduces $\text{H}^+(\text{aq})$ to $\text{H}_2(\text{g})$ (e.g., $\text{Zn}, \text{Fe}, \text{Mg}$).
  - Metals with positive $E^\circ$ (e.g., $\text{Cu} = +0.34\,\text{V}, \text{Ag} = +0.80\,\text{V}$) **cannot** liberate $\text{H}_2$ from dilute non-oxidizing acids like $\text{HCl}$ or dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.

### 4.3 High-Yield Board Exam Reaction Balancing Checklist

#### A. Acidic Medium
1. **Permanganate with Ferrous**:
   

$$
\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{Fe}^{2+} + 8\text{H}^+ \to \text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{Fe}^{3+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} + 16\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 10\text{CO}_2 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

3. **Dichromate with Sulfite**:
   

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 3\text{SO}_3^{2-} + 8\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{SO}_4^{2-} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

4. **Dichromate with Iodide**:
   

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 6\text{I}^- + 14\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{I}_2 + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}

5. **Permanganate with Hydrogen Peroxide**:
   

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 + 6\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{O}_2 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

#### B. Basic / Faintly Alkaline Medium
1. **Permanganate with Iodide (Converts to Iodate, NOT $\text{I}_2$)**:
   

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{I}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{MnO}_2 + \text{IO}_3^- + 2\text{OH}^-

   *(Critical trap: In acidic medium, $\text{I}^- \to \text{I}_2$; in neutral/alkaline medium, $\text{I}^- \to \text{IO}_3^-$).*
2. **Permanganate with Thiosulfate**:
   

$$
8\text{MnO}_4^- + 3\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to 8\text{MnO}_2 + 6\text{SO}_4^{2-} + 2\text{OH}^-
$$

3. **Disproportionation of White Phosphorus**:
   

$$
\text{P}_4 + 3\text{OH}^- + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{PH}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^- \quad (\text{Phosphine} + \text{Hypophosphite})

4. **Disproportionation of Chlorine in Cold, Dilute Alkali**:
   

$$
\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{OH}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cold, dilute}} \text{Cl}^- + \text{ClO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{Chloride} + \text{Hypochlorite})

$$
3\text{Cl}_2 + 6\text{OH}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Hot, conc.}} 5\text{Cl}^- + \text{ClO}_3^- + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{Chloride} + \text{Chlorate})
$$
