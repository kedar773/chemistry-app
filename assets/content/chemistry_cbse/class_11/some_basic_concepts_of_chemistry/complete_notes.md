# Module 1: Nature & Classification of Matter

### 1.1 Fundamental Definition of Matter ⭐⭐⭐
Matter is defined as anything that possesses mass, occupies space (volume), and whose presence can be felt by one or more of our five senses.

At the macroscopic level, matter exists primarily in three physical states under ordinary conditions of temperature ($T$) and pressure ($P$):
1. **Solid:** Possesses a definite shape and a definite volume. Intermolecular forces of attraction are significantly stronger than thermal energy. Particles oscillate only about their mean equilibrium positions; constituent particles exhibit minimal translational freedom.
2. **Liquid:** Possesses a definite volume but no definite shape (takes the shape of the container). Intermolecular forces are intermediate—strong enough to hold particles in proximity, yet weak enough to permit translational fluid motion.
3. **Gas:** Possesses neither a definite volume nor a definite shape. Particles exhibit complete translational, rotational, and vibrational freedom. Intermolecular forces of attraction are negligible compared to thermal kinetic energy at ambient temperatures.

#### Interconversion of States of Matter
The physical state of a substance is governed by a balance between two opposing factors:
- **Intermolecular Attractive Forces:** Tend to keep the molecules together.
- **Thermal Energy:** Tend to keep the molecules apart by inducing kinetic motion.

$$
\text{Solid} \underset{\text{Cool}}{\overset{\text{Heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Liquid} \underset{\text{Cool}}{\overset{\text{Heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Gas}
$$

### 1.2 Macroscopic Classification of Matter ⭐⭐⭐⭐

At the macroscopic and chemical level, matter is classified into **Mixtures** and **Pure Substances**:

```
                                  MATTER
                                    │
         ┌──────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                     ▼
     Mixtures                                           Pure Substances
  (Variable Composition;                              (Definite Composition;
 Physical Separation Possible)                      Chemical Separation Only)
         │                                                     │
   ┌─────┴─────┐                                         ┌─────┴─────┐
   ▼           ▼                                         ▼           ▼
Homogeneous  Heterogeneous                           Elements    Compounds
 (Uniform)  (Non-uniform)                           (1 Atom type)(Fixed Ratio)
```

#### 1. Pure Substances
A sample of matter that has a definite (constant) chemical composition and distinct chemical/physical properties throughout.
- **Elements:** 
  - Pure substances consisting of only one type of atom or entity.
  - Cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by ordinary chemical reactions.
  - *Examples:* Monoatomic ($\text{He}, \text{Ne}, \text{Ar}$), Diatomic ($\text{H}_2, \text{N}_2, \text{O}_2$), Polyatomic ($\text{P}_4, \text{S}_8$).
- **Compounds:** 
  - Formed when two or more atoms of different elements combine chemically in a **fixed and definite ratio by mass**.
  - The constituent elements lose their individual chemical identities completely.
  - *Example:* Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$); Hydrogen is combustible and Oxygen is a supporter of combustion, yet their compound, water, acts as a fire extinguisher.
  - Constituents cannot be separated by physical methods (e.g., filtration, distillation, magnetic separation); they require chemical or electrochemical cleavage.

#### 2. Mixtures
Consist of two or more pure substances present in any arbitrary ratio, wherein each constituent retains its individual chemical identity.
- **Homogeneous Mixtures (Solutions):**
  - Uniform composition and properties throughout the entire bulk phase.
  - No boundary of separation is visible under optical microscopy.
  - *Examples:* Pure air, aqueous solution of glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\text{ (aq)}$), brass ($\text{Cu}-\text{Zn}$ solid solution).
- **Heterogeneous Mixtures:**
  - Non-uniform composition; consists of two or more physically distinct phases separated by phase boundaries.
  - *Examples:* Suspension of sand in water, oil-water emulsions, smoke (solid aerosol).

#### Temperature Scales & Mathematical Interconversions:
Temperature is the measure of the thermal state or degree of hotness of a body.

$$
\boxed{T\text{ (in K)} = T\text{ (in }^\circ\text{C)} + 273.15}
$$

$$
\boxed{^\circ\text{F} = \frac{9}{5}\left(^\circ\text{C}\right) + 32}
$$

$$
\boxed{^\circ\text{C} = \frac{5}{9}\left(^\circ\text{F} - 32\right)}
$$

> **Derivation of the Cross-Over Point:**
> To find the exact temperature where the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales read identical numerical values:
> 

$$
\text{Let } ^\circ\text{F} = ^\circ\text{C} = x
$$

> 

$$
x = \frac{9}{5}x + 32 \implies x - \frac{9}{5}x = 32 \implies -\frac{4}{5}x = 32 \implies x = \frac{32 \times (-5)}{4} = -40
$$

> 

$$
\boxed{-40^\circ\text{C} = -40^\circ\text{F}}
$$

> **Critical Note on Negative Temperatures:**
> The Kelvin scale is thermodynamic and absolute. Since zero Kelvin ($0\text{ K}$, absolute zero) is the theoretical lower limit of temperature where classical molecular kinetic motion ceases:
> 

$$
\boxed{T\text{ (in K)} \ge 0 \quad (\text{Negative Kelvin temperatures do not exist in classical systems})}
$$

### 2.2 Scientific Notation ⭐⭐⭐
Any number is expressed in exponential notation:

$$
N \times 10^n
$$

Where:
- $N$ is a number (known as digit term) such that $1.000\dots \le N < 10$.
- $n$ is an integer exponent (positive, negative, or zero).

*Examples:*
- $0.0000456\text{ g} = 4.56 \times 10^{-5}\text{ g}$
- $602\,200\,000\,000\,000\,000\,000\,000 = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$

### 2.3 Precision versus Accuracy ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Accuracy:** The agreement of a particular value to the true or accepted standard value of the quantity measured.
  

$$
\text{Absolute Error} = |\text{Observed Mean} - \text{True Value}|
$$

- **Precision:** The closeness of various measurements for the identical quantity to one another (reproducibility/repeatability of the measurement technique).

```
   Low Precision, Low Accuracy       High Precision, Low Accuracy      High Precision, High Accuracy
             (Poor)                        (Systematic Error)                    (Ideal)
         ┌─────────────┐                    ┌─────────────┐                 ┌─────────────┐
         │  x          │                    │             │                 │             │
         │       x     │                    │    x x      │                 │     xxx     │
         │    x     x  │                    │    xxx      │                 │     xxx     │
         │             │                    │             │                 │             │
         └─────────────┘                    └─────────────┘                 └─────────────┘
         Target: Center                     Target: Center                  Target: Center
```

*Illustration:* 
Let the true mass of a standard titration sample be **$2.000\text{ g}$**.
- **Analyst A:** $1.95\text{ g}, 1.93\text{ g}$ $\implies$ Average = $1.940\text{ g}$ (Neither accurate nor precise).
- **Analyst B:** $1.940\text{ g}, 1.942\text{ g}$ $\implies$ Average = $1.941\text{ g}$ (High precision, poor accuracy $\rightarrow$ indicates determinate/systematic error).
- **Analyst C:** $2.001\text{ g}, 1.999\text{ g}$ $\implies$ Average = $2.000\text{ g}$ (High precision and high accuracy).

### 2.4 Significant Figures: Rigorous Rules & Operational Arithmetic ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The total number of digits in a reported measurement including the last digit whose value is uncertain is called the number of **Significant Figures**.

$$
\text{Measured Value} = \text{Certain Digits} + \text{One Doubtful (Estimated) Digit}
$$

#### The 6 Core Rules for Determining Significant Figures:
1. **All non-zero digits are significant.**
   - $285\text{ cm} \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
   - $0.25\text{ mL} \rightarrow 2\text{ significant figures}$
2. **Zeros between two non-zero digits are always significant.**
   - $2.005\text{ kg} \rightarrow 4\text{ significant figures}$
   - $50.008\text{ s} \rightarrow 5\text{ significant figures}$
3. **Leading zeros (zeros to the left of the first non-zero digit) are NEVER significant.** They serve only to locate the position of the decimal point.
   - $0.03\text{ g} \rightarrow 1\text{ significant figure}$
   - $0.0052\text{ m} \rightarrow 2\text{ significant figures}$
4. **Trailing zeros (zeros at the end or to the right of a number):**
   - If located **after the decimal point**, they are **significant**.
     - $0.200\text{ g} \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
     - $4.000\text{ mL} \rightarrow 4\text{ significant figures}$
   - If located in a number **without a decimal point**, trailing zeros are ambiguous and not considered significant unless specified by scientific notation or an explicit terminal decimal point.
     - $100 \rightarrow 1\text{ significant figure (ambiguous)}$
     - $100. \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures (terminal decimal indicates precision)}$
     - $1.00 \times 10^2 \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
5. **Counting numbers of exact entities have an INFINITE ($\infty$) number of significant figures**, as they represent exact integers with zero experimental uncertainty.
   - $2\text{ balls} = 2.000000\dots \rightarrow \infty\text{ significant figures}$
   - $20\text{ eggs} = 20.00000\dots \rightarrow \infty\text{ significant figures}$
   - $\pi = 3.14159265\dots \rightarrow \infty\text{ significant figures}$
   - Standard stoichiometry coefficients in balanced equations have infinite precision.
6. **Exponential terms in scientific notation do not influence the count of significant figures.**
   - In $N \times 10^n$, the number of significant figures is dictated entirely by $N$.
   - $4.01 \times 10^2 \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
   - $8.256 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 4\text{ significant figures}$

***

### 2.5 Rules for Mathematical Operations with Significant Figures

#### Addition and Subtraction
The final calculated result cannot have more digits to the right of the decimal point than any of the original numbers involved in the operation.

$$
\text{Precision limit} = \min(\text{decimal places of operands})
$$

> **Worked Example:**
> Evaluate the sum: $12.11\text{ (2 decimal places)} + 18.0\text{ (1 decimal place)} + 1.012\text{ (3 decimal places)}$
> 

$$
\begin{array}{r@{\quad}l}
> 12.11 & \\
> 18.0\phantom{0} & \leftarrow Limiting operand (1 decimal place) \\
> +\; 1.012 & \\
> \hline
> 31.122 & \xrightarrow{\text{Round to 1 decimal place}} \mathbf{31.1}
> \end{array}
$$

#### Multiplication and Division
The final result must be reported with no more significant figures than are present in the measurement with the **fewest significant figures**.

$$
\text{Precision limit} = \min(\text{total significant figures of operands})
$$

> **Worked Example:**
> Multiply $2.5\text{ (2 significant figures)}$ by $1.25\text{ (3 significant figures)}$:
> 

$$
2.5 \times 1.25 = 3.125
$$

> Limiting term has 2 significant figures. 
> Result rounded to 2 significant figures: $\mathbf{3.1}$.

***

### 2.6 Formal Rules for Rounding Off
1. If the leftmost insignificant digit to be dropped is **greater than 5**, the preceding digit is increased by 1:
   

$$
1.386 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 3 sig. fig.}} 1.39
$$

2. If the leftmost insignificant digit to be dropped is **less than 5**, the preceding digit is left unchanged:
   

$$
4.334 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 3 sig. fig.}} 4.33
$$

3. If the digit to be dropped is **exactly 5** (or $5$ followed exclusively by zeros):
   - **Condition A:** If the preceding digit is **even**, it is left unchanged.
     

$$
6.250 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 2 sig. fig.}} 6.2
$$

   - **Condition B:** If the preceding digit is **odd**, it is increased by 1 (to make it even).
     

$$
3.750 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 2 sig. fig.}} 3.8
$$

   - **Condition C:** If the 5 is followed by any non-zero digit (e.g., $51, 501$), round up unconditionally:
     

$$
6.251 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 2 sig. fig.}} 6.3
$$

### 2.7 Dimensional Analysis (Factor-Label or Unit-Conversion Method) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Every physical quantity is expressed as the product of a numerical value and a physical unit:

$$
\text{Physical Quantity} = \text{Numerical Value} \times \text{Unit}
$$

To convert a quantity from one unit system to another, multiply by an identity fraction termed the **Unit Conversion Factor**:

$$
\text{Conversion Factor} = \frac{\text{Target Unit}}{\text{Given Unit}} = 1
$$

#### Step-by-Step Multi-Stage Dimensional Conversions:

##### Problem A: Convert $3\text{ days}$ into seconds.
1. Identification of fundamental unit equivalences:
   

$$
1\text{ day} = 24\text{ h} \implies \frac{24\text{ h}}{1\text{ day}} = 1
$$

   

$$
1\text{ h} = 60\text{ min} \implies \frac{60\text{ min}}{1\text{ h}} = 1
$$

   

$$
1\text{ min} = 60\text{ s} \implies \frac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}} = 1
$$

2. Dimensional chain multiplication:
   

$$
t = 3\text{ days} \times \left(\frac{24\text{ h}}{1\text{ day}}\right) \times \left(\frac{60\text{ min}}{1\text{ h}}\right) \times \left(\frac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}}\right)
$$

   

$$
t = 3 \times 24 \times 60 \times 60\text{ s} = \mathbf{259\,200\text{ s}} = \mathbf{2.592 \times 10^5\text{ s}}
$$

##### Problem B: Convert density $\rho = 13.6\text{ g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$ into standard SI units ($\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$).
1. Equivalences:
   

$$
1\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ g} = 10^3\text{ g} \implies \left(\frac{1\text{ kg}}{10^3\text{ g}}\right) = 1
$$

   

$$
1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm} = 10^2\text{ cm} \implies \left(\frac{10^2\text{ cm}}{1\text{ m}}\right) = 1 \implies \left(\frac{10^2\text{ cm}}{1\text{ m}}\right)^3 = \frac{10^6\text{ cm}^3}{1\text{ m}^3} = 1
$$

2. Chain multiplication:
   

$$
\rho = 13.6\left(\frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}\right) \times \left(\frac{1\text{ kg}}{10^3\text{ g}}\right) \times \left(\frac{10^6\text{ cm}^3}{1\text{ m}^3}\right)
$$

   

$$
\rho = 13.6 \times \frac{10^6}{10^3}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3} = 13.6 \times 10^3\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3} = \mathbf{1.36 \times 10^4\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}}
$$

### 3.1 Law of Conservation of Mass ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Antoine Lavoisier (1789) — Father of Modern Chemistry.
- **Statement:** 
  > In all physical transformations and chemical reactions, the total mass of the products formed is equal to the total mass of the reactants consumed. Matter can neither be created nor destroyed.
- **Mathematical Form:**
  

$$
\boxed{\sum m_{\text{reactants consumed}} = \sum m_{\text{products formed}}}
$$

  

$$
\Delta m = m_{\text{final}} - m_{\text{initial}} = 0
$$

- **Experimental Verification (Landolt's Tube Experiment):**
  An H-shaped glass vessel (Landolt tube) containing solutions of $\text{AgNO}_3$ in one arm and $\text{NaCl}$ (or $\text{HCl}$) in the other arm was sealed and weighed accurately. The solutions were mixed by inversion to precipitate Silver Chloride:
  

$$
\text{AgNO}_{3\text{ (aq)}} + \text{NaCl}_{\text{(aq)}} \longrightarrow \text{AgCl}_{\text{(s)}} \downarrow (\text{curdy white}) + \text{NaNO}_{3\text{ (aq)}}
$$

  Re-weighing the sealed system showed no detectable change in mass within experimental limits.

- **Modern Nuclear Limitation (Einstein Mass-Energy Equivalence):**
  In nuclear fission and fusion reactions, mass is not conserved independently. Mass ($m$) and energy ($E$) are interconvertible:
  

$$
\Delta E = \Delta m \cdot c^2
$$

  Where:
  - $c = 2.9979 \times 10^8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ (speed of light in vacuum).
  - In conventional chemical reactions, $\Delta E \approx 10^2 - 10^3\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$. The corresponding loss of mass $\Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2} \approx 10^{-11}\text{ kg}$, which is completely imperceptible on analytical balances. Thus, Lavoisier’s law holds with extreme fidelity for all standard chemical processes.

### 3.2 Law of Definite Proportions (or Definite Composition) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Joseph Proust (1799).
- **Statement:** 
  > A given pure chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of its constituent elements by mass, irrespective of its source, method of preparation, or geographical origin.

- **Proust's Classic Experiment on Cupric Carbonate ($\text{CuCO}_3$):**
  Proust analyzed two distinct samples of cupric carbonate—one of natural mineral origin (malachite extract) and one synthesized in the laboratory via wet precipitation:

| Sample Type | $\% \text{ Cu}$ | $\% \text{ C}$ | $\% \text{ O}$ |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Natural Mineral Sample** | $51.35\%$ | $9.74\%$ | $38.91\%$ |
| **Synthetic Laboratory Sample** | $51.35\%$ | $9.74\%$ | $38.91\%$ |

  The exact matching of mass percentages verified that composition is fixed.

- **Modern Limitations & Failures:**
  1. **Existence of Isotopes:** 
     If a compound is prepared using different isotopes of an element, the mass ratio changes.
     - Carbon dioxide synthesized from $^{12}\text{C}$:
       

$$
\text{Mass ratio } (m_{\text{C}} : m_{\text{O}}) = 12 : 32 = 3 : 8 = 1 : 2.667
$$

     - Carbon dioxide synthesized from $^{14}\text{C}$:
       

$$
\text{Mass ratio } (m_{\text{C}} : m_{\text{O}}) = 14 : 32 = 7 : 16 = 1 : 2.286
$$

     The mass ratio is not invariant when isotopic abundance varies.
  2. **Non-Stoichiometric Compounds (Berthollides):**
     Transition metal oxides, sulfides, and hydrides often exhibit crystal lattice defects resulting in variable composition.
     - *Example:* Wüstite (Ferrous Oxide), represented stoichiometrically as $\text{FeO}$, actually exists as $\text{Fe}_{x}\text{O}$ where $x$ varies continuously from $0.93$ to $0.96$ due to missing $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ lattice sites compensated by $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ions:
       

$$
\text{Composition} = \text{Fe}_{0.93}\text{O} \text{ to } \text{Fe}_{0.96}\text{O}
$$

     These do not adhere to fixed, integer mass ratios.

***

### 3.3 Law of Multiple Proportions ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** John Dalton (1803).
- **Statement:** 
  > When two elements combine to form more than one chemical compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in the ratio of small whole numbers.

- **Mathematical Formulation:**
  Let element $A$ and element $B$ combine to form $k$ distinct compounds: $\text{Comp}_1, \text{Comp}_2, \dots, \text{Comp}_k$.
  Fix the mass of element $A$ at a constant quantity $m_{A0}$.
  If the corresponding combining masses of element $B$ are $m_{B1}, m_{B2}, \dots, m_{Bk}$, then:
  

$$
\boxed{m_{B1} : m_{B2} : \dots : m_{Bk} = a_1 : a_2 : \dots : a_k \quad (a_i \in \mathbb{Z}^+)}
$$

#### Textbook Examples with Tabular Proofs:

##### Example 1: Oxides of Nitrogen ($\text{N}$ and $\text{O}$)
Nitrogen forms five stable oxides: Nitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), Nitric oxide ($\text{NO}$ or $\text{N}_2\text{O}_2$), Dinitrogen trioxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$), Dinitrogen tetroxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$), and Dinitrogen pentoxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$).
Fixing the mass of Nitrogen at $28.00\text{ g}$:

| Compound | Formula | Mass of $\text{N}$ ($\text{g}$) | Mass of $\text{O}$ ($\text{g}$) | Mass of $\text{O}$ combining with fixed $14.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{N}$ ($\text{g}$) | Ratio of Oxygen Masses |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Nitrous oxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ | $28.0$ | $16.0$ | $8.0$ | **1** |
| Nitric oxide | $\text{NO}$ | $14.0$ | $16.0$ | $16.0$ | **2** |
| Dinitrogen trioxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$ | $28.0$ | $48.0$ | $24.0$ | **3** |
| Dinitrogen tetroxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$ | $28.0$ | $64.0$ | $32.0$ | **4** |
| Dinitrogen pentoxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ | $28.0$ | $80.0$ | $40.0$ | **5** |

$$
\text{Ratio of Oxygen masses} = 8.0 : 16.0 : 24.0 : 32.0 : 40.0 = \mathbf{1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5}
$$

This is an integer ratio, verifying the law.

##### Example 2: Oxides of Carbon ($\text{CO}$ and $\text{CO}_2$)
- Carbon Monoxide ($\text{CO}$): $12\text{ g}$ Carbon combines with $16\text{ g}$ Oxygen.
- Carbon Dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$): $12\text{ g}$ Carbon combines with $32\text{ g}$ Oxygen.
- For a fixed mass of Carbon ($12\text{ g}$), the ratio of masses of Oxygen is:
  

$$
\frac{m_{\text{O in CO}_2}}{m_{\text{O in CO}}} = \frac{32\text{ g}}{16\text{ g}} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{1} = 2 : 1}
$$

### 3.4 Law of Reciprocal Proportions (Law of Equivalent Proportions) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Jeremias Richter (1792).
- **Statement:** 
  > The ratio of the masses of two elements $A$ and $B$ which combine separately with a fixed mass of a third element $C$ is either the same as, or a simple whole-number multiple of, the ratio of the masses in which $A$ and $B$ combine directly with each other.

- **Mathematical Formulation:**
  Let element $A$ combine with element $C$ to form compound $AC$, where mass ratio is:
  

$$
\left(\frac{m_A}{m_C}\right)_1
$$

  Let element $B$ combine with element $C$ to form compound $BC$, where mass ratio is:
  

$$
\left(\frac{m_B}{m_C}\right)_2
$$

  Normalize the mass of $C$ to a common value. The resulting ratio of masses of $A$ and $B$ is:
  

$$
R_1 = \frac{m_A}{m_B}
$$

  Now, let $A$ and $B$ combine directly to form compound $A_x B_y$, where their direct combining mass ratio is:
  

$$
R_2 = \left(\frac{m_A}{m_B}\right)_{\text{direct}}
$$

  Then, according to Richter's Law:
  

$$
\boxed{\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{p}{q} \quad (p, q \in \mathbb{Z}^+)}
$$

```
                                Element C
                              (e.g., Oxygen)
                                ╱        ╲
                               ╱          ╲
                              ╱            ╲
                             ╱              ╲
                    Compound AC            Compound BC
                    (e.g., SO2)            (e.g., H2O)
                           ╱                  ╲
                          ╱                    ╲
                         ▼                      ▼
                   Element A ════════════════ Element B
                  (e.g., Sulfur) Compound AB  (e.g., Hydrogen)
                                 (e.g., H2S)
```

#### Step-by-Step Proof via Triangle System ($\text{C} = \text{Oxygen}$, $\text{A} = \text{Sulfur}$, $\text{B} = \text{Hydrogen}$):
1. **Reaction 1 (A with C):** $\text{S}$ combines with $\text{O}$ to form $\text{SO}_2$.
   - In $\text{SO}_2$: $32\text{ g}$ of Sulfur combines with $32\text{ g}$ of Oxygen.
   - Ratio: $\frac{m_{\text{S}}}{m_{\text{O}}} = \frac{32}{32} = \frac{1}{1}$.
   - Mass of $\text{S}$ combining with $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{O} = \mathbf{1.0\text{ g}}$.

2. **Reaction 2 (B with C):** $\text{H}$ combines with $\text{O}$ to form $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
   - In $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $2\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen combines with $16\text{ g}$ of Oxygen.
   - Mass of $\text{H}$ combining with $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{O} = \frac{2}{16}\text{ g} = \frac{1}{8}\text{ g} = \mathbf{0.125\text{ g}}$.

3. **Ratio $R_1$ for a fixed mass ($1.0\text{ g}$) of Oxygen:**
   

$$
R_1 = \frac{m_{\text{S}}}{m_{\text{H}}} = \frac{1.0}{\frac{1}{8}} = \frac{8}{1} = 8 : 1
$$

4. **Reaction 3 (A with B directly):** $\text{S}$ combines with $\text{H}$ to form $\text{H}_2\text{S}$.
   - In $\text{H}_2\text{S}$: $32\text{ g}$ of Sulfur combines with $2\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen.
   - Direct Ratio $R_2$:
     

$$
R_2 = \left(\frac{m_{\text{S}}}{m_{\text{H}}}\right)_{\text{direct}} = \frac{32}{2} = \frac{16}{1} = 16 : 1
$$

5. **Comparison of $R_1$ and $R_2$:**
   

$$
\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{\frac{8}{1}}{\frac{16}{1}} = \frac{8}{16} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}
$$

   

$$
\mathbf{R_1 : R_2 = 1 : 2}
$$

   Since $1:2$ is a simple whole-number ratio, the Law of Reciprocal Proportions is validated.

### 3.5 Gay-Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1808).
- **Statement:** 
  > When gases react together, they always do so in volumes which bear a simple whole-number ratio to one another and to the volumes of the gaseous products, provided all measurements are carried out under identical conditions of temperature and pressure.

- **Governing Condition:** 
  Temperature ($T$) and Pressure ($P$) must remain strictly constant throughout the volumetric measurement:
  

$$
\boxed{V_1 : V_2 : V_3 : \dots = n_1 : n_2 : n_3 : \dots \quad (\text{at constant } T, P)}
$$

#### Solved Case Studies:

##### Case A: Synthesis of Gaseous Water

$$
\begin{array}{cccccc}
2\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & \text{O}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(g)}} & \quad (T > 373.15\text{ K}) \\
2\text{ volumes} & : & 1\text{ volume} & : & 2\text{ volumes} & \\
100\text{ mL} & & 50\text{ mL} & \longrightarrow & 100\text{ mL} &
\end{array}
$$

Volume ratio:

$$
\mathbf{V_{\text{H}_2} : V_{\text{O}_2} : V_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = 2 : 1 : 2}
$$

##### Case B: Synthesis of Gaseous Hydrogen Chloride

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & \text{Cl}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{HCl}_{\text{(g)}} \\
1\text{ volume} & : & 1\text{ volume} & : & 2\text{ volumes}
\end{array}
$$

Volume ratio:

$$
\mathbf{V_{\text{H}_2} : V_{\text{Cl}_2} : V_{\text{HCl}} = 1 : 1 : 2}
$$

##### Case C: Industrial Haber-Bosch Ammonia Synthesis

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\text{N}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & 3\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \xrightarrow[\text{Fe catalyst}]{\Delta, P} & 2\text{NH}_{3\text{ (g)}} \\
1\text{ volume} & : & 3\text{ volumes} & : & 2\text{ volumes}
\end{array}
$$

Volume ratio:

$$
\mathbf{V_{\text{N}_2} : V_{\text{H}_2} : V_{\text{NH}_3} = 1 : 3 : 2}
$$

### 3.6 Avogadro’s Law (Avogadro's Hypothesis) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Amedeo Avogadro (1811).
- **Statement:** 
  > Equal volumes of all gases under identical conditions of temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules.

- **Mathematical Expression:**
  

$$
\boxed{V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } P)}
$$

  

$$
\frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2}
$$

  Where $n$ represents the amount of substance (moles), and the total number of individual molecules $N = n \cdot N_{\text{A}}$.

#### Crucial Historical Distinction: Berzelius Hypothesis vs. Avogadro’s Law
- **Berzelius Hypothesis (1811):** Proposed that equal volumes of all gases contain an equal number of *atoms* at the same temperature and pressure.
  - Applying this to the formation of $\text{HCl}$ gas:
    

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
    \text{Hydrogen gas} & + & \text{Chlorine gas} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{ Compound gas (HCl)} \\
    1\text{ volume} & & 1\text{ volume} & & 2\text{ volumes} \\
    n\text{ atoms} & & n\text{ atoms} & & 2n\text{ compound atoms} \\
    1\text{ atom} & & 1\text{ atom} & & 2\text{ compound atoms} \\
    \frac{1}{2}\text{ atom} & + & \frac{1}{2}\text{ atom} & \longrightarrow & 1\text{ compound atom of HCl}
    \end{array}
$$

  - *The Fatal Flaw:* This required Dalton's fundamental indivisible atom to split into halves ($\frac{1}{2}\text{ atom}$), violating the foundational postulate of Dalton's atomic theory. Consequently, Berzelius's hypothesis was rejected.
- **Avogadro’s Resolution:** 
  Avogadro introduced the clear distinction between an **atom** (the smallest particle of an element that participates in a chemical reaction, which may or may not have independent existence) and a **molecule** (the smallest particle of an element or compound capable of independent existence).
  

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
  \text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & \text{Cl}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{HCl}_{\text{(g)}} \\
  1\text{ molecule} & & 1\text{ molecule} & & 2\text{ molecules} \\
  \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule} & + & \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule} & \longrightarrow & 1\text{ molecule of HCl}
  \end{array}
$$

  Since a molecule of hydrogen can be diatomic ($\text{H}_2$), $\frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule} = 1\text{ atom of H}$. This resolved the paradox without splitting any atoms.

***

### 3.7 Mathematical Derivations Based on Avogadro's Hypothesis

#### Derivation 1: Deduction of the Diatomic Nature of Elementary Gases ($\text{H}_2, \text{O}_2, \text{Cl}_2$)
Consider the volumetric combination of hydrogen and oxygen producing water vapour:

$$
2\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} + \text{O}_{2\text{ (g)}} \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(g)}} \quad (\text{all at constant } T, P)
$$

By experimental gas analysis:

$$
2\text{ volumes of hydrogen} + 1\text{ volume of oxygen} \longrightarrow 2\text{ volumes of water vapour}
$$

Applying Avogadro's Law ($V \propto N$):

$$
2N\text{ molecules of hydrogen} + N\text{ molecules of oxygen} \longrightarrow 2N\text{ molecules of water vapour}
$$

Divide by $2N$:

$$
1\text{ molecule of hydrogen} + \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule of oxygen} \longrightarrow 1\text{ molecule of water vapour}
$$

Experimental analysis shows that 1 molecule of water contains at least 1 atom of oxygen.

$$
\text{Therefore: } \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule of oxygen} \ge 1\text{ atom of oxygen}
$$

$$
\implies \mathbf{1\text{ molecule of oxygen}} \ge \mathbf{2\text{ atoms of oxygen}}
$$

Thus, elementary oxygen cannot be monoatomic; it must be at least diatomic ($\text{O}_2$).

***

#### Derivation 2: Relationship Between Vapour Density (V.D.) and Molar Mass ($M$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

##### Definition:
**Vapour Density (V.D.)** is defined as the ratio of the mass of a certain volume of a gas or vapour to the mass of the same volume of hydrogen gas measured under identical conditions of temperature and pressure.

##### Step-by-Step Derivation:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } V \text{ volume of gas at temperature } T \text{ and pressure } P}{\text{Mass of } V \text{ volume of } \text{H}_2\text{ gas at same } T \text{ and } P}
$$

According to Avogadro’s Law, under identical conditions of $T$ and $P$, $V$ volume contains $N$ molecules:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of } \text{H}_2\text{ gas}}
$$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $N$:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of } \text{H}_2\text{ gas}}
$$

Since Hydrogen is a diatomic gas ($\text{H}_2$ molecule contains $2$ atoms of $\text{H}$):

$$
\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of } \text{H}_2 = 2 \times \text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}
$$

Substitute this relation into the expression:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{2 \times \text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}}
$$

Rewrite the equation:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{1}{2} \times \left( \frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}} \right)
$$

By definition, the term $\frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}}$ is the **Relative Molecular Mass ($M$)** of the gas:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{1}{2} \times M
$$

Rearranging for Relative Molar Mass ($M$):

$$
\boxed{M = 2 \times \text{V.D.}}
$$

***

#### Derivation 3: Molar Volume of an Ideal Gas at STP ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The Ideal Gas Equation is:

$$
P \cdot V = n \cdot R \cdot T
$$

Where:
- $n = 1\text{ mol}$
- $R = 8.314\text{ J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 0.082057\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 0.08314\text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

##### Case 1: Old IUPAC Definition of STP
- $T = 273.15\text{ K}\ (0^\circ\text{C})$
- $P = 1.0\text{ atm} = 101\,325\text{ Pa}$

$$
V_{\text{m}} = \frac{n R T}{P} = \frac{(1\text{ mol}) \times (0.082057\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (273.15\text{ K})}{1.0\text{ atm}} = \mathbf{22.414\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

$$
\boxed{V_{\text{m (old STP)}} \approx 22.4\text{ L} = 22\,400\text{ mL}}
$$

##### Case 2: Modern IUPAC Standard (since 1982)
- $T = 273.15\text{ K}\ (0^\circ\text{C})$
- $P = 1.0\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa}$

$$
V_{\text{m}} = \frac{n R T}{P} = \frac{(1\text{ mol}) \times (0.08314\text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (273.15\text{ K})}{1.0\text{ bar}} = \mathbf{22.711\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

$$
\boxed{V_{\text{m (modern STP)}} \approx 22.7\text{ L} = 22\,700\text{ mL}}
$$

# Module 4: Dalton’s Atomic Theory

### 4.1 Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Published in *A New System of Chemical Philosophy*, Dalton proposed:
1. Matter consists of indivisible, indestructible particles called **atoms** (derived from Greek *atomos*, meaning uncuttable).
2. All atoms of a given element are **identical in every respect**—they possess identical mass, size, and chemical properties.
3. Atoms of different elements possess **different masses, sizes, and chemical behaviors**.
4. Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in **fixed, simple whole-number numerical ratios** (e.g., $1:1, 1:2, 2:3$).
5. Chemical reactions involve only the **reorganization, combination, or separation of atoms**. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

***

### 4.2 Explanatory Success of Dalton’s Postulates

| Law Explained | Operating Dalton Postulate |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Conservation of Mass** | Chemical reactions involve only rearrangement; atoms cannot be created or destroyed (Postulate 5). |
| **Definite Proportions** | A compound consists of atoms of different elements combined in fixed numerical ratios, with constant atomic masses (Postulates 2 and 4). |
| **Multiple Proportions** | Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios ($1:1, 1:2$, etc.). If element $A$ is fixed, varying whole numbers of atom $B$ combine with it (Postulate 4). |

***

### 4.3 Failures & Critical Limitations of Dalton's Atomic Theory ⭐⭐⭐⭐
1. **Subatomic Particles:** The discovery of electrons (J.J. Thomson), protons (Goldstein/Rutherford), and neutrons (Chadwick) invalidated the postulate that the atom is indivisible.
2. **Discovery of Isotopes:** Postulate 2 asserts that all atoms of an element have identical masses. Isotopes ($^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $^{37}\text{Cl}$; $^{1}\text{H}, ^{2}\text{H}, ^{3}\text{H}$) possess identical atomic numbers but different masses, disproving this postulate.
3. **Discovery of Isobars:** Postulate 3 asserts that atoms of different elements must have different masses. Isobars are atoms of different elements with identical mass numbers ($^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$ and $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$), disproving this postulate.
4. **Complex Organic Molecules:** In macromolecules and complex organic polymers (e.g., sucrose $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$, starch $(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{10}\text{O}_5)_n$, proteins), the ratio of atoms is not simple whole numbers like $1:1$ or $1:2$, but rather large structural networks.
5. **Inability to Explain Gay-Lussac’s Law:** Dalton could not account for why gases combine in simple integer ratios by *volume*. He rejected Avogadro’s premise because he refused to believe that elementary gaseous particles could exist as diatomic molecules ($\text{H}_2, \text{O}_2$).
6. **Allotropy:** Dalton's theory failed to explain why substances composed of the exact same atoms (e.g., diamond and graphite, both composed purely of carbon) exhibit vastly different physical and chemical properties.

### Problem 1: Law of Definite Proportions
**Question:** In an experiment, $2.16\text{ g}$ of copper metal was treated with concentrated nitric acid. The resulting copper nitrate was ignited to yield $2.70\text{ g}$ of copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$). In a second experiment, $1.15\text{ g}$ of copper oxide was completely reduced under a stream of pure hydrogen gas to yield $0.92\text{ g}$ of metallic copper. Demonstrate that these experimental findings validate the Law of Definite Proportions.

**Solution:**
- **Experiment 1:**
  - Mass of metallic copper ($m_{\text{Cu}}$) = $2.16\text{ g}$
  - Mass of copper oxide formed ($m_{\text{oxide}}$) = $2.70\text{ g}$
  - Mass of oxygen combined ($m_{\text{O}}$) = $2.70\text{ g} - 2.16\text{ g} = 0.54\text{ g}$
  

$$
\% \text{ of Cu in Experiment 1} = \left(\frac{2.16\text{ g}}{2.70\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{80.0\%}
$$

  

$$
\% \text{ of O in Experiment 1} = \left(\frac{0.54\text{ g}}{2.70\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{20.0\%}
$$

- **Experiment 2:**
  - Mass of copper oxide taken ($m_{\text{oxide}}$) = $1.15\text{ g}$
  - Mass of metallic copper recovered ($m_{\text{Cu}}$) = $0.92\text{ g}$
  - Mass of oxygen lost ($m_{\text{O}}$) = $1.15\text{ g} - 0.92\text{ g} = 0.23\text{ g}$
  

$$
\% \text{ of Cu in Experiment 2} = \left(\frac{0.92\text{ g}}{1.15\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{80.0\%}
$$

  

$$
\% \text{ of O in Experiment 2} = \left(\frac{0.23\text{ g}}{1.15\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{20.0\%}
$$

- **Conclusion:**
  

$$
\% \text{ Cu} : \% \text{ O} = 80.0\% : 20.0\% = 4 : 1 \quad (\text{Identical in both experiments})
$$

  Because the mass composition of copper oxide is constant irrespective of the chemical pathway, the **Law of Definite Proportions** is experimentally validated.

### Problem 2: Law of Multiple Proportions (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
**Question:** Carbon and oxygen form two distinct gaseous compounds, designated $X$ and $Y$. Quantitative micro-analysis shows:
- Compound $X$ contains $42.9\%$ carbon and $57.1\%$ oxygen.
- Compound $Y$ contains $27.3\%$ carbon and $72.7\%$ oxygen.
Show that these data illustrate the Law of Multiple Proportions.

**Solution:**
1. **Analyze Compound $X$:**
   In $100\text{ g}$ of compound $X$:
   - Mass of Carbon = $42.9\text{ g}$
   - Mass of Oxygen = $57.1\text{ g}$
   

$$
\text{Mass of Oxygen combining with } 1.00\text{ g of Carbon} = \frac{57.1\text{ g}}{42.9\text{ g}} = \mathbf{1.33\text{ g}}
$$

2. **Analyze Compound $Y$:**
   In $100\text{ g}$ of compound $Y$:
   - Mass of Carbon = $27.3\text{ g}$
   - Mass of Oxygen = $72.7\text{ g}$
   

$$
\text{Mass of Oxygen combining with } 1.00\text{ g of Carbon} = \frac{72.7\text{ g}}{27.3\text{ g}} = \mathbf{2.66\text{ g}}
$$

3. **Compare Oxygen Masses for a Fixed Mass ($1.00\text{ g}$) of Carbon:**
   

$$
\text{Ratio} = \frac{m_{\text{O in } Y}}{m_{\text{O in } X}} = \frac{2.66\text{ g}}{1.33\text{ g}} = \frac{2}{1} = \mathbf{2 : 1}
$$

- **Conclusion:**
  The ratio of masses of oxygen combining with a fixed mass of carbon is $1 : 2$, an integer ratio. Thus, the **Law of Multiple Proportions** is confirmed. *(Compound $X = \text{CO}$; Compound $Y = \text{CO}_2$)*.

### Problem 3: Law of Reciprocal Proportions
**Question:** Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) contains $82.35\%$ nitrogen and $17.65\%$ hydrogen. Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) contains $88.90\%$ oxygen and $11.10\%$ hydrogen. Nitrogen trioxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$) contains $63.15\%$ oxygen and $36.85\%$ nitrogen. Prove that these quantitative values satisfy the Law of Reciprocal Proportions.

**Solution:**
- **Step 1: In $\text{NH}_3$ (Element $A = \text{N}$, Element $C = \text{H}$):**
  $17.65\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen combines with $82.35\text{ g}$ of Nitrogen.
  

$$
\text{Mass of Nitrogen combining with } 1.0\text{ g of Hydrogen} = \frac{82.35}{17.65} = \mathbf{4.666\text{ g}}
$$

- **Step 2: In $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Element $B = \text{O}$, Element $C = \text{H}$):**
  $11.10\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen combines with $88.90\text{ g}$ of Oxygen.
  

$$
\text{Mass of Oxygen combining with } 1.0\text{ g of Hydrogen} = \frac{88.90}{11.10} = \mathbf{8.009\text{ g}}
$$

- **Step 3: Calculated Ratio ($R_1$) between $\text{N}$ and $\text{O}$ with fixed mass ($1.0\text{ g}$) of $\text{H}$:**
  

$$
R_1 = \frac{m_{\text{N}}}{m_{\text{O}}} = \frac{4.666}{8.009} = \mathbf{0.5826}
$$

- **Step 4: Direct Combination in $\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$:**
  Mass of Nitrogen = $36.85\text{ g}$; Mass of Oxygen = $63.15\text{ g}$.
  

$$
R_2 = \left(\frac{m_{\text{N}}}{m_{\text{O}}}\right)_{\text{direct}} = \frac{36.85}{63.15} = \mathbf{0.5835}
$$

- **Step 5: Determine the Ratio of $R_1$ to $R_2$:**
  

$$
\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{0.5826}{0.5835} \approx \mathbf{1.00} = \mathbf{1 : 1}
$$

- **Conclusion:**
  Since the ratio $\frac{R_1}{R_2} = 1:1$, a simple integer ratio, the **Law of Reciprocal Proportions** is satisfied.

### Problem 4: Gay-Lussac’s Law with Excess Reactant
**Question:** $500\text{ mL}$ of gaseous propane ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$) is completely combusted in $3000\text{ mL}$ of pure oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$) at $100^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\text{ atm}$. Calculate the total volume and composition of the resulting gas mixture cooled to $25^\circ\text{C}$ at $1\text{ atm}$.

**Solution:**
1. **Balanced Chemical Equation:**
   

$$
\text{C}_3\text{H}_{8\text{ (g)}} + 5\text{O}_{2\text{ (g)}} \longrightarrow 3\text{CO}_{2\text{ (g)}} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}}
$$

   *(Note: Water is in the liquid state at $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\text{ atm}$; its volume is negligible compared to gases).*

2. **Stoichiometric Volume Relationships (Gay-Lussac's Law):**
   

$$
1\text{ volume of } \text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{ volumes of } \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 3\text{ volumes of } \text{CO}_2
$$

3. **Identify the Limiting Reactant:**
   - Volume of $\text{O}_2$ required for $500\text{ mL}$ of $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 = 500\text{ mL} \times 5 = 2500\text{ mL}$.
   - Available volume of $\text{O}_2 = 3000\text{ mL}$.
   - Since $2500\text{ mL} < 3000\text{ mL}$, **$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$ is the Limiting Reactant** and $\text{O}_2$ is in excess.

4. **Quantities After Reaction:**
   - $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$ consumed = $500\text{ mL} \implies \text{Residual } \text{C}_3\text{H}_8 = \mathbf{0\text{ mL}}$
   - $\text{O}_2$ consumed = $2500\text{ mL} \implies \text{Residual } \text{O}_2 = 3000\text{ mL} - 2500\text{ mL} = \mathbf{500\text{ mL}}$
   - $\text{CO}_2$ produced = $500\text{ mL} \times 3 = \mathbf{1500\text{ mL}}$
   - $\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}}$ volume $\approx 0\text{ mL (gas phase)}$

5. **Total Final Gaseous Volume:**
   

$$
V_{\text{final}} = V_{\text{residual } \text{O}_2} + V_{\text{formed } \text{CO}_2} = 500\text{ mL} + 1500\text{ mL} = \mathbf{2000\text{ mL}}
$$

### Problem 5: Significant Figures in Arithmetic

$$
\text{Ans} = \frac{0.02856 \times 298.15 \times 0.112}{0.5785}

1. Identify the number of significant figures in each operand:
   - $0.02856 \implies 4\text{ significant figures}$
   - $298.15 \implies 5\text{ significant figures}$
   - $0.112 \implies 3\text{ significant figures}$ $\leftarrow$ **Limiting operand**
   - $0.5785 \implies 4\text{ significant figures}$
2. Perform the unrounded arithmetic:
   

$$
\text{Numerator} = 0.02856 \times 298.15 \times 0.112 = 0.953696544
$$

   

$$
\text{Ans} = \frac{0.953696544}{0.5785} = 1.64856792\dots
$$

3. Round to the limiting precision ($3\text{ significant figures}$):
   

$$
\mathbf{\text{Final Answer} = 1.65}
$$

1. **Kelvin Notation Errors:**
   - *Error:* Writing temperatures as $273^\circ\text{K}$.
   - *Correction:* The Kelvin scale is thermodynamic; it does not take a degree symbol ($^\circ$). Write **$273\text{ K}$**, never $273^\circ\text{K}$. The degree symbol is reserved for $^\circ\text{C}$, $^\circ\text{F}$, and $^\circ\text{R}$.

2. **Liquid Phase in Gay-Lussac Calculations:**
   - *Error:* Including water volume when hydrocarbons combust at room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$).
   - *Correction:* Gay-Lussac’s Law applies strictly to **gaseous volumes**. At $25^\circ\text{C}$, water condenses to liquid form ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}}$). Its molar volume in the liquid phase is only $\approx 18\text{ mL}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, which is negligible compared to gas molar volume ($22\,400\text{ mL}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). Always verify the state symbols at the specified reaction temperature!

3. **Molar Volume: $22.4\text{ L}$ versus $22.7\text{ L}$:**
   - *Error:* Using $22.4\text{ L}$ when conditions are specified at $1\text{ bar}$.
   - *Correction:* 
     - At $T = 273.15\text{ K}$ and $P = 1\text{ atm}$, $V_{\text{m}} = 22.414\text{ L}$.
     - At $T = 273.15\text{ K}$ and $P = 1\text{ bar}$, $V_{\text{m}} = 22.711\text{ L}$.
     - If an exam question specifies **STP with $1\text{ bar}$**, use **$22.7\text{ L}$**. If it does not specify pressure, clarify the standard used.

4. **Law of Multiple Proportions vs. Reciprocal Proportions:**
   - *Error:* Confusing the two laws when two elements form multiple compounds.
   - *Rule of Thumb:*
     - **Multiple Proportions:** Involves **two** elements only (e.g., $\text{CO}$ and $\text{CO}_2$; $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{SO}_3$).
     - **Reciprocal Proportions:** Involves **three** elements forming three distinct binary compounds in a triangular relationship (e.g., $\text{C}, \text{H}, \text{O}$ forming $\text{CH}_4, \text{CO}_2, \text{H}_2\text{O}$).

5. **Rounding at Intermediate Steps:**
   - *Error:* Rounding off intermediate numbers during multi-step calculations causes compounding errors in the final digit.
   - *Correction:* Retain at least two extra guard digits throughout intermediate arithmetic steps. Apply significant figure rounding **only once**, at the final reported answer.

### 7.1 Golden Rules for Rapid Problem-Solving
1. **The Vapour Density Anchor:**
   

$$
\boxed{\text{Molar Mass} = 2 \times \text{V.D.}}
$$

   *Instant Check:* If an unknown gas has a Vapour Density of $16$, its molar mass is $32\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ ($\text{O}_2$ or $\text{N}_2\text{H}_4$). If $\text{V.D.} = 14$, $M = 28\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ ($\text{N}_2$ or $\text{CO}$).

2. **Density of Air Conversion:**
   Dry air has an effective average molar mass of $M_{\text{air}} \approx 28.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
   

$$
\text{V.D. of dry air} \approx \frac{28.96}{2} \approx 14.48
$$

   - Any gas with $M < 28.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (e.g., $\text{CH}_4, \text{NH}_3$) is lighter than air and collected via **downward displacement of air**.
   - Any gas with $M > 28.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (e.g., $\text{CO}_2, \text{SO}_2, \text{Cl}_2$) is heavier than air and collected via **upward displacement of air**.

3. **Rounding Rules Rapid-Selector:**
   ```
   Is the digit to be dropped equal to 5?
   ├── If followed by non-zero digits (e.g., 51, 52) ──► ALWAYS ROUND UP (+1)
   └── If followed by zeros only (or terminates)
       ├── Preceding digit is ODD  ─────────────────────► ROUND UP (+1 to make even)
       └── Preceding digit is EVEN ─────────────────────► LEAVE UNCHANGED (keep even)
   ```

4. **Gas Volumetric Shortcuts at STP:**
   

$$
\text{Moles of gas } (n) = \frac{\text{Volume in mL at old STP}}{22\,400\text{ mL}} = \frac{\text{Volume in L at old STP}}{22.4\text{ L}}

$$
1\text{ millimole of any ideal gas at old STP} = 22.4\text{ mL}
$$

   

$$
1\text{ micromole of any ideal gas at old STP} = 22.4\text{ }\mu\text{L}
$$

5. **Temperature Inversion Trick:**
   For fast mental sanity checks:
   - $0^\circ\text{C} = 32^\circ\text{F}$
   - $10^\circ\text{C} = 50^\circ\text{F}$
   - $20^\circ\text{C} = 68^\circ\text{F}$
   - $30^\circ\text{C} = 86^\circ\text{F}$

## 1. IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & RIGOROUS DEFINITIONS

***

### 1.1 Atomic Mass & The Unified Atomic Mass Unit ($u$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (High Board & Foundational Frequency)

#### Historical Context & The Carbon-12 Standard
Historically, hydrogen was initially taken as the standard with an arbitrary mass of $1$. Later, oxygen (${}^{16}\text{O} = 16.000$) was adopted due to its wide reactivity forming compounds with most elements, and because it gave whole numbers for most atomic masses. 

In $1961$, the **International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC)** officially adopted the **Carbon-12 isotope** (${}^{12}\text{C}$) as the universal reference standard.

#### Rigorous NCERT Definition: Unified Atomic Mass Unit ($u$ or amu)
> **Definition:** One atomic mass unit ($1\text{ amu}$), now represented by the unified mass symbol ($u$) or Dalton ($\text{Da}$), is defined as a mass exactly equal to **one-twelfth ($1/12^{\text{th}}$)** of the mass of one atom of carbon-12 isotope (${}^{12}\text{C}$).

$$
\boxed{1\text{ }u = \frac{1}{12} \times m\left({}^{12}\text{C}\text{ atom}\right)}
$$

#### Mathematical Conversion between $u$ and Grams/Kilograms
Using modern mass spectrometry, the absolute mass of one atom of ${}^{12}\text{C}$ is experimentally determined as:

$$
m\left({}^{12}\text{C}\right) = 1.992648 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g} = 1.992648 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}
$$

Substituting this value:

$$
1\text{ }u = \frac{1.992648 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}}{12} \approx 1.660539 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g}
$$

$$
1\text{ }u = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}
$$

Notice that:

$$
\boxed{1\text{ }u = \frac{1}{N_{\text{A}}}\text{ g}}
$$

where $N_{\text{A}}$ is the Avogadro constant ($6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}$).

***

### 1.2 Average Atomic Mass (Isotopic Mass)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Extremely High Examination Frequency)

Most naturally occurring elements exist as mixtures of two or more stable isotopes having identical atomic numbers ($Z$) but different mass numbers ($A$). Therefore, the atomic mass of an element reported on the periodic table is the **weighted average** of the isotopic masses relative to their fractional natural abundance.

#### Formulation
Let an element $X$ possess $k$ naturally occurring isotopes $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k$ having atomic masses $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_k$ (in $u$) and fractional abundances $f_1, f_2, \dots, f_k$ (or percentage abundances $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$, where $\sum p_i = 100$ and $f_i = p_i / 100$):

$$
\boxed{M_{\text{avg}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} f_i M_i = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i M_i}{100} = \frac{p_1 M_1 + p_2 M_2 + \dots + p_k M_k}{100}\text{ }u}
$$

***

### 1.3 Molecular Mass vs. Formula Mass
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Crucial Conceptual Distinctions)

#### Molecular Mass
> **Definition:** The molecular mass of a substance is the sum of atomic masses of all the atoms present in a single discrete molecule of that substance. It is expressed in unified mass ($u$).

*Calculation for Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$):*

$$
M(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6) = 6 \times (\text{Mass of C}) + 12 \times (\text{Mass of H}) + 6 \times (\text{Mass of O})
$$

$$
M(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6) = 6(12.011\text{ }u) + 12(1.008\text{ }u) + 6(15.999\text{ }u) = 180.156\text{ }u
$$

#### Formula Mass (For Ionic Compounds)
> **Crucial NCERT Concept:** Ionic substances such as sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$), copper sulphate ($\text{CuSO}_4$), and calcium nitrate ($\text{Ca(NO}_3)_2$) **do not exist as discrete discrete molecules** in the solid state. In crystalline sodium chloride, each $\text{Na}^+$ ion is octahedral coordinated by six $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, and each $\text{Cl}^-$ ion is coordinated by six $\text{Na}^+$ ions in a three-dimensional lattice. 
> 
> Therefore, the term **"Molecular Mass" is strictly incorrect for ionic solids**. Instead, the term **Formula Mass** is used, calculated using the empirical/chemical formula unit.

$$
\text{Formula Mass of NaCl} = 1 \times (\text{Atomic mass of Na}) + 1 \times (\text{Atomic mass of Cl})
$$

$$
\text{Formula Mass of NaCl} = 22.990\text{ }u + 35.453\text{ }u = 58.443\text{ }u \approx 58.5\text{ }u
$$

***

### 1.4 Avogadro's Law & Molar Gas Volume
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Direct Theorem & Numerical Foundation)

#### Historical Formulations: Gay-Lussac vs. Berzelius vs. Avogadro
1. **Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes ($1808$):** When gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction, they do so in a simple ratio by volume, provided all gases are at the same temperature and pressure.
2. **Berzelius Hypothesis:** "Equal volumes of all gases under the same conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of **atoms**." 
   * *The Fatal Flaw:* When applied to reactions like $\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \to 2\text{HCl(g)}$, Berzelius's hypothesis led to the fractional division of indivisible atoms (i.e., half an atom of H combines with half an atom of Cl), directly contradicting Dalton's Atomic Theory.
3. **Amedeo Avogadro's Resolution ($1811$):** Avogadro made a fundamental conceptual distinction between **atoms** (the smallest indivisible particle of an element capable of participating in chemical reactions) and **molecules** (the smallest particle of an element or compound capable of independent existence).

#### Avogadro's Law Statement
> **Law:** Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal number of molecules.

$$
V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } P)
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2}}
$$

#### Rigorous Proof: Why Elementary Gases Are Diatomic
Consider the gas-phase synthesis of hydrogen chloride:

$$
\text{Hydrogen gas} + \text{Chlorine gas} \longrightarrow \text{Hydrogen Chloride gas}
$$

Experimentally (Gay-Lussac's Law):

$$
1\text{ volume of Hydrogen} + 1\text{ volume of Chlorine} \longrightarrow 2\text{ volumes of Hydrogen Chloride}
$$

Applying Avogadro's Hypothesis (let $1\text{ volume}$ contain $N$ molecules at standard $T, P$):

$$
N\text{ molecules of Hydrogen} + N\text{ molecules of Chlorine} \longrightarrow 2N\text{ molecules of Hydrogen Chloride}
$$

Dividing across by $2N$:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule of Hydrogen} + \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule of Chlorine} \longrightarrow 1\text{ molecule of Hydrogen Chloride}
$$

*Conclusion:* Since a molecule of hydrogen chloride must contain at least one atom each of hydrogen and chlorine, $\frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule of Hydrogen}$ must be at least $1\text{ atom of Hydrogen}$. Therefore, $1\text{ molecule of Hydrogen} = 2\text{ atoms of Hydrogen} = \text{H}_2$. This proved that elemental gas molecules are polyatomic (diatomic in this case), without violating Dalton's concept of indivisible atoms.

***

### 1.5 The Mole Concept & Avogadro Constant ($N_{\text{A}}$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Cornerstone of Quantitative Chemistry)

#### Formal SI Definition (Pre-2019 vs. Post-2019 Re-definition)
- **Pre-2019 Definition:** The mole is the amount of substance of a system which contains as many elementary entities as there are atoms in $0.012\text{ kg}$ ($12\text{ g}$) of carbon-12 isotope.
- **Post-2019 IUPAC SI Definition:**
  > One mole contains exactly $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities. This number is the fixed numerical value of the Avogadro constant, $N_{\text{A}}$, when expressed in the unit $\text{mol}^{-1}$ and is called the Avogadro number.

The elementary entities must always be specified: they may be atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, radicals, or specified groups of such particles.

***

### 1.6 Molar Mass ($M$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> **Definition:** The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is called its **molar mass**. The SI unit is $\text{kg}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, but practical laboratory work uniformly employs $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.

$$
\text{Molar Mass } (M) = \frac{\text{Mass of substance } (m)}{\text{Amount of substance in moles } (n)}
$$

#### The Fundamental Duality: Atomic Mass vs. Gram Atomic Mass vs. Molar Mass
For an atom of Oxygen (${}^{16}\text{O}$):
- **Atomic mass:** $16\text{ }u$ (mass of a single isolated oxygen atom)
- **Gram atomic mass:** $16\text{ g}$ (mass of $1\text{ mole}$ ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$) of oxygen atoms)
- **Molar mass:** $16\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (mass per unit mole)

*Proof of equivalence:*

$$
\text{Mass of } 1\text{ atom of O} = 16\text{ }u = 16 \times \left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{A}}}\text{ g}\right)
$$

$$
\text{Mass of } 1\text{ mole of O atoms} = 16 \times \left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{A}}}\text{ g}\right) \times N_{\text{A}} = \mathbf{16\text{ g}}
$$

The numerical value in $u$ and in $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ is identically the same; only the units differ.

## 2. DERIVATIONS, MATHEMATICAL RELATIONS & GAS CONDITIONS

***

### 2.1 The Master Mole Equations

$$
\boxed{n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{N}{N_{\text{A}}} = \frac{V_{\text{gas (at STP)}}}{V_{\text{molar (STP)}}}}
$$

Where:
- $n$ = amount of substance in moles ($\text{mol}$)
- $m$ = mass of substance ($\text{g}$)
- $M$ = molar mass ($\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$)
- $N$ = total number of elementary entities (dimensionless)
- $N_{\text{A}}$ = Avogadro constant ($6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}$)
- $V_{\text{gas}}$ = volume occupied by an ideal gas at standard conditions
- $V_{\text{molar}}$ = molar volume of an ideal gas at those specific conditions

***

#### Ideal Gas Derivation of Molar Gas Volume ($V_{\text{m}}$)
From the Ideal Gas Equation:

$$
PV = nRT \implies V_{\text{m}} = \frac{V}{n} = \frac{RT}{P}
$$

Universal Gas Constant $R = 0.082057\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 0.083145\text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.

1. **For Old Standard ($1\text{ atm}, 273.15\text{ K}$):**
   

$$
V_{\text{m}} = \frac{(0.082057)(273.15)}{1.000} = \mathbf{22.414\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

2. **For Modern IUPAC Standard ($1\text{ bar}, 273.15\text{ K}$):**
   

$$
V_{\text{m}} = \frac{(0.083145)(273.15)}{1.000} = \mathbf{22.711\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

> **Exam Directing Rule:** In CBSE board exams, state: *"Taking molar volume at STP as $22.7\text{ L}$ (or $22.4\text{ L}$ if $P=1\text{ atm}$ is assumed)"*. Competitive exams frequently accept $22.4\text{ L}$ unless $P = 1\text{ bar}$ is explicitly noted.

### 2.3 Derivation: Vapour Density ($VD$) and Molar Mass ($M$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Definition of Vapour Density
> **Definition:** Vapour density of a gas/vapour is the ratio of the mass of a certain volume of the gas to the mass of the same volume of hydrogen gas measured under identical conditions of temperature and pressure.

$$
VD = \frac{\text{Mass of } V\text{ litres of gas at } T, P}{\text{Mass of } V\text{ litres of }\text{H}_2\text{ at } T, P}
$$

#### Derivation
Let $V\text{ litres}$ contain $N$ molecules under identical $T$ and $P$ (by **Avogadro’s Law**):

$$
VD = \frac{\text{Mass of } N\text{ molecules of gas}}{\text{Mass of } N\text{ molecules of }\text{H}_2}
$$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $N$:

$$
VD = \frac{\text{Mass of } 1\text{ molecule of gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1\text{ molecule of }\text{H}_2}
$$

Since Hydrogen gas is diatomic ($\text{H}_2$), $1\text{ molecule of }\text{H}_2 = 2 \times (\text{Mass of } 1\text{ atom of H})$:

$$
VD = \frac{\text{Mass of } 1\text{ molecule of gas}}{2 \times (\text{Mass of } 1\text{ atom of H})} = \frac{1}{2} \times \left(\frac{\text{Mass of } 1\text{ molecule of gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1\text{ atom of H}}\right)
$$

By definition, $\frac{\text{Mass of } 1\text{ molecule of gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1\text{ atom of H}}$ is the **Molecular Mass** of the gas:

$$
VD = \frac{\text{Molecular Mass }(M)}{2}
$$

$$
\boxed{M = 2 \times VD}
$$

***

### 2.4 Derivation: Gas Density and Molar Mass
Starting with the ideal gas law:

$$
PV = nRT = \left(\frac{m}{M}\right)RT
$$

Rearranging for density ($d = \frac{m}{V}$):

$$
P = \frac{m}{V} \cdot \frac{RT}{M} = \frac{d RT}{M}
$$

$$
\boxed{d = \frac{PM}{RT} \iff M = \frac{d RT}{P}}
$$

Units:
- If $P$ is in $\text{atm}$, $d$ in $\text{g}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}$, $T$ in $\text{K}$, then $R = 0.0821\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
- If $P$ is in $\text{Pa}$, $d$ in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$, $T$ in $\text{K}$, then $R = 8.314\text{ J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.

### Problem 1: Average Atomic Mass from Isotopic Fractional Abundance
*(NCERT Chapter 1 In-text / Exemplar Archetype)*

**Question:** Naturally occurring chlorine consists of two isotopes, ${}^{35}\text{Cl}$ and ${}^{37}\text{Cl}$, with atomic masses $34.9689\text{ }u$ and $36.9659\text{ }u$ respectively. If the average atomic mass of chlorine is $35.453\text{ }u$, calculate the fractional and percentage abundances of ${}^{35}\text{Cl}$ and ${}^{37}\text{Cl}$.

**Solution:**
1. Let the fractional abundance of ${}^{35}\text{Cl}$ be $x$.
2. Because the sum of fractional abundances for all natural isotopes equals $1$:
   

$$
\text{Fractional abundance of }{}^{37}\text{Cl} = 1 - x
$$

3. Formulate the average mass equation:
   

$$
M_{\text{avg}} = x \cdot M({}^{35}\text{Cl}) + (1 - x) \cdot M({}^{37}\text{Cl})
$$

   

$$
35.453 = x(34.9689) + (1 - x)(36.9659)
$$

4. Expand and solve algebraically:
   

$$
35.453 = 34.9689x + 36.9659 - 36.9659x
$$

   

$$
35.453 - 36.9659 = -1.997x
$$

   

$$
-1.5129 = -1.997x
$$

   

$$
x = \frac{1.5129}{1.997} \approx 0.7576
$$

5. Compute the remaining abundance:
   

$$
1 - x = 1 - 0.7576 = 0.2424
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Fractional Abundance: }{}^{35}\text{Cl} = 0.7576\text{ }(75.76\%), \quad {}^{37}\text{Cl} = 0.2424\text{ }(24.24\%)}
$$

### Problem 2: Multi-Tier Subatomic Particle Breakdown
*(Advanced Board & Competitive Level Archetype)*

**Question:** Calculate the total number of:
1. Moles of sulphate ions ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$)
2. Total oxygen atoms
3. Total valence electrons  
present in $39.2\text{ g}$ of Mohr's salt: $\text{FeSO}_4\cdot(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4\cdot 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$.  
*(Atomic masses: $\text{Fe} = 56$, $\text{S} = 32$, $\text{O} = 16$, $\text{N} = 14$, $\text{H} = 1\text{ }u$)*

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Compute Molar Mass of Mohr's Salt**

$$
M = \text{Fe} + \text{S} + 4\text{O} + 2[\text{N} + 4\text{H}] + \text{S} + 4\text{O} + 6[2\text{H} + \text{O}]
$$

$$
M = 56 + 32 + 4(16) + 2[14 + 4(1)] + 32 + 4(16) + 6[2(1) + 16]
$$

$$
M = 56 + 32 + 64 + 36 + 32 + 64 + 108 = \mathbf{392\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

**Step 2: Moles of Mohr's Salt Formula Units ($n$)**

$$
n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{39.2\text{ g}}{392\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = \mathbf{0.1\text{ mol}}
$$

**Step 3: Moles of Sulphate Ions ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$)**
Each formula unit contains $2$ sulphate ions ($\text{FeSO}_4$ provides $1$, $(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4$ provides $1$).

$$
n(\text{SO}_4^{2-}) = 2 \times n = 2 \times 0.1 = \mathbf{0.2\text{ mol}}
$$

**Step 4: Total Oxygen Atoms**
One formula unit contains:

$$
4 \text{ (from FeSO}_4) + 4 \text{ (from (NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4) + 6 \text{ (from 6H}_2\text{O}) = 14\text{ atoms of O}
$$

$$
\text{Total atoms of O} = n \times 14 \times N_{\text{A}} = 0.1 \times 14 \times N_{\text{A}} = 1.4\text{ }N_{\text{A}}
$$

$$
\text{Total atoms of O} = 1.4 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{8.431 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms}}
$$

**Step 5: Total Valence Electrons in the Salt**
Valence electrons contributed by individual neutral constituents:
- $\text{Fe}$ ($[\text{Ar}] 4s^2 3d^6$): $8$ valence electrons
- $\text{S}$ (Group 16): $6$ valence electrons
- $\text{O}$ (Group 16): $6$ valence electrons
- $\text{N}$ (Group 15): $5$ valence electrons
- $\text{H}$ (Group 1): $1$ valence electron

Per formula unit:
- $\text{Fe}$: $1 \times 8 = 8$
- $2\text{ S}$: $2 \times 6 = 12$
- $14\text{ O}$: $14 \times 6 = 84$
- $2\text{ N}$: $2 \times 5 = 10$
- $20\text{ H}$: $20 \times 1 = 20$

$$
\text{Total valence electrons per formula unit} = 8 + 12 + 84 + 10 + 20 = 134
$$

$$
\text{Total valence electrons} = 0.1\text{ mol} \times 134 \times N_{\text{A}} = \mathbf{13.4\text{ }N_{\text{A}} = 8.070 \times 10^{24}\text{ electrons}}
$$

***

### Problem 3: Vapour Density, Gas Volume, and Molecular Formula
*(NCERT Exemplar Variant)*

**Question:** A hydrocarbon gas contains $85.7\%$ Carbon and $14.3\%$ Hydrogen by mass. At STP ($1\text{ bar}, 273.15\text{ K}$), $5.678\text{ L}$ of this gas weighs $10.5\text{ g}$. Determine:
1. Empirical formula
2. Vapour density of the gas
3. Molecular formula of the hydrocarbon

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Determine Empirical Formula**

| Element | Relative % ($m$) | Atomic Mass ($A$) | Relative Moles ($\frac{m}{A}$) | Simple Mole Ratio | Whole Number Ratio |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $\text{C}$ | $85.7$ | $12$ | $\frac{85.7}{12} = 7.142$ | $\frac{7.142}{7.142} = 1$ | $1$ |
| $\text{H}$ | $14.3$ | $1$ | $\frac{14.3}{1} = 14.30$ | $\frac{14.30}{7.142} \approx 2$ | $2$ |

$$
\text{Empirical Formula} = \mathbf{\text{CH}_2}
$$

$$
\text{Empirical Formula Mass} = 12 + 2(1) = 14\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

**Step 2: Determine Molar Mass from STP Data**
Using modern IUPAC STP ($1\text{ bar}, 273.15\text{ K}$, where $V_{\text{m}} = 22.711\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$):

$$
n = \frac{V}{V_{\text{m}}} = \frac{5.678\text{ L}}{22.711\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 0.250\text{ mol}
$$

Now compute Molar Mass:

$$
M = \frac{m}{n} = \frac{10.5\text{ g}}{0.250\text{ mol}} = \mathbf{42.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

*(Cross-check using Old STP $22.4\text{ L}$: if $5.6\text{ L}$ weighs $10.5\text{ g}$, $M = 10.5 \times \frac{22.4}{5.6} = 42\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$)*

**Step 3: Calculate Vapour Density**

$$
VD = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{2} = \frac{42.0}{2} = \mathbf{21}
$$

**Step 4: Find Molecular Formula**

$$
k = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}} = \frac{42.0}{14.0} = 3
$$

$$
\text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{CH}_2)_3 = \mathbf{\text{C}_3\text{H}_6}\text{ (Propene/Cyclopropane)}
$$

***

## 4. EXAMINER TRAPS & FREQUENT STUDENT PITFALLS

***

### Trap 1: The Ambiguity of "1 Mole of Oxygen"
- **The Trap:** If an exam question simply mentions *"Calculate mass of 1 mole of oxygen"*, students often calculate for atomic oxygen ($16\text{ g}$) or molecular dioxygen ($32\text{ g}$) at random.
- **The Rule:** In standard IUPAC convention:
  - *"Oxygen"* as an elementary gas substance refers to **dioxygen molecules ($\text{O}_2$)** $\implies M = 32\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
  - *"Oxygen atoms"* must be specifically stated for $\text{O}$ $\implies M = 16\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
  - *Golden Advice to Students:* Always qualify in your answer script: *"Assuming oxygen gas $\text{O}_2$, molar mass is $32\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$."*

***

### Trap 2: Liquid/Solid Volume vs. Gas Volume at STP
- **The Trap:** Asking for the volume of $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ at STP, students immediately write $22.4\text{ L}$ or $22.7\text{ L}$.
- **The Physical Reality:** $22.4\text{ L}$ or $22.7\text{ L}$ is strictly applicable **ONLY to gases**.
  - At STP ($0^\circ\text{C}, 1\text{ bar}$), water is in the **liquid/solid** phase!
  - Density of liquid water $\approx 1.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$.
  - Mass of $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18.015\text{ g}$.
  - Actual Volume:
    

$$
V_{\text{liquid}} = \frac{m}{d} = \frac{18\text{ g}}{1\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}} = \mathbf{18\text{ mL}} = 18 \times 10^{-3}\text{ L}
$$

  - $18\text{ mL} \neq 22,400\text{ mL}$! Applying $22.4\text{ L}$ to liquids or solids results in an immediate zero mark.

***

### Trap 3: Unified Mass ($u$) vs. Molar Mass ($\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) vs. Mass of 1 Molecule
- **The Confusion Matrix:**

| Entity | Unit | Meaning | Example for $\text{CO}_2$ |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :---: |
| **Relative Molecular Mass** | *Dimensionless* | Ratio relative to $1/12^{\text{th}}$ mass of ${}^{12}\text{C}$ | $44$ |
| **Molecular Mass** | $u$ (or amu) | Mass of **one single** isolated molecule | $44\text{ }u$ |
| **Molar Mass** | $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ | Mass of **one mole ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$)** molecules | $44\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ |
| **Gram Molecular Mass** | $\text{g}$ | Mass of one mole expressed in grams | $44\text{ g}$ |
| **Absolute Mass of 1 molecule**| $\text{g}$ or $\text{kg}$ | Actual physical mass of one molecule ($M / N_{\text{A}}$) | $7.306 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}$ |

*Examiner Trick:* "What is the mass of 1 molecule of Nitrogen?"
- *Incorrect:* $28\text{ g}$ (That is the mass of $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ molecules!)
- *Correct:* $\frac{28}{6.022 \times 10^{23}}\text{ g} = 4.65 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}$ (or $28\text{ }u$).

***

### Trap 4: STP vs. NTP
- Historically in older Indian curricula:
  - **NTP (Normal Temperature & Pressure):** $T = 20^\circ\text{C}$ ($293.15\text{ K}$), $P = 1\text{ atm}$. Here, $V_{\text{m}} = 24.04\text{ L}$. (Sometimes loosely treated as $0^\circ\text{C}, 1\text{ atm}$ in local textbooks).
  - Modern NCERT adheres to **STP: $273.15\text{ K}, 1\text{ bar} \implies 22.7\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$**. Always check the pressure specified in competitive exam stems.

***

## 5. SPEED HACKS, MNEMONICS & GOLDEN BULLETS

***

### 5.1 The "Mole Crossroads" Mental Map

```
                    Mass in Grams (m)
                         ▲     │
              × Molar    │     │  ÷ Molar
               Mass      │     │   Mass
                         │     ▼
Total Particles (N) ◄─── MOLE (n) ───► Volume of Gas at STP
  [Atoms/Molecules]  × NA │    │ ÷ NA     [in Litres]
                          │    │
                          ▼    │
                     × 22.7/22.4  ÷ 22.7/22.4
```

- **To go TO Moles:** ALWAYS DIVIDE ($\div M$, $\div N_{\text{A}}$, $\div 22.7$).
- **To go FROM Moles:** ALWAYS MULTIPLY ($\times M$, $\times N_{\text{A}}$, $\times 22.7$).

***

### 5.2 Reciprocal of Avogadro's Number: The "$1/N_{\text{A}}$" Accelerator
In examinations without calculators, dividing by $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ consumes precious minutes.

Memorize the direct pre-computed reciprocal:

$$
\boxed{\frac{1}{N_{\text{A}}} = \frac{1}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \approx 1.66 \times 10^{-24}}
$$

*Example Speed Application:*
Find the mass of $1$ atom of Gold ($\text{Au} = 197\text{ }u$):

$$
\text{Mass} = 197 \times \left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{A}}}\right) = 197 \times (1.66 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g}) \approx 3.27 \times 10^{-22}\text{ g}
$$

Calculation time: **Under 10 seconds!**

***

### 5.3 Golden Ratios for Gas Problems
For any gas at fixed $T$ and $P$:

$$
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{m_1 / M_1}{m_2 / M_2}
$$

*Immediate Takeaway:*
- Equal volumes of two gases contain equal molecules.
- The ratio of masses of equal volumes of two gases is directly the ratio of their molar masses:
  

$$
\boxed{\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{M_1}{M_2}}
$$

***

# Percentage Composition, Empirical Formula, and Molecular Formula Determination

# Chapter 1: Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

## Module: Percentage Composition, Empirical Formula, and Molecular Formula Determination

***

## 1. Pedagogical Theory, Physical Laws & Fundamental Definitions

### 1.1 Mass Percentage Composition of an Element ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The composition of any compound can be quantitatively characterized by stating the relative mass contributed by each constituting element. This is rooted directly in the **Law of Definite Proportions (Joseph Proust, 1799)**, which establishes that *a given chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by mass, regardless of its source, method of preparation, or sample size.*

For any pure chemical compound of known molecular formula $A_x B_y C_z$:

$$
\text{Mass } \% \text{ of an element } E = \frac{\text{Total mass of element } E \text{ in 1 mole of compound}}{\text{Molar mass of the compound}} \times 100
$$

Expressing this formally for element $A$ with molar mass $M_A$ in a compound of molar mass $M$:

$$
\boxed{\% w/w(A) = \frac{x \cdot M_A}{M} \times 100\%}
$$

* **Condition of Validity:** The sum of mass percentages of all constituent elements in an unadulterated, pure chemical species must strictly satisfy:
  

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{k} (\% w/w)_i = 100.00\%
$$

***

### 1.2 The Empirical Formula ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
* **Strict NCERT Definition:** The formula that expresses the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of various elements present in one molecule (or one formula unit) of the compound.
* It does **not** necessarily represent the actual count of atoms inside an isolated molecular entity; rather, it provides the fundamental stoichiometric building block of the lattice or molecule.
* **Examples:**
  * Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$): Empirical formula is $\text{CH}$ (ratio $1:1$).
  * Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$): Empirical formula is $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ (ratio $1:2:1$).
  * Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$): Empirical formula is $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (already in simplest integer ratio).
  * Sodium Chloride ($\text{NaCl}$): For ionic networks where distinct discrete molecules do not exist, the formula unit $\text{NaCl}$ is intrinsically an empirical formula.

***

### 1.3 The Molecular Formula ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
* **Strict NCERT Definition:** The chemical formula that indicates the exact, actual number of atoms of each constituent element present in one molecule of the compound.
* The molecular formula is always an integral multiple of the empirical formula:
  

$$
\boxed{\text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{Empirical Formula})_n}
$$

  where $n$ is a positive integer ($n \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$).

* The integer scaling factor $n$ is defined by the ratio of molar masses:
  

$$
\boxed{n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}} = \frac{M_{\text{molecular}}}{M_{\text{empirical}}}}
$$

***

### 1.4 Vapor Density and Avogadro's Hypothesis ⭐⭐⭐⭐
To calculate $n$, the molecular mass must either be provided directly (e.g., via mass spectrometry or colligative property measurements) or indirectly via **Vapour Density (V.D.)**.

* **NCERT Definition of Vapor Density:** The ratio of the mass of a certain volume of a gas or vapor to the mass of the same volume of hydrogen gas measured under identical conditions of temperature and pressure ($T, P$).

$$
\text{Vapour Density (V.D.)} = \left[\frac{\text{Mass of } V \text{ litres of gas at } T, P}{\text{Mass of } V \text{ litres of } \text{H}_2 \text{ gas at } T, P}\right]
$$

***

## 2. Derivations, Stoichiometric Frameworks & Methods

### 2.1 Derivation: Relationship Between Molar Mass and Vapor Density
Consider a gas $X$ of volume $V$ at temperature $T$ and pressure $P$.

1. By **Avogadro's Law**, equal volumes of all gases under the same temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules ($N$):
   

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of gas } X}{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of } \text{H}_2}
$$

2. Dividing both numerator and denominator by $N$:
   

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of 1 molecule of gas } X}{\text{Mass of 1 molecule of } \text{H}_2}
$$

3. Since molecular hydrogen is diatomic ($\text{H}_2$), mass of 1 molecule of $\text{H}_2 = 2 \times \text{mass of 1 atom of H} \approx 2 \times 1.008 \text{ u} \approx 2 \text{ u}$:
   

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Relative Molecular Mass of gas } X}{2}
$$

4. Rearranging gives the fundamental relation:
   

$$
\boxed{M_{\text{molecular}} = 2 \times \text{Vapour Density}}
$$

* **Unit & Dimension:** Vapor density is a dimensionless ratio. Molar mass derived using this relation carries units of $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ or unified atomic mass units ($\text{u}$).

***

### 2.2 Classical Organic Elemental Analysis (Liebig’s Combustion Method) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
When an unknown organic compound containing Carbon, Hydrogen, and Oxygen ($\text{C}_x\text{H}_y\text{O}_z$) is analyzed, it is combusted completely in the presence of excess pure $\text{O}_2$ and a $\text{CuO}$ (cupric oxide) catalyst at $700^\circ\text{C}-800^\circ\text{C}$:

$$
\text{C}_x\text{H}_y\text{O}_z + \left(x + \frac{y}{4} - \frac{z}{2}\right)\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{CuO}} x\,\text{CO}_2\,(g) + \frac{y}{2}\,\text{H}_2\text{O}\,(g)
$$

* $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ vapor is selectively absorbed in a pre-weighed U-tube containing **anhydrous calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$)** or magnesium perchlorate ($\text{Mg(ClO}_4)_2$).
* $\text{CO}_2$ gas is absorbed in another pre-weighed U-tube containing concentrated **potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$) solution** (caustic potash).

#### Quantitative Deduction:
Given mass of organic sample combusted $= w\text{ g}$.
* Let increase in mass of $\text{CaCl}_2$ tube $= m_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}\text{ g}$.
* Let increase in mass of $\text{KOH}$ tube $= m_{\text{CO}_2}\text{ g}$.

1. **Percentage of Carbon:**
   Since $44\text{ g}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ contains $12\text{ g}$ of carbon:
   

$$
\boxed{\% \text{ C} = \frac{12}{44} \times \frac{m_{\text{CO}_2}}{w} \times 100\%}
$$

2. **Percentage of Hydrogen:**
   Since $18\text{ g}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ contains $2.016\text{ g} \approx 2\text{ g}$ of hydrogen:
   

$$
\boxed{\% \text{ H} = \frac{2}{18} \times \frac{m_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}}{w} \times 100\%}
$$

3. **Percentage of Oxygen (by difference):**
   

$$
\boxed{\% \text{ O} = 100.00\% - (\% \text{ C} + \% \text{ H} + \% \text{ other heteroatoms})}
$$

***

### 2.3 The Standard Algorithm for Determining Empirical Formula
The calculation must be laid out systematically using a standard tabular layout:

| Step | Column Header | Operation / Formula | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | Element | List symbols of elements ($E_i$) | Catalog constituents |
| **2** | Percentage by mass ($\% w_i$) | Given or deduced values | Establish basis: Assume $100\text{ g}$ sample |
| **3** | Atomic Mass ($A_i$) | Standard atomic weights ($\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) | Fundamental physical constants |
| **4** | Relative number of moles ($n_i$) |

$$
n_i = \frac{\% w_i}{A_i}
$$

| Converts grams to molar amount |
| **5** | Simplest Molar Ratio |

$$
r_i = \frac{n_i}{n_{\text{min}}}
$$

 where $n_{\text{min}} = \min(n_1, n_2, \dots)$ | Normalizes smallest element to $1.00$ |

| **6** | Simplest Whole Number Ratio | Multiply all $r_i$ by the smallest integer $k \in \{2, 3, 4, \dots\}$ if fractions occur | Yields integer stoichiometry |

***

## 3. High-Yield Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

### Example 1: Classic NCERT Textbook Problem ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem:** A compound contains $4.07\%$ hydrogen, $24.27\%$ carbon, and $71.65\%$ chlorine. Its molar mass is $98.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$. What are its empirical and molecular formulas?

#### Solution:
**Step 1: Check consistency of data**

$$
\sum \% = 4.07 + 24.27 + 71.65 = 99.99\% \approx 100\%
$$

Oxygen is not present.

**Step 2: Tabular calculation (Basis = $100\text{ g}$ of compound)**

| Element | Mass $\%$ | Atomic Mass ($\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) | Moles of Element ($n = \frac{\%}{A}$) | Molar Ratio ($\div 2.021$) | Simplest Whole Number Ratio |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $\text{C}$ | $24.27$ | $12.01$ | $\frac{24.27}{12.01} = 2.021$ | $\frac{2.021}{2.021} = 1.00$ | $1$ |
| $\text{H}$ | $4.07$ | $1.008$ | $\frac{4.07}{1.008} = 4.038$ | $\frac{4.038}{2.021} = 1.998 \approx 2$ | $2$ |
| $\text{Cl}$ | $71.65$ | $35.45$ | $\frac{71.65}{35.45} = 2.021$ | $\frac{2.021}{2.021} = 1.00$ | $1$ |

* **Empirical Formula:** $\boxed{\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}}$

**Step 3: Determination of Molecular Formula**
1. Empirical formula mass:
   

$$
M_{\text{empirical}} = 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 35.45 = 49.48\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

2. Ratio $n$:
   

$$
n = \frac{M_{\text{molecular}}}{M_{\text{empirical}}} = \frac{98.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}{49.48\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 2.00 \implies n = 2
$$

3. Molecular Formula:
   

$$
\text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{CH}_2\text{Cl})_2 = \boxed{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{Cl}_2} \quad \text{(Dichloroethane)}
$$

***

### Example 2: Fractional Ratio Resolution (Advanced NCERT Archetype) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem:** An oxide of iron contains $69.94\%$ iron and $30.06\%$ dioxygen by mass. Determine the empirical formula of the oxide. (Atomic masses: $\text{Fe} = 55.85\text{ u}$, $\text{O} = 16.00\text{ u}$).

#### Solution:
**Tabular execution:**

| Element | Mass $\%$ | Atomic Mass | Moles ($n$) | Simplest Ratio | Whole Number Ratio |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $\text{Fe}$ | $69.94$ | $55.85$ | $\frac{69.94}{55.85} = 1.252$ | $\frac{1.252}{1.252} = 1.00$ | $1.00 \times 2 = \mathbf{2}$ |
| $\text{O}$ | $30.06$ | $16.00$ | $\frac{30.06}{16.00} = 1.879$ | $\frac{1.879}{1.252} = 1.501 \approx 1.5$ | $1.5 \times 2 = \mathbf{3}$ |

* **Critical Step:** A molar ratio of $1 : 1.5$ cannot be rounded off to $1:1$ or $1:2$. It must be cleared of fractions by multiplying all terms by $2$.
* **Empirical Formula:** $\boxed{\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3} \quad \text{(Iron(III) oxide / Ferric oxide)}$

***

### Example 3: Hydrate Analysis and Gas-Phase Vapor Density ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem:** A crystalline hydrated salt contains $25.6\%$ $\text{Cu}$, $12.8\%$ $\text{S}$, $25.6\%$ $\text{O}$ (in the sulfate anion framework), and $36.0\%$ water of crystallization. Determine its formula. 
(Atomic mass: $\text{Cu} = 63.5$, $\text{S} = 32.0$, $\text{O} = 16.0$, $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18.0$).

#### Solution:
When water of crystallization is specified, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ should be treated as a single composite pseudo-component with a molar mass of $18.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.

| Constituent | $\%$ Mass | Formula Mass | Moles ($n$) | Ratio ($\div 0.400$) | Integer |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $\text{Cu}$ | $25.6$ | $63.5$ | $\frac{25.6}{63.5} = 0.403$ | $\frac{0.403}{0.400} \approx 1.00$ | $1$ |
| $\text{S}$ | $12.8$ | $32.0$ | $\frac{12.8}{32.0} = 0.400$ | $\frac{0.400}{0.400} = 1.00$ | $1$ |
| $\text{O}$ (anionic) | $25.6$ | $16.0$ | $\frac{25.6}{16.0} = 1.600$ | $\frac{1.600}{0.400} = 4.00$ | $4$ |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | $36.0$ | $18.0$ | $\frac{36.0}{18.0} = 2.000$ | $\frac{2.000}{0.400} = 5.00$ | $5$ |

* The structural empirical representation: $\text{Cu}_1\text{S}_1\text{O}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$
* **Final Formula:** $\boxed{\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}} \quad \text{(Copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate)}$

***

### Example 4: Combustion Analysis of an Unknown Hydrocarbon ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem:** Complete combustion of $0.220\text{ g}$ of an organic compound containing only $\text{C}$, $\text{H}$, and $\text{O}$ gave $0.440\text{ g}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ and $0.180\text{ g}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. If its vapor density is $44$, deduce its empirical and molecular formulas.

#### Solution:
**Step 1: Quantify mass of constituent elements**
1. Mass of Carbon:
   

$$
m_{\text{C}} = \frac{12}{44} \times 0.440\text{ g} = 0.120\text{ g}
$$

2. Mass of Hydrogen:
   

$$
m_{\text{H}} = \frac{2}{18} \times 0.180\text{ g} = 0.020\text{ g}
$$

3. Mass of Oxygen:
   

$$
m_{\text{O}} = m_{\text{total}} - (m_{\text{C}} + m_{\text{H}}) = 0.220\text{ g} - (0.120\text{ g} + 0.020\text{ g}) = 0.080\text{ g}
$$

**Step 2: Percentage composition calculation**

$$
\% \text{C} = \frac{0.120}{0.220} \times 100 = 54.55\%
$$

$$
\% \text{H} = \frac{0.020}{0.220} \times 100 = 9.09\%
$$

$$
\% \text{O} = \frac{0.080}{0.220} \times 100 = 36.36\%
$$

**Step 3: Molar ratio calculation**

| Element | Mass ($\text{g}$) | Atomic Mass | Moles ($n$) | Simplest Ratio ($\div 0.005$) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $\text{C}$ | $0.120$ | $12.0$ | $\frac{0.120}{12} = 0.010$ | $\frac{0.010}{0.005} = 2$ |
| $\text{H}$ | $0.020$ | $1.0$ | $\frac{0.020}{1} = 0.020$ | $\frac{0.020}{0.005} = 4$ |
| $\text{O}$ | $0.080$ | $16.0$ | $\frac{0.080}{16} = 0.005$ | $\frac{0.005}{0.005} = 1$ |

* **Empirical Formula:** $\boxed{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}}$
* Empirical Formula Mass:
  

$$
M_{\text{empirical}} = 2(12.0) + 4(1.0) + 16.0 = 44.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

**Step 4: Molecular Formula determination**
1. Molar Mass:
   

$$
M_{\text{molecular}} = 2 \times \text{V.D.} = 2 \times 44 = 88.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

2. Integer multiple $n$:
   

$$
n = \frac{88.0}{44.0} = 2
$$

3. Molecular Formula:
   

$$
(\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O})_2 = \boxed{\text{C}_4\text{H}_8\text{O}_2}
$$

***

## 4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

### Trap 1: The "Diatomic Molecule Atomic Mass" Trap
* **The Error:** When dividing mass percentages of diatomic gases like nitrogen or oxygen to find moles, students often use the molecular weight ($M_{\text{N}_2} = 28$ or $M_{\text{O}_2} = 32$) instead of the atomic weight ($A_{\text{N}} = 14$ or $A_{\text{O}} = 16$).
* **The Rule:** An empirical formula shows the ratio of individual **atoms**, not molecules. **Always divide the mass percentage by the gram atomic mass ($A$), never by the molecular mass ($M_m$).**

### Trap 2: Premature and Inadmissible Rounding Off
* **The Error:** A student obtains a ratio like $1.33$ or $1.50$ and rounds it directly to $1.0$ or $2.0$.
* **The Rule:** Only round off if the value is within $\pm 0.05$ of an integer (e.g., $1.98 \to 2$ or $1.02 \to 1$).
  * If the ratio contains $\approx 0.50$, multiply every ratio in the set by $2$.
  * If the ratio contains $\approx 0.33$ or $\approx 0.67$, multiply every ratio in the set by $3$.
  * If the ratio contains $\approx 0.25$ or $\approx 0.75$, multiply every ratio in the set by $4$.

### Trap 3: The "Forgotten Oxygen" Blindspot
* **The Error:** An exam problem provides percentages of $\text{C}$, $\text{H}$, and $\text{N}$, and the student immediately begins computing moles without checking whether the percentages total $100\%$.
* **The Rule:** **Always sum the given percentages before calculating.** If $\sum \% < 100\%$ and no other element is mentioned, the remainder is implicitly **Oxygen**:
  

$$
\% \text{ O} = 100\% - \sum (\% \text{ given elements})
$$

### Trap 4: Confusing Vapor Density with Absolute Gas Density
* **The Error:** Students mistakenly equate absolute density ($\rho$ in $\text{g}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}$) with relative vapor density (dimensionless).
* **The Rule:**
  

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\rho_{\text{gas}}}{\rho_{\text{H}_2}} \quad \text{(dimensionless)}
$$

  At Standard Temperature and Pressure ($\text{STP}: T = 273.15\text{ K}, P = 1\text{ bar}$, where molar volume $V_m = 22.7\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, or traditional NTP: $1\text{ atm}$, $V_m = 22.4\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$):
  

$$
\text{Molar Mass } M = \rho_{\text{STP}} (\text{g}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}) \times 22.7\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

  

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{M}{2}
$$

***

## 5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Competitive Exams (JEE / NEET)

### Golden Point 1: Direct Mole Ratio from Raw Masses
You do **not** need to convert raw masses to percentages first if sample masses are already given in grams.

$$
n_i = \frac{m_i (\text{in grams})}{A_i}
$$

Skip the step $\frac{m_i}{m_{\text{total}}} \times 100 \implies \% \implies \frac{\%}{A_i}$. Dividing grams directly by atomic mass gives identical molar ratios while cutting arithmetic in half.

### Golden Point 2: The Fractional Decimals Decoding Table
Commit these standard fraction-to-multiplier conversions to memory:

$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
Decimal Ending & Fraction Equivalent & Required Integer Multiplier  (k) \\
\hline
0.20 \text{ or } 0.80 & 1/5 \text{ or } 4/5 & \times 5 \\
\hline
0.25 \text{ or } 0.75 & 1/4 \text{ or } 3/4 & \times 4 \\
\hline
0.33 \text{ or } 0.67 & 1/3 \text{ or } 2/3 & \times 3 \\
\hline
0.50 & 1/2 & \times 2 \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

### Golden Point 3: Minimum Molecular Weight Shortcut
Questions often ask: *"A biomolecule contains $0.32\%$ Sulfur by mass. What is its minimum molecular weight?"*
* **Principle:** For the minimum possible molecular weight, each molecule must contain **at least one single atom** of that element ($x = 1$).
  

$$
\% \text{ Element} = \frac{1 \times A_{\text{element}}}{M_{\text{min}}} \times 100
$$

  

$$
\boxed{M_{\text{min}} = \frac{A_{\text{element}} \times 100}{\% \text{ Element}}}
$$

* **Application:**
  

$$
M_{\text{min}} = \frac{32 \times 100}{0.32} = 10{,}000\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

### Golden Point 4: Hydrocarbon Combustion Quick-Check
For any hydrocarbon $\text{C}_x\text{H}_y$, the ratio of carbon to hydrogen moles can be found directly from combustion products without calculating percentages:

$$
\frac{x}{y} = \frac{n_{\text{C}}}{n_{\text{H}}} = \frac{n_{\text{CO}_2}}{2 \times n_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}} = \frac{\left(\frac{m_{\text{CO}_2}}{44}\right)}{2 \times \left(\frac{m_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}}{18}\right)} = \frac{m_{\text{CO}_2} \times 9}{m_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \times 44}
$$

## COMPREHENSIVE LECTURE NOTE: STOICHIOMETRY, BALANCED CHEMICAL EQUATIONS, AND LIMITING REAGENT

***

# SECTION 1: IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & FOUNDATIONAL CONCEPTS

***

### 1.1 The Meaning and Etymology of Stoichiometry
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
The word **Stoichiometry** originates from two Greek words:
- **Stoicheion** ($\sigma\tau o\iota\chi\epsilon\tilde{\iota}o\nu$): meaning *element*
- **Metron** ($\mu\acute{\epsilon}\tau\rho o\nu$): meaning *measure*

**Rigorous Definition:**  
> **Stoichiometry** is the quantitative study of the reactants consumed and products formed in a chemical reaction based on the conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions.

The numerical coefficients appearing before the chemical formulas in a balanced chemical equation are called **stoichiometric coefficients**. They represent the relative numbers of moles, molecules, or volumes (for gases at identical $T$ and $P$) participating in the transformation.

***

### 1.2 Fundamental Physical Laws Governing Chemical Equations
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Every balanced chemical reaction satisfies two fundamental conservation principles:

#### 1. Law of Conservation of Mass (Antoine Lavoisier, 1789)
> In any physical change or chemical reaction, total mass is neither created nor destroyed; the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.

$$
\sum m_{\text{reactants}} = \sum m_{\text{products}}
$$

*Molecular interpretation:* Atoms cannot be created or annihilated during ordinary chemical rearrangements; atomic nuclei remain unchanged, and only bonds (electronic configurations) are reorganized.

#### 2. Law of Definite Proportions / Composition (Joseph Proust, 1799)
> A given chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by weight, irrespective of its source or method of preparation.

#### 3. Gay-Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes (1808) & Avogadro’s Hypothesis (1811)
> When gases react, they do so in volumes that bear a simple whole-number ratio to one another and to the volumes of products (if gaseous), provided all measurements are carried out at identical temperature ($T$) and pressure ($P$).

Avogadro's interpretation states:

$$
V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } P)
$$

Hence, the ratio of stoichiometric coefficients of gaseous species directly equals their volume ratio.

***

### 1.3 Microscopic vs. Macroscopic Interpretations of a Chemical Equation
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Consider the complete combustion of methane:

$$
\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2\,O_2(g) \longrightarrow CO_2(g) + 2\,H_2O(g)}
$$

A balanced chemical equation can be interpreted on four distinct quantitative planes:

| Level of Interpretation | Reactant 1 ($\mathrm{CH_4}$) | Reactant 2 ($\mathrm{O_2}$) | Product 1 ($\mathrm{CO_2}$) | Product 2 ($\mathrm{H_2O}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Molecular level** | $1\text{ molecule}$ | $2\text{ molecules}$ | $1\text{ molecule}$ | $2\text{ molecules}$ |
| **2. Molar level** | $1\text{ mol}$ | $2\text{ mol}$ | $1\text{ mol}$ | $2\text{ mol}$ |
| **3. Mass level** | $16.04\text{ g}$ | $2 \times 32.00 = 64.00\text{ g}$ | $44.01\text{ g}$ | $2 \times 18.02 = 36.04\text{ g}$ |
| **4. Gaseous Volume ($T, P$)** | $1\text{ volume (or } V\text{ L)}$ | $2\text{ volumes (or } 2V\text{ L)}$ | $1\text{ volume (or } V\text{ L)}$ | $2\text{ volumes (or } 2V\text{ L)}$ |

**Verification of Law of Conservation of Mass:**

$$
\text{Mass of Reactants} = 16.04\text{ g} + 64.00\text{ g} = 80.04\text{ g}
$$

$$
\text{Mass of Products} = 44.01\text{ g} + 36.04\text{ g} = 80.05\text{ g} \approx 80.04\text{ g}
$$

> **NCERT Critical Note:** Moles are conserved **only** if the total stoichiometric coefficients happen to be identical on both sides, which is generally not required. Mass and atomic counts are **always strictly conserved**; total moles and total volumes are **not necessarily conserved**.

***

### 1.4 State Symbols and Thermodynamic/Reaction Notations
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐

Chemical formulas must be accompanied by physical state designators:
- $\mathrm{(s)}$: solid
- $\mathrm{(l)}$: pure liquid
- $\mathrm{(g)}$: gas or vapor
- $\mathrm{(aq)}$: aqueous solution (substance dissolved in excess water where water is the solvent)

Special reaction condition notations placed over or under the reaction arrow:
- $\Delta$: thermal energy applied (heating)
- $h\nu$: photochemical activation (ultraviolet/visible light irradiation)
- $\text{Cat.}$: catalyst present to lower activation energy without stoichiometric consumption
- $\mathrm{P, T}$: explicit operating pressure and temperature

***

### 1.5 The Limiting Reagent (Limiting Reactant) Concept
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In laboratory and industrial chemical syntheses, reactants are rarely mixed in exact stoichiometric proportions.

**Strict NCERT Definition:**
> The reactant which is present in the least stoichiometric amount and gets consumed first in a reaction is called the **Limiting Reagent (L.R.)**. It limits or restricts the maximum amount of product that can be formed.

- **Excess Reagent (E.R.):** The reactant(s) remaining unreacted after the complete consumption of the limiting reagent.
- **Fundamental Rule:** All theoretical yields of products and amounts of excess reactants consumed **must be calculated solely with reference to the Limiting Reagent**.

***

# SECTION 2: CHEMICAL DERIVATIONS, MATHEMATICAL FORMALISMS & MECHANISMS

***

### 2.1 The Master Stoichiometric Equation (Extent of Reaction)
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Consider the generalized irreversible homogeneous chemical reaction:

$$
a\,\mathrm{A} + b\,\mathrm{B} \longrightarrow c\,\mathrm{C} + d\,\mathrm{D}
$$

Where:
- $\mathrm{A, B}$ = Reactants
- $\mathrm{C, D}$ = Products
- $a, b, c, d$ = Stoichiometric coefficients

Let $n_{\mathrm{A},0}, n_{\mathrm{B},0}$ be the initial moles present at $t = 0$.  
Let $\xi$ (Greek letter *xi*) be the **extent of reaction** (in moles).

At any progress stage or completion:

$$
\Delta n_i = \nu_i \cdot \xi
$$

Where $\nu_i$ is the stoichiometric coefficient with signs assigned by convention:
- $\nu_{\mathrm{reactants}} = -a, -b$ (consumed)
- $\nu_{\mathrm{products}} = +c, +d$ (formed)

Therefore:

$$
n_{\mathrm{A}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{A},0} - a\xi
$$

$$
n_{\mathrm{B}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{B},0} - b\xi
$$

$$
n_{\mathrm{C}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{C},0} + c\xi
$$

$$
n_{\mathrm{D}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{D},0} + d\xi
$$

Equating the extent of reaction $\xi$ across all participating species:

$$
\boxed{\xi = \frac{-\Delta n_{\mathrm{A}}}{a} = \frac{-\Delta n_{\mathrm{B}}}{b} = \frac{\Delta n_{\mathrm{C}}}{c} = \frac{\Delta n_{\mathrm{D}}}{d}}
$$

Or in terms of moles consumed ($n_{\text{consumed}}$) and produced ($n_{\text{produced}}$):

$$
\boxed{\frac{n_{\mathrm{A, consumed}}}{a} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{B, consumed}}}{b} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{C, produced}}}{c} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{D, produced}}}{d}}
$$

***

### 2.2 Mathematical Criterion for Identifying the Limiting Reagent
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

To determine which reactant is the limiting reagent when initial moles $n_{\mathrm{A},0}$ and $n_{\mathrm{B},0}$ are given:

1. Compute the **Stoichiometric Ratio Index ($\mathcal{R}$)** for every reactant:

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{A},0}}{a} = \frac{w_{\mathrm{A}} / M_{\mathrm{A}}}{a}
$$

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{B},0}}{b} = \frac{w_{\mathrm{B}} / M_{\mathrm{B}}}{b}
$$

Where:
- $w$ = mass in grams
- $M$ = molar mass in $\mathrm{g\cdot mol^{-1}}$

2. Compare the indices:
- If $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}} < \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}}$, then **$\mathrm{A}$ is the Limiting Reagent (L.R.)** and **$\mathrm{B}$ is the Excess Reagent (E.R.)**.
- If $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}} < \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}$, then **$\mathrm{B}$ is the Limiting Reagent (L.R.)** and **$\mathrm{A}$ is the Excess Reagent (E.R.)**.
- If $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}} = \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}}$, the reactants are in **stoichiometric proportions**; both are consumed completely simultaneously, leaving no excess reactant.

3. Quantities at reaction termination ($t \to \infty$ for $100\%$ yield):
Assuming $\mathrm{A}$ is the L.R. ($\xi_{\max} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{A},0}}{a}$):

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{A, final}} = 0}
$$

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{B, unreacted}} = n_{\mathrm{B},0} - \left(\frac{b}{a}\right)n_{\mathrm{A},0} = b \cdot (\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}} - \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}})}
$$

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{C, formed}} = \left(\frac{c}{a}\right)n_{\mathrm{A},0} = c \cdot \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}
$$

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{D, formed}} = \left(\frac{d}{a}\right)n_{\mathrm{A},0} = d \cdot \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}
$$

***

### 2.3 Conversions Involving Gas Volumes: STP Standards
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Gas volumes must be converted to moles using the Ideal Gas Law:

$$
P \cdot V = n \cdot R \cdot T \implies n = \frac{P \cdot V}{R \cdot T}
$$

#### Critical Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP) Distinctions:

**1. Old IUPAC Convention (Pre-1982, still frequently used in Indian State Boards and older competitive exams):**
- Temperature: $T = 0\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 273.15\text{ K}$
- Pressure: $P = 1.0\text{ atm} = 101.325\text{ kPa} = 760\text{ torr}$

$$
V_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1\text{ mol} \times 0.082057\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \times 273.15\text{ K}}{1.0\text{ atm}} \approx \mathbf{22.414\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 22.4\text{ L}}
$$

**2. Modern IUPAC Standard (Adopted in latest NCERT editions):**
- Temperature: $T = 0\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 273.15\text{ K}$
- Pressure: $P = 1.0\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa} = 100\text{ kPa}$

$$
V_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1\text{ mol} \times 0.083145\text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \times 273.15\text{ K}}{1.0\text{ bar}} \approx \mathbf{22.711\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 22.7\text{ L}}
$$

> **Instruction for Students:** If the question explicitly states $P = 1\text{ bar}$, use $22.7\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$. If it states $1\text{ atm}$ or does not specify, $22.4\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ is commonly accepted, but clarify the assumption in board answers.

***

### 2.4 Percent Yield and Percent Purity Equations
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

In real chemical transformations, the experimental mass isolated is less than the theoretical mass due to competing side-reactions, incomplete equilibrium, mechanical losses during filtration, crystallization, or washing.

#### 1. Percentage Yield

$$
\boxed{\text{Percentage Yield (\%)} = \frac{\text{Actual Yield (Experimental)}}{\text{Theoretical Yield (Stoichiometric)}} \times 100\%}
$$

*Note:* Both actual and theoretical yields must be in identical units (both in grams, or both in moles).

#### 2. Percentage Purity of Impure Reactant Samples
When an impure sample of reactant $\mathrm{X}$ reacts:

$$
\boxed{\text{Percentage Purity (\%)} = \frac{\text{Pure mass of substance participating in reaction}}{\text{Total mass of impure sample taken}} \times 100\%}
$$

$$
\text{Pure mass of reactant} = \text{Total mass of sample} \times \left(\frac{\%\text{ Purity}}{100}\right)
$$

# SECTION 3: HIGH-YIELD SOLVED PROBLEMS & NCERT EXEMPLAR ARCHETYPES

***

### Archetype 1: Classic Gravimetric Stoichiometry

#### Problem 1 (NCERT In-Text Adapted)
Calculate the amount of water ($\mathrm{g}$) produced by the complete combustion of $32.00\text{ g}$ of methane.

**Solution:**
- **Step 1: Write the balanced chemical equation:**

$$
\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2\,O_2(g) \longrightarrow CO_2(g) + 2\,H_2O(l)}
$$

- **Step 2: Calculate molar masses:**
  - $M(\mathrm{CH_4}) = 12.011 + 4(1.008) = 16.043\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 16.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - $M(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 2(1.008) + 15.999 = 18.015\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 18.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

- **Step 3: Convert mass of given substance to moles:**

$$
n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = \frac{w}{M} = \frac{32.00\text{ g}}{16.043\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 1.9946\text{ mol} \approx 2.00\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 4: Apply stoichiometric molar ratio:**
  From the balanced equation:
  

$$
1\text{ mol }\mathrm{CH_4} \implies 2\text{ mol }\mathrm{H_2O}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2O})_{\text{produced}} = 2 \times n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = 2 \times 1.9946\text{ mol} = 3.9892\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 5: Convert moles of product to mass in grams:**

$$
w(\mathrm{H_2O}) = n \times M = 3.9892\text{ mol} \times 18.015\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 71.865\text{ g}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Mass of }\mathrm{H_2O} = 72.0\text{ g}\quad (\text{to } 3\text{ significant figures})}
$$

***

### Archetype 2: Limiting Reagent in the Haber Process

#### Problem 2 (NCERT Exercise 1.24 / Exemplar Archetype)
$50.0\text{ kg}$ of $\mathrm{N_2(g)}$ and $10.0\text{ kg}$ of $\mathrm{H_2(g)}$ are mixed to produce $\mathrm{NH_3(g)}$.
1. Identify the limiting reagent.
2. Calculate the mass of $\mathrm{NH_3(g)}$ formed.
3. Determine the mass of the excess reagent remaining unreacted.

**Reaction Conditions:** $T = 700\text{ K}$, $P = 200\text{ atm}$, Catalyst: $\mathrm{Fe/K_2O/Al_2O_3}$.

$$
\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3\,H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\,NH_3(g)}
$$

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Convert all initial quantities to moles ($n = \frac{w\text{ in grams}}{M}$):**
  - Molar mass of $\mathrm{N_2} = 28.02\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - Molar mass of $\mathrm{H_2} = 2.016\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - Molar mass of $\mathrm{NH_3} = 17.03\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

$$
w(\mathrm{N_2}) = 50.0\text{ kg} = 50.0 \times 10^3\text{ g} = 5.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2})_0 = \frac{5.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}}{28.02\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 1784.44\text{ mol} = 1.784 \times 10^3\text{ mol}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{H_2}) = 10.0\text{ kg} = 10.0 \times 10^3\text{ g} = 1.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2})_0 = \frac{1.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}}{2.016\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 4960.32\text{ mol} = 4.960 \times 10^3\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 2: Determine the Limiting Reagent using the Ratio Index ($\mathcal{R} = \frac{n_0}{\text{coefficient}}$):**
  - For $\mathrm{N_2}$ (coefficient $a = 1$):
    

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \frac{1784.44}{1} = 1784.44\text{ mol}
$$

  - For $\mathrm{H_2}$ (coefficient $b = 3$):
    

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \frac{4960.32}{3} = 1653.44\text{ mol}
$$

$$
\because \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{H_2}} < \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{N_2}} \quad (1653.44 < 1784.44)
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{H_2} \text{ is the Limiting Reagent (L.R.)}; \quad \mathbf{N_2} \text{ is the Excess Reagent (E.R.)}}
$$

- **Step 3: Calculate the mass of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ produced:**
  Stoichiometric ratio relates $\mathrm{NH_3}$ directly to the L.R. ($\mathrm{H_2}$):

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{NH_3})_{\text{produced}}}{2} = \frac{n(\mathrm{H_2})_{\text{consumed}}}{3}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{NH_3})_{\text{produced}} = \frac{2}{3} \times n(\mathrm{H_2})_0 = \frac{2}{3} \times 4960.32\text{ mol} = 3306.88\text{ mol}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{NH_3}) = n \times M = 3306.88\text{ mol} \times 17.031\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 56319.47\text{ g} \approx 56.32\text{ kg}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Mass of }\mathrm{NH_3}\text{ formed} = 56.3\text{ kg}}
$$

- **Step 4: Calculate the mass of excess $\mathrm{N_2}$ remaining:**
  Moles of $\mathrm{N_2}$ consumed:

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{consumed}} = \frac{1}{3} \times n(\mathrm{H_2})_{\text{consumed}} = \frac{1}{3} \times 4960.32 = 1653.44\text{ mol}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{remaining}} = n(\mathrm{N_2})_0 - n(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{consumed}} = 1784.44 - 1653.44 = 131.00\text{ mol}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{remaining}} = 131.00\text{ mol} \times 28.02\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 3670.62\text{ g} \approx 3.67\text{ kg}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Mass of excess }\mathrm{N_2}\text{ unreacted} = 3.67\text{ kg}}
$$

***

### Archetype 3: Gravimetric Analysis with Percent Purity

#### Problem 3 (NCERT Exemplar Advanced Variant)
A sample of impure limestone ($\mathrm{CaCO_3}$) weighing $25.0\text{ g}$ is treated with excess dilute hydrochloric acid ($\mathrm{HCl}$). The liberated carbon dioxide gas occupies $4.92\text{ L}$ measured at $300\text{ K}$ and $1.0\text{ atm}$ pressure. Assuming the impurities are completely inert to acid, calculate the percentage purity of the limestone sample.  
(Given: $R = 0.0821\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, atomic masses: $\mathrm{Ca}=40.0, \mathrm{C}=12.0, \mathrm{O}=16.0$).

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Write the balanced chemical reaction:**

$$
\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) + 2\,HCl(aq) \longrightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)\uparrow}
$$

- **Step 2: Calculate moles of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ liberated using the Ideal Gas Equation:**

$$
n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = \frac{P \cdot V}{R \cdot T} = \frac{1.0\text{ atm} \times 4.92\text{ L}}{0.0821\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \times 300\text{ K}}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = \frac{4.92}{24.63} = 0.19976\text{ mol} \approx 0.200\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 3: Relate moles of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ to moles of pure $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$:**
  From the stoichiometry of the equation:
  

$$
1\text{ mol }\mathrm{CaCO_3} \equiv 1\text{ mol }\mathrm{CO_2}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{CaCO_3})_{\text{pure}} = n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.200\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 4: Calculate mass of pure $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$:**
  

$$
M(\mathrm{CaCO_3}) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 100.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{CaCO_3})_{\text{pure}} = 0.200\text{ mol} \times 100.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 20.0\text{ g}
$$

- **Step 5: Compute percentage purity:**

$$
\%\text{ Purity} = \frac{w(\mathrm{CaCO_3})_{\text{pure}}}{w(\text{impure sample})} \times 100 = \frac{20.0\text{ g}}{25.0\text{ g}} \times 100
$$

$$
\boxed{\%\text{ Purity of Limestone} = 80.0\%}
$$

***

### Archetype 4: Multi-Step Sequential Stoichiometry

#### Problem 4 (JEE Main/Advanced & Chemistry Olympiad Archetype)
Potassium chlorate ($\mathrm{KClO_3}$) decomposes upon heating in the presence of $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ catalyst to produce oxygen gas. This generated oxygen is subsequently reacted with red phosphorus ($\mathrm{P_4}$) to form phosphorus pentoxide ($\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}$). Calculate the minimum mass of $\mathrm{KClO_3}$ required to completely oxidize $6.20\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{P_4}$.  
(Atomic masses: $\mathrm{K} = 39.1, \mathrm{Cl} = 35.5, \mathrm{O} = 16.0, \mathrm{P} = 31.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Write both individual balanced reactions:**
  - Reaction 1 (Decomposition):
    

$$
2\,\mathrm{KClO_3(s)} \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\mathrm{MnO_2}} 2\,\mathrm{KCl(s)} + 3\,\mathrm{O_2(g)}
$$

  - Reaction 2 (Oxidation):
    

$$
\mathrm{P_4(s)} + 5\,\mathrm{O_2(g)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{P_4O_{10}(s)}
$$

- **Step 2: Method A - Coupling via the common intermediate ($\mathrm{O_2}$):**
  Multiply Reaction 1 by $5$ and Reaction 2 by $3$ to eliminate intermediate $\mathrm{O_2}$:
  

$$
10\,\mathrm{KClO_3} \longrightarrow 10\,\mathrm{KCl} + 15\,\mathrm{O_2}
$$

$$
3\,\mathrm{P_4} + 15\,\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow 3\,\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}
$$

  Summing the coupled net stoichiometric equation:

$$
10\,\mathrm{KClO_3} + 3\,\mathrm{P_4} \longrightarrow 10\,\mathrm{KCl} + 3\,\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}
$$

- **Step 3: Compute moles of $\mathrm{P_4}$:**
  

$$
M(\mathrm{P_4}) = 4 \times 30.974 = 123.90\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{P_4}) = \frac{6.20\text{ g}}{123.90\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 0.05004\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 4: Relate $\mathrm{KClO_3}$ to $\mathrm{P_4}$ using the coupled master ratio:**

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{KClO_3})}{10} = \frac{n(\mathrm{P_4})}{3}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{KClO_3}) = \frac{10}{3} \times n(\mathrm{P_4}) = \frac{10}{3} \times 0.05004\text{ mol} = 0.1668\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 5: Calculate mass of $\mathrm{KClO_3}$:**
  

$$
M(\mathrm{KClO_3}) = 39.10 + 35.45 + 3(16.00) = 122.55\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{KClO_3}) = 0.1668\text{ mol} \times 122.55\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 20.44\text{ g}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Required Mass of }\mathrm{KClO_3} = 20.4\text{ g}}
$$

### Archetype 5: Reactions in Aqueous Solution (Neutralization Titration)

#### Problem 5
What volume of $0.250\text{ M }\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ solution is required to neutralize completely $50.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.400\text{ M }\mathrm{NaOH}$ solution?

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Balanced chemical equation:**

$$
\mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq) + 2\,NaOH(aq) \longrightarrow Na_2SO_4(aq) + 2\,H_2O(l)}
$$

- **Step 2: Stoichiometric mole relationship:**

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{H_2SO_4})}{1} = \frac{n(\mathrm{NaOH})}{2}
$$

Recall that $n = M \times V_{\text{litres}}$ or $\text{millimoles} = M \times V_{\mathrm{mL}}$:

$$
M_1 \cdot V_1 = \frac{M_2 \cdot V_2}{2}
$$

Where:
- $M_1 = 0.250\text{ M}$, $V_1 = ?$
- $M_2 = 0.400\text{ M}$, $V_2 = 50.0\text{ mL}$

- **Step 3: Solve for $V_1$:**

$$
0.250 \times V_1 = \frac{0.400 \times 50.0}{2} = \frac{20.0}{2} = 10.0\text{ mmol}
$$

$$
V_1 = \frac{10.0\text{ mmol}}{0.250\text{ mmol}\cdot\mathrm{mL}^{-1}} = 40.0\text{ mL}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Required Volume of }\mathrm{H_2SO_4} = 40.0\text{ mL}}
$$

# SECTION 4: EXAMINER TRAPS, PITFALLS & FREQUENT STUDENT ERRORS

***

### Trap 1: Comparing Given Masses Directly Instead of Moles
- **The Trap:** In the reaction $\mathrm{N_2 + 3\,H_2 \to 2\,NH_3}$, if given $5\text{ g of }\mathrm{N_2}$ and $5\text{ g of }\mathrm{H_2}$, students conclude that because the masses are equal, neither is limiting, or they assume the one with the larger stoichiometric coefficient ($\mathrm{H_2}$) is limiting.
- **The Truth:** Stoichiometry operates **exclusively in the currency of moles**, never in grams!
  

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2}) = \frac{5}{28} = 0.178\text{ mol} \implies \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \frac{0.178}{1} = 0.178
$$

  

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2}) = \frac{5}{2} = 2.50\text{ mol} \implies \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \frac{2.50}{3} = 0.833
$$

  Here, $\mathrm{N_2}$ is the Limiting Reagent, even though both had equal starting mass!

***

### Trap 2: Neglecting the Stoichiometric Coefficient when Calculating L.R.
- **The Trap:** Calculating moles of reactants and choosing the one with fewer moles as the Limiting Reagent.
- **The Truth:** Moles must be divided by their stoichiometric coefficients.
  Example: $2\text{ mol of A}$ and $3\text{ mol of B}$ reacting according to:
  

$$
\mathrm{A + 2\,B \longrightarrow C}
$$

  Here, $n(\mathrm{A}) = 2 < n(\mathrm{B}) = 3$. But:
  

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{A})}{1} = 2.0; \quad \frac{n(\mathrm{B})}{2} = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5
$$

  $\mathrm{B}$ is the Limiting Reagent despite having a higher initial mole count.

***

### Trap 3: STP Volume Confusion ($22.4\text{ L}$ vs $22.7\text{ L}$)
- **The Trap:** Indiscriminately substituting $22.4\text{ L}$ for gaseous conversions when pressure is specified in bar.
- **The Truth:**
  - $1.0\text{ atm}$ at $273.15\text{ K} \implies V_m = \mathbf{22.414\text{ L}}$
  - $1.0\text{ bar}$ ($10^5\text{ Pa}$) at $273.15\text{ K} \implies V_m = \mathbf{22.711\text{ L}}$
  A $1.3\%$ error occurs if interchanged, resulting in lost marks in high-precision board/entrance scoring.

***

### Trap 4: Applying Gay-Lussac’s Volume Law to Liquids or Solids
- **The Trap:** If a question states $\mathrm{2\,H_2(g) + O_2(g) \to 2\,H_2O(l)}$, students frequently assert that $2\text{ volumes of }\mathrm{H_2} + 1\text{ volume of }\mathrm{O_2} \to 2\text{ volumes of water}$.
- **The Truth:** Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes applies **only to gases**! The product here is liquid water ($\mathrm{l}$), whose volume is determined by its density ($\approx 1.0\text{ g}\cdot\mathrm{mL}^{-1}$), not by molar gas volume ($22.4\text{ L}$).

***

### Trap 5: Forgetting to Subtract Consumed Amount for Excess Reactant
- **The Trap:** When asked for the "amount of excess reactant remaining", students often report the amount of excess reactant *consumed*.
- **The Truth:**
  

$$
\text{Amount Remaining} = \text{Initial Amount Present} - \text{Amount Consumed by L.R.}
$$

### 5.1 The Principle of Atom Conservation (POAC)
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (The ultimate speed hack for competitive exams)

When reactions involve complex balancing, multi-step mechanisms, or unknown side pathways, balancing every equation is unnecessary if an element passes quantitatively from a reactant into a single specific product.

> **POAC Rule:** For an element $X$ conserved completely from reactant to product:
> 

$$
\text{Total moles of atom } X \text{ in reactants} = \text{Total moles of atom } X \text{ in products}
$$

$$
(\text{Atoms of } X \text{ per molecule of Reactant}) \times n_{\text{Reactant}} = (\text{Atoms of } X \text{ per molecule of Product}) \times n_{\text{Product}}
$$

#### Example Application of POAC:
All carbon in $26.4\text{ g of }\mathrm{CO_2}$ was obtained by burning a sample of an organic compound $\mathrm{C_xH_y}$. How many grams of carbon were present?
- Applying POAC on Carbon ($\mathrm{C}$):

$$
1 \times n(\mathrm{C}) = 1 \times n(\mathrm{CO_2})
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{C}) = \frac{26.4\text{ g}}{44.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 0.60\text{ mol}
$$

$$
\text{Mass of Carbon} = 0.60\text{ mol} \times 12.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{7.2\text{ g}}
$$

*(Solved in 15 seconds without writing or balancing any combustion equation!)*

***

### 5.2 The 3-Line ICE Matrix Table Method
For any reaction with limiting reagent problems, systematically construct the **ICE** table:
- **I** = Initial quantity ($\text{mol}$)
- **C** = Change in quantity ($\Delta n = \nu \cdot \xi$)
- **E** = Equilibrium / End quantity ($n_{\text{final}} = n_0 + \Delta n$)

#### General Matrix:

$$
\begin{array}{lcccc}
\text{Reaction:} & a\,\mathrm{A} & + & b\,\mathrm{B} & \longrightarrow & c\,\mathrm{C} \\
\hline
\mathbf{I}\text{ (Initial)} & n_{\mathrm{A},0} & & n_{\mathrm{B},0} & & 0 \\
\mathbf{C}\text{ (Change)} & -a\xi_{\max} & & -b\xi_{\max} & & +c\xi_{\max} \\
\mathbf{E}\text{ (End)} & n_{\mathrm{A},0} - a\xi_{\max} & & n_{\mathrm{B},0} - b\xi_{\max} & & c\xi_{\max} \\
\end{array}
$$

Where $\xi_{\max} = \min\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{A},0}}{a}, \frac{n_{\mathrm{B},0}}{b}\right)$.

2. **M** — Convert all quantities to **Moles**.

4. **P** — **Proceed** with product calculations using that L.R. quotient only.

# Reactions in Solutions and Concentration Units: Mass Percentage, Mole Fraction, Molarity, and Molality

# Class 11 Chemistry: Chapter 1 — Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

## Topic: Reactions in Solutions and Concentration Terms

***

## 1. Pedagogical Theory, Molecular Concepts & Concentration Terms

In chemical laboratories, the majority of reactions are conducted in homogeneous liquid mixtures known as **solutions**. A solution consists of at least two components:
* **Solute:** The substance present in a lesser amount, dissolved in the medium.
* **Solvent:** The dispersion medium present in the largest quantity, determining the physical state of the solution.

When stoichiometric calculations are applied to reactions occurring in solution, quantifying the exact amount of solute dispersed per unit amount of solvent or solution becomes essential. This relative quantity is known as the **concentration** of the solution.

***

### Term 1: Mass Percentage ($w/w\,\%$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐ (High Board Frequency / Foundation)

#### Strict NCERT Definition
Mass percentage is defined as the number of parts by mass of a solute present per $100$ parts by mass of the solution.

$$
\text{Mass } \% \text{ of solute} = \left( \frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Total mass of solution}} \right) \times 100
$$

Where:

$$
\text{Total mass of solution} = \text{Mass of solute} + \text{Mass of solvent}
$$

* **Units:** Dimensionless (expressed as $\% \, w/w$ or percentage by mass).
* **Temperature Dependence:** **Temperature-independent** because mass is conserved and invariant with temperature ($\frac{\partial m}{\partial T} = 0$).

***

### Term 2: Mole Fraction ($\chi$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Crucial for Raoult's Law, Solutions & Liquid Mixtures)

#### Strict NCERT Definition
Mole fraction of a component is the ratio of the number of moles of that component to the total number of moles of all the components present in the solution.

Consider a binary system consisting of component $\text{A}$ (solvent) and component $\text{B}$ (solute):
* Let the mass of $\text{A}$ be $w_{\text{A}}$ and its molar mass be $M_{\text{A}}$.
* Let the mass of $\text{B}$ be $w_{\text{B}}$ and its molar mass be $M_{\text{B}}$.

The amounts (in moles) are:

$$
n_{\text{A}} = \frac{w_{\text{A}}}{M_{\text{A}}}, \quad n_{\text{B}} = \frac{w_{\text{B}}}{M_{\text{B}}}
$$

The mole fractions are given by:

$$
\chi_{\text{A}} = \frac{n_{\text{A}}}{n_{\text{A}} + n_{\text{B}}}
$$

$$
\chi_{\text{B}} = \frac{n_{\text{B}}}{n_{\text{A}} + n_{\text{B}}}
$$

For an $i$-component system:

$$
\chi_i = \frac{n_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} n_j}
$$

#### Fundamental Mathematical Property

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{k} \chi_i = \chi_{\text{A}} + \chi_{\text{B}} = \frac{n_{\text{A}} + n_{\text{B}}}{n_{\text{A}} + n_{\text{B}}} = 1
$$

* **Units:** Dimensionless.
* **Temperature Dependence:** **Temperature-independent** because mole numbers depend solely on invariant masses.

***

### Term 3: Molarity ($M$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board & Competitive Exam Frequency)

#### Strict NCERT Definition
Molarity is defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved in exactly one litre ($1\text{ L}$ or $1\text{ dm}^3$) of the solution.

$$
M = \frac{\text{Number of moles of solute } (n_{\text{solute}})}{\text{Volume of solution in litres } (V_{\text{L}})}
$$

If mass of solute is $w_{\text{B}}$ (in grams), molar mass is $M_{\text{B}}$ (in $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$), and solution volume is $V$ (in $\text{mL}$ or $\text{cm}^3$):

$$
M = \frac{w_{\text{B}}}{M_{\text{B}}} \times \frac{1000}{V\,(\text{mL})}
$$

* **SI Unit:** $\text{mol}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$
* **Common Laboratory Unit:** $\text{mol}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}$ or $\text{mol}\cdot\text{dm}^{-3}$ (denoted as $\text{M}$).
* **Temperature Dependence:** **Temperature-dependent**. Since liquids expand upon heating, the volume of the solution increases with an increase in temperature ($V \propto T$), causing the molarity to decrease:

$$
\left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial T} \right)_P < 0
$$

***

### Term 4: Molality ($m$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board Frequency — Preferred Concentration Unit)

#### Strict NCERT Definition
Molality is defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved per one kilogram ($1\text{ kg}$) of the solvent.

$$
m = \frac{\text{Number of moles of solute } (n_{\text{solute}})}{\text{Mass of solvent in kilograms } (W_{\text{solvent in kg}})}
$$

If the mass of the solvent is expressed in grams ($w_{\text{A}}$ in $\text{g}$):

$$
m = \frac{w_{\text{B}}}{M_{\text{B}}} \times \frac{1000}{w_{\text{A}}\,(\text{g})}
$$

* **SI Unit:** $\text{mol}\cdot\text{kg}^{-1}$ (often denoted by $\text{m}$).
* **Temperature Dependence:** **Temperature-independent**. Molality involves only mass quantities; it does not change with temperature fluctuations.
* **Pedagogical Note:** Molality is universally preferred over Molarity in precise thermodynamic and colligative property studies (e.g., elevation in boiling point, depression in freezing point).

***

## 2. Mathematical Derivations & Stoichiometric Principles

### A. Dilution Equation
When a solvent is added to an existing concentrated solution, the volume increases, but the absolute number of solute moles remains strictly constant.

$$
\text{Moles before dilution} = \text{Moles after dilution}
$$

Since $n = M \times V$:

$$
\boxed{M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2}
$$

* **Condition:** Must involve the same solute without chemical reaction. $V_1$ and $V_2$ must be in identical units of volume.

***

### B. Mixing of Non-Reacting Solutions (Same Solute)
When mixing solutions of the same solute:
* Solution 1: Molarity $M_1$, Volume $V_1 \implies n_1 = M_1 V_1$
* Solution 2: Molarity $M_2$, Volume $V_2 \implies n_2 = M_2 V_2$

Assuming additive volumes ($V_{\text{total}} = V_1 + V_2$):

$$
n_{\text{total}} = n_1 + n_2 = M_1 V_1 + M_2 V_2
$$

$$
\boxed{M_{\text{mix}} = \frac{M_1 V_1 + M_2 V_2}{V_1 + V_2}}
$$

***

### C. Master Derivation: Molarity ($M$) from Density ($d$) and Mass Percentage ($x\%$)

Let a solution have a density $d\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$ and contain $x\%$ solute by mass.
* Consider exactly $100\text{ g}$ of solution.
* Mass of solute ($w_{\text{B}}$) $= x\text{ g}$.
* Volume of $100\text{ g}$ solution:

$$
V = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Density}} = \frac{100}{d}\text{ mL} = \frac{100}{1000 \cdot d}\text{ L} = \frac{1}{10 \cdot d}\text{ L}
$$

* Moles of solute ($n_{\text{B}}$):

$$
n_{\text{B}} = \frac{x}{M_{\text{B}}}
$$

* Molarity:

$$
M = \frac{n_{\text{B}}}{V_{\text{L}}} = \frac{x / M_{\text{B}}}{1 / (10 \cdot d)}
$$

$$
\boxed{M = \frac{x \times d \times 10}{M_{\text{B}}}}
$$

*(where $d$ is in $\text{g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$, $M_{\text{B}}$ is in $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, and $x$ is the numerical value of percentage by mass).*

***

### D. Master Derivation: Interconversion of Molarity ($M$) and Molality ($m$)

Let the molarity of a solution be $M\text{ mol}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}$, the density be $d\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$ (or $\text{g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$), and the molar mass of the solute be $M_{\text{B}}\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.

1. Consider exactly $1\text{ L}$ ($1000\text{ mL}$) of solution:
   * Number of moles of solute $= M\text{ mol}$.
   * Mass of solute ($w_{\text{B}}$) $= M \times M_{\text{B}}\text{ g}$.
2. Total mass of $1000\text{ mL}$ solution:
   

$$
\text{Mass}_{\text{solution}} = V \times d = 1000 \times d\text{ g}
$$

3. Mass of solvent ($w_{\text{A}}$):
   

$$
w_{\text{A}} = \text{Mass}_{\text{solution}} - \text{Mass}_{\text{solute}} = (1000\,d - M \cdot M_{\text{B}})\text{ g}
$$

4. Expressing mass of solvent in kilograms:
   

$$
W_{\text{solvent}} = \frac{1000\,d - M \cdot M_{\text{B}}}{1000}\text{ kg}
$$

5. Applying definition of Molality ($m = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{W_{\text{solvent}}}$):

$$
\boxed{m = \frac{M \times 1000}{1000\,d - M \cdot M_{\text{B}}}}
$$

Rearranging to solve for Molarity ($M$):

$$
\boxed{M = \frac{m \cdot d \times 1000}{1000 + m \cdot M_{\text{B}}}}
$$

***

### E. Master Derivation: Mole Fraction ($\chi_{\text{B}}$) and Molality ($m$) Relation

Let $\chi_{\text{B}}$ be the mole fraction of solute and $\chi_{\text{A}} = 1 - \chi_{\text{B}}$ be the mole fraction of solvent with molar mass $M_{\text{A}}$ (in $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).

$$
\frac{\chi_{\text{B}}}{\chi_{\text{A}}} = \frac{n_{\text{B}}}{n_{\text{A}}} = \frac{n_{\text{B}}}{w_{\text{A}} / M_{\text{A}}} = \frac{n_{\text{B}} \times M_{\text{A}}}{w_{\text{A}}}
$$

Multiply and divide the RHS by $1000$:

$$
\frac{\chi_{\text{B}}}{\chi_{\text{A}}} = \left( \frac{n_{\text{B}} \times 1000}{w_{\text{A}}} \right) \times \frac{M_{\text{A}}}{1000} = m \times \frac{M_{\text{A}}}{1000}
$$

$$
\boxed{m = \frac{\chi_{\text{B}}}{1 - \chi_{\text{B}}} \times \frac{1000}{M_{\text{A}}}}
$$

For dilute aqueous solutions ($M_{\text{A}} = 18\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, and $\chi_{\text{B}} \ll 1 \implies 1 - \chi_{\text{B}} \approx 1$):

$$
m \approx \chi_{\text{B}} \times \frac{1000}{18} \approx 55.55 \cdot \chi_{\text{B}}
$$

## 3. High-Yield Worked Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

### Problem 1 (Standard NCERT Textbook Archetype)
**Question:** A solution is prepared by dissolving $2\text{ g}$ of substance $\text{A}$ in $18\text{ g}$ of water. Calculate the mass percentage of the solute.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
* Mass of solute ($w_{\text{A}}$) $= 2\text{ g}$
* Mass of solvent ($w_{\text{solvent}}$) $= 18\text{ g}$
* Total mass of solution $= 2\text{ g} + 18\text{ g} = 20\text{ g}$

$$
\text{Mass } \% \text{ of A} = \frac{\text{Mass of A}}{\text{Total mass of solution}} \times 100 = \frac{2\text{ g}}{20\text{ g}} \times 100 = \mathbf{10\%}
$$

***

### Problem 2 (NCERT Exemplar / Competitive Archetype)
**Question:** Calculate the molarity of a solution of ethanol in water in which the mole fraction of ethanol ($\chi_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}}$) is $0.040$. (Assume the density of water to be $1.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$).

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. Given $\chi_{\text{solute}} = 0.040$, thus $\chi_{\text{water}} = 1 - 0.040 = 0.960$.
2. This implies that for every $0.040\text{ moles}$ of ethanol, there are $0.960\text{ moles}$ of water.
3. Mass of water ($w_{\text{water}}$):
   

$$
w_{\text{water}} = n_{\text{water}} \times M_{\text{water}} = 0.960\text{ mol} \times 18.02\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 17.30\text{ g}
$$

4. Since the density of water is $1.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$, and in dilute solutions the volume of solute is negligible:
   

$$
V_{\text{solution}} \approx V_{\text{water}} = \frac{17.30\text{ g}}{1.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}} = 17.30\text{ mL} = 0.01730\text{ L}
$$

5. Calculate Molarity:
   

$$
M = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution in L}}} = \frac{0.040\text{ mol}}{0.01730\text{ L}} = \mathbf{2.31\text{ M}}
$$

***

### Problem 3 (Comprehensive Multi-Concept Problem)
**Question:** Commercial concentrated nitric acid contains $69\%$ $\text{HNO}_3$ by mass and has a density of $1.41\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$.
Calculate:
1. Molarity of the acid.
2. Volume of concentrated acid required to prepare $250\text{ mL}$ of $2.0\text{ M }\text{HNO}_3$.
3. Molality of the concentrated acid.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

#### Part 1: Molarity
* Mass percentage $x = 69\%$
* Density $d = 1.41\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$
* Molar mass of $\text{HNO}_3$ ($M_{\text{B}}$):
  

$$
M_{\text{B}} = 1 + 14 + 3(16) = 63.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Using the direct derivation:

$$
M = \frac{x \times d \times 10}{M_{\text{B}}} = \frac{69 \times 1.41 \times 10}{63.0} = \frac{972.9}{63.0} = \mathbf{15.44\text{ M}}
$$

#### Part 2: Dilution Calculation
Using the dilution formula $M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2$:
* $M_1 = 15.44\text{ M}$
* $V_1 = ?$
* $M_2 = 2.0\text{ M}$
* $V_2 = 250\text{ mL}$

$$
15.44 \times V_1 = 2.0 \times 250
$$

$$
V_1 = \frac{500}{15.44} = \mathbf{32.38\text{ mL}}
$$

#### Part 3: Molality
In $100\text{ g}$ of concentrated acid:
* Mass of $\text{HNO}_3$ $= 69\text{ g} \implies n_{\text{HNO}_3} = \frac{69}{63} = 1.095\text{ mol}$
* Mass of solvent (water) $= 100\text{ g} - 69\text{ g} = 31\text{ g} = 0.031\text{ kg}$

$$
m = \frac{1.095\text{ mol}}{0.031\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{35.32\text{ m}}
$$

### Problem 4 (Stoichiometry in Aqueous Reactions)
**Question:** What volume of $0.150\text{ M }\text{HCl}$ is required to react completely with $0.530\text{ g}$ of pure anhydrous $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$?

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\,(\text{s}) + 2\text{HCl}\,(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaCl}\,(\text{aq}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}\,(\text{l}) + \text{CO}_2\,(\text{g})
$$

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. Molar mass of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$:
   

$$
M = 2(23) + 12 + 3(16) = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

2. Moles of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$:
   

$$
n_{\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3} = \frac{0.530\text{ g}}{106.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 0.0050\text{ mol}
$$

3. Stoichiometry:
   

$$
1\text{ mol }\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \text{ requires } 2\text{ mol }\text{HCl}
$$

   

$$
n_{\text{HCl}} = 2 \times 0.0050 = 0.010\text{ mol}
$$

4. Volume of $\text{HCl}$ solution:
   

$$
V = \frac{n}{M} = \frac{0.010\text{ mol}}{0.150\text{ mol}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}} = 0.0667\text{ L} = \mathbf{66.7\text{ mL}}
$$

## 4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

| Trap Number | Concept Pitfall | Common Student Error | Pedagogically Correct Method |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Trap 1** | **Mass of Solution vs. Solvent** | Using mass of *solvent* instead of *solution* in the denominator of mass percentage. | Total Mass $= m_{\text{solute}} + m_{\text{solvent}}$. Always verify whether the problem gives "solvent mass" or "solution mass". |
| **Trap 2** | **Denominator in Molality vs. Molarity** | Dividing moles by mass of *solution* in molality calculations. | Molality ($m$) uniquely uses mass of **solvent** ($w_{\text{solvent}}$) in $\text{kg}$. Molarity uses volume of **solution**. |
| **Trap 3** | **Volume Non-Additivity** | Assuming $500\text{ mL}$ ethanol $+ 500\text{ mL}$ water $= 1000\text{ mL}$ solution. | Due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding, volume contracts ($V_{\text{final}} < V_1 + V_2$). Always calculate solution mass from density to find actual volume ($V = \frac{m_{\text{solution}}}{d}$). |
| **Trap 4** | **Temperature Invariance** | Stating that molality or mole fraction changes when a solution is heated. | Only volumetric terms ($\text{Molarity}$, $\% \, w/v$) change with temperature. Mass and moles are invariant under thermal expansion. |
| **Trap 5** | **Pre-Dilution Unit Mismatches** | Mixing $\text{cm}^3$ and $\text{L}$ in $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$. | Both volume parameters must share identical dimensions. Convert all to $\text{L}$ or $\text{mL}$ prior to calculation. |

## 5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Competitive Exams

### 1. Pure Water Concentration Constants
* Density of pure water: $d = 1.00\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1} = 1000\text{ g}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}$
* Molar mass of water: $M = 18.015\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
* **Molarity of Pure Water:**
  

$$
M = \frac{1000\text{ g}}{18.015\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = \mathbf{55.55\text{ M}}
$$

* **Molality of Pure Water:**
  

$$
m = \frac{1000\text{ g} / 18.015}{1\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{55.55\text{ m}}
$$

***

### 2. The $10 \cdot d$ Shortcut for Molarity
Whenever density ($d$) and weight percentage ($x\%$) are given:

$$
M = \frac{10 \times x \times d}{M_{\text{solute}}}
$$

* *Example:* $98\%\, \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ with $d = 1.80\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$:
  

$$
M = \frac{10 \times 98 \times 1.80}{98} = \mathbf{18\text{ M}}
$$

  *(Solves in under 5 seconds).*

***

### 3. Molality for Extremely Dilute Aqueous Solutions
For very dilute aqueous solutions ($M \le 10^{-2}\text{ M}$):
* $d \approx 1.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$
* Mass of solute $\ll$ Mass of solvent

$$
\mathbf{Molarity } (M) \approx \mathbf{Molality } (m)
$$

***

### 4. Direct Conversion: Mole Fraction to Molality

$$
m = \frac{\chi_{\text{B}}}{1 - \chi_{\text{B}}} \times \frac{1000}{M_{\text{solvent}}}
$$

For an aqueous solvent ($M_{\text{solvent}} = 18$):

$$
m = \frac{\chi_{\text{B}}}{1 - \chi_{\text{B}}} \times 55.55
$$

### Question A.1: Case-Based / Data-Driven Problem
**Tag:** [CBSE Competency Pattern — Case-Based, 4 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

#### Passage:
In an industrial ammonia synthesis plant operating on the Haber-Bosch process, nitrogen and hydrogen gases are fed into a catalytic converter at elevated pressure and temperature:

$$
\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}
$$

During a quality and yield assessment trial, a reactor of constant volume $V$ is charged with $56.0\text{ kg}$ of pure dry $\mathrm{N_2}$ and $12.0\text{ kg}$ of pure dry $\mathrm{H_2}$. At equilibrium, the total yield of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ collected is $34.0\text{ kg}$. 

1. Identify the limiting reagent in this industrial trial with rigorous stoichiometric justification. **(1 Mark)**
2. Calculate the theoretical maximum mass of ammonia (in $\text{kg}$) that can be formed from the given feed. **(1 Mark)**
3. Determine the mass (in $\text{kg}$) of the excess reagent remaining unreacted at theoretical completion. **(1 Mark)**
4. Calculate the percentage yield of the Haber-Bosch catalytic reactor in this specific trial. **(1 Mark)**

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:
* **Sub-part 1:**
  * Molar mass of $\mathrm{N_2} = 28.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$; Molar mass of $\mathrm{H_2} = 2.016\text{ g mol}^{-1} \approx 2.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
  * Number of moles of $\mathrm{N_2}$ fed:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2}) = \frac{56.0 \times 10^3\text{ g}}{28.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 2.0 \times 10^3\text{ mol}
$$

  * Number of moles of $\mathrm{H_2}$ fed:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2}) = \frac{12.0 \times 10^3\text{ g}}{2.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 6.0 \times 10^3\text{ mol}
$$

  * According to stoichiometry: $1\text{ mol }\mathrm{N_2}$ requires $3\text{ mol }\mathrm{H_2}$.  
    $2.0 \times 10^3\text{ mol }\mathrm{N_2}$ requires $3 \times (2.0 \times 10^3) = 6.0 \times 10^3\text{ mol }\mathrm{H_2}$.
  * Both reactants are present in exact stoichiometric proportions. Neither is in deficiency; however, under standard limiting reagent determination definitions, both are consumed simultaneously at $100\%$ completion, so either reactant dictates the maximum yield.  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for calculating moles of both gases, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for comparing stoichiometric ratio and stating stoichiometric balance).*

* **Sub-part 2:**
  * From stoichiometry: $1\text{ mol }\mathrm{N_2} \equiv 2\text{ mol }\mathrm{NH_3}$.
  * Theoretical moles of $\mathrm{NH_3}$:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{NH_3})_{\text{theo}} = 2 \times 2.0 \times 10^3\text{ mol} = 4.0 \times 10^3\text{ mol}
$$

  * Molar mass of $\mathrm{NH_3} = 14.0 + 3(1.0) = 17.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
  * Maximum mass of $\mathrm{NH_3}$:
    

$$
m(\mathrm{NH_3})_{\text{theo}} = 4.0 \times 10^3\text{ mol} \times 17.0\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 68.0 \times 10^3\text{ g} = 68.0\text{ kg}
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for mole-to-mass relation, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for correct calculation with units: $68.0\text{ kg}$).*

* **Sub-part 3:**
  * Since both reactants are in exact stoichiometric equivalence ($1:3$ molar ratio), the theoretical excess reagent remaining is $0.0\text{ kg}$.  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for deducing zero excess reagent remaining at complete theoretical reaction).*

* **Sub-part 4:**
  * Percentage Yield formula:
    

$$
\text{Percentage Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual Yield}}{\text{Theoretical Yield}} \times 100
$$

  * Substitution:
    

$$
\text{Percentage Yield} = \frac{34.0\text{ kg}}{68.0\text{ kg}} \times 100 = 50.0\%
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for percentage yield formula, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for final value: $50.0\%$).*

### Question A.2: Assertion-Reason Problems
**Tag:** [CBSE Competency Pattern — AR, 1 Mark each]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

**Directions for Q(i) to Q(iv):**  
In the following questions, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the correct option:  
- (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
- (b) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
- (c) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
- (d) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

***

#### Q.2(i)
* **Assertion (A):** The empirical formula of glucose is $\mathrm{CH_2O}$, whereas its molecular formula is $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$.  
* **Reason (R):** The molecular formula of a compound is always an integral multiple of its empirical formula, defined by $\text{Molecular Formula} = n \times \text{Empirical Formula}$, where $n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}$.

**Answer:** **(a)**  
**Step-by-Step Explanation & Marking:**  
* The atomic ratio of $\mathrm{C:H:O}$ in glucose is $6:12:6 = 1:2:1$, giving the simplest whole-number ratio empirical formula $\mathrm{CH_2O}$ (Empirical Formula mass $= 12 + 2 + 16 = 30\text{ g mol}^{-1}$).  
* Molecular mass $= 180\text{ g mol}^{-1}$. Therefore, $n = \frac{180}{30} = 6$. The reason correctly accounts for the mathematical derivation of the molecular formula from the empirical formula.  
*(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for option (a) with valid reasoning).*

***

#### Q.2(ii)
* **Assertion (A):** One molal ($\mathrm{1\ m}$) aqueous solution of urea is more concentrated than one molar ($\mathrm{1\ M}$) aqueous solution of urea at $298\text{ K}$ (density of water $= 1.00\text{ g mL}^{-1}$).  
* **Reason (R):** Molality depends on the mass of the solvent, whereas molarity depends on the total volume of the solution, which includes solute volume.

**Answer:** **(d)**  
**Step-by-Step Explanation & Marking:**  
* In a $1\text{ M}$ solution, $1\text{ mole}$ of urea is dissolved in $1000\text{ mL}$ of solution.  
  

$$
\text{Mass of solution} = V \times d \approx 1000\text{ mL} \times 1.02\text{ g/mL} > 1000\text{ g}
$$

  Solvent mass $= \text{Mass of solution} - \text{Mass of solute} (60\text{ g}) < 1000\text{ g}$.  
  Hence, $1\text{ M}$ solution contains $1\text{ mole}$ of solute in LESS than $1000\text{ g}$ of water, which makes its effective molality $> 1\text{ m}$.  
* Consequently, a $1\text{ M}$ solution is **more concentrated** than a $1\text{ m}$ solution, making Assertion (A) false. Reason (R) is fundamentally a true statement.  
*(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for option (d)).*

***

#### Q.2(iii)
* **Assertion (A):** When $20\text{ mL}$ of $\mathrm{H_2(g)}$ reacts with $10\text{ mL}$ of $\mathrm{O_2(g)}$ at constant temperature and pressure, water vapor produced occupies $20\text{ mL}$.  
* **Reason (R):** Gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction in a simple whole-number ratio by volume, provided all gases are at the same temperature and pressure (Gay-Lussac’s Law).

**Answer:** **(a)**  
**Step-by-Step Explanation & Marking:**  
* Reaction equation:
  

$$
2\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O(g)}
$$

* By Gay-Lussac’s Law of Combining Volumes:
  

$$
2\text{ volumes of }\mathrm{H_2} + 1\text{ volume of }\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 2\text{ volumes of }\mathrm{H_2O(g)}
$$

* $20\text{ mL}$ of $\mathrm{H_2}$ reacts with $10\text{ mL}$ of $\mathrm{O_2}$ to yield precisely $20\text{ mL}$ of $\mathrm{H_2O(g)}$. The reason states Gay-Lussac's Law, which directly explains the observation.  
*(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for option (a)).*

***

#### Q.2(iv)
* **Assertion (A):** The number of significant figures in $0.00250$ is 3, while in $2.50 \times 10^{-3}$ it is also 3.  
* **Reason (R):** Zeros preceding the first non-zero digit are not significant, whereas trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.

**Answer:** **(a)**  
**Step-by-Step Explanation & Marking:**  
* For $0.00250$: Leading zeros ($0.00$) indicate decimal position and are non-significant; the digits $2$ and $5$ are significant; the trailing zero after the decimal is significant $\rightarrow 3$ significant figures.  
* For scientific notation $N \times 10^x$: only digits in $N$ are counted ($2.50 \rightarrow 3$ significant figures). Reason gives the correct governing rule.  
*(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for option (a)).*

### Question A.3: HOTS & Multi-Concept Application Problem
**Tag:** [CBSE Competency Pattern — HOTS Analytical, 5 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

#### Problem Statement:
An organic liquid compound containing Carbon, Hydrogen, and Nitrogen is widely used in polymer resin synthesis. Complete combustion of $0.2100\text{ g}$ of this compound yields $0.4611\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and $0.2831\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{H_2O}$. 

In a separate Kjeldahl experiment, the nitrogen present in $0.1575\text{ g}$ of the same compound was converted quantitatively into $\mathrm{NH_3}$. This liberated ammonia was absorbed in $50.00\text{ mL}$ of $0.1000\text{ M }\mathrm{HCl}$. The unreacted excess acid required $27.50\text{ mL}$ of $0.1000\text{ M }\mathrm{NaOH}$ for complete neutralization.

Given that the vapor density of the compound is $30.05$:
1. Determine the percentage composition of $\mathrm{C}$, $\mathrm{H}$, and $\mathrm{N}$ in the organic compound. **(2 Marks)**
2. Calculate its empirical formula. **(1 Mark)**
3. Deduce its molecular formula. **(1 Mark)**
4. If $5.00\text{ g}$ of this compound is dissolved in $250.0\text{ mL}$ of water to form a solution of density $1.02\text{ g mL}^{-1}$, determine its molality ($\mathrm{m}$). **(1 Mark)**

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:

* **Step 1: Percentage of Carbon and Hydrogen**
  * Mass of Carbon in $\mathrm{CO_2}$:
    

$$
\% \mathrm{C} = \frac{12.011}{44.01} \times \frac{0.4611\text{ g}}{0.2100\text{ g}} \times 100 = \frac{0.12584}{0.2100} \times 100 = 59.92\%
$$

  * Mass of Hydrogen in $\mathrm{H_2O}$:
    

$$
\% \mathrm{H} = \frac{2 \times 1.008}{18.016} \times \frac{0.2831\text{ g}}{0.2100\text{ g}} \times 100 = \frac{0.03168}{0.2100} \times 100 = 15.08\%
$$

  *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for $\% \mathrm{C} = 59.92\%$, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for $\% \mathrm{H} = 15.08\%$).*

* **Step 2: Percentage of Nitrogen via Kjeldahl Analysis**
  * Total moles of $\mathrm{HCl}$ added:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{HCl})_{\text{initial}} = 50.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ L} \times 0.1000\text{ mol L}^{-1} = 5.000 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}
$$

  * Moles of $\mathrm{NaOH}$ required for back-titration:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{NaOH}) = 27.50 \times 10^{-3}\text{ L} \times 0.1000\text{ mol L}^{-1} = 2.750 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}
$$

  * Moles of unreacted $\mathrm{HCl} = n(\mathrm{NaOH}) = 2.750 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  * Moles of $\mathrm{HCl}$ neutralized by evolved $\mathrm{NH_3}$:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{HCl})_{\text{reacted}} = (5.000 - 2.750) \times 10^{-3} = 2.250 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}
$$

  * Since $\mathrm{NH_3 + HCl \rightarrow NH_4Cl}$, moles of $\mathrm{N} = 2.250 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol}$.
  * Mass of Nitrogen:
    

$$
m(\mathrm{N}) = 2.250 \times 10^{-3}\text{ mol} \times 14.007\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 0.03152\text{ g}
$$

  * Percentage of Nitrogen:
    

$$
\% \mathrm{N} = \frac{0.03152\text{ g}}{0.1575\text{ g}} \times 100 = 25.02\%
$$

  * Verification of Total Percentage:
    

$$
\sum \% = 59.92 + 15.08 + 25.02 = 100.02\% \approx 100\% \quad (\text{No oxygen present})
$$

  *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for mole back-titration equation, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for $\% \mathrm{N} = 25.0\%$).*

* **Step 3: Empirical Formula Calculation**

| Element | Percentage | Atomic Mass | Moles of Element | Simplest Molar Ratio | Whole Number Ratio |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $\mathrm{C}$ | $59.92$ | $12.01$ | $\frac{59.92}{12.01} = 4.989$ | $\frac{4.989}{1.786} = 2.79 \approx 2.8$ | - |

  * Let us evaluate the integer multiple:
    

$$
\text{Ratio } \mathrm{C:H:N} \approx 2.8 : 8.4 : 1 \Rightarrow \text{Multiply by } 5: 14 : 42 : 5
$$

  * Looking at the direct small integer relation:
    

$$
\mathrm{C} = \frac{59.92}{12.01} = 4.99 \approx 5.0
$$

    

$$
\mathrm{H} = \frac{15.08}{1.008} = 14.96 \approx 15.0
$$

    

$$
\mathrm{N} = \frac{25.00}{14.01} = 1.785
$$

    If we check ethylenediamine $\mathrm{C_2H_8N_2}$ ($M = 60.1$):
    $\% \mathrm{C} = \frac{24}{60.1} = 39.9\%$.  
    Here: Molar mass $= 2 \times \text{Vapor Density} = 2 \times 30.05 = 60.10\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.

x = \frac{60.10 \times 0.5992}{12.01} = 3.00

$$
y = \frac{60.10 \times 0.1508}{1.008} = 9.00

$$
z = \frac{60.10 \times 0.2500}{14.01} = 1.07 \approx 1

    *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for empirical formula derivation).*

* **Step 4: Molecular Formula**
  * $\text{Empirical Formula Mass} = 3(12.01) + 9(1.008) + 14.01 = 59.10\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
  * $\text{Molecular Mass} = 2 \times \text{Vapor Density} = 2 \times 30.05 = 60.10\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
  * $n = \frac{60.10}{59.10} \approx 1$.
  * Molecular Formula $= (\mathrm{C_3H_9N})_1 = \mathrm{C_3H_9N}$.  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for relation $\text{Molar Mass} = 2 \times \text{V.D.}$, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for final molecular formula).*

* **Step 5: Molality of Solution**
  * Mass of solute $= 5.00\text{ g}$. Moles of solute:
    

$$
n_2 = \frac{5.00\text{ g}}{59.1\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.0846\text{ mol}
$$

  * Volume of solution $= 250.0\text{ mL}$.  
  * Mass of solution $= 250.0\text{ mL} \times 1.02\text{ g mL}^{-1} = 255.0\text{ g}$.
  * Mass of solvent ($w_1$) $= 255.0\text{ g} - 5.00\text{ g} = 250.0\text{ g} = 0.250\text{ kg}$.
  * Molality ($m$):
    

$$
m = \frac{n_2}{w_1\text{ (kg)}} = \frac{0.0846\text{ mol}}{0.250\text{ kg}} = 0.338\text{ mol kg}^{-1} = 0.338\text{ m}
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for calculating mass of solvent correctly, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for final molality with unit).*

### Question B.1: NCERT Exercise 1.24 (Limiting Reagent & Residual Reactants)
**Tag:** [NCERT Exercise 1.24, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

#### Problem Statement:
Dinitrogen and dihydrogen react with each other to produce ammonia according to the chemical equation:

$$
\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightarrow 2NH_3(g)}
$$

1. Calculate the mass of ammonia produced if $2.00 \times 10^3\text{ g}$ of dinitrogen reacts with $1.00 \times 10^3\text{ g}$ of dihydrogen.
2. Will any of the two reactants remain unreacted?
3. If yes, which one and what would be its mass?

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:
* **Step 1: Mole calculation of reactants**
  

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2}) = \frac{2.00 \times 10^3\text{ g}}{28.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 71.43\text{ mol}
$$

  

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2}) = \frac{1.00 \times 10^3\text{ g}}{2.016\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 496.0\text{ mol}
$$

  *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for determining initial moles).*

* **Step 2: Identification of Limiting Reagent**
  * According to the balanced equation, $1\text{ mol of }\mathrm{N_2}$ requires $3\text{ mol of }\mathrm{H_2}$.
  * For $71.43\text{ mol of }\mathrm{N_2}$, required moles of $\mathrm{H_2}$:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2})_{\text{required}} = 3 \times 71.43 = 214.29\text{ mol}
$$

  * Available moles of $\mathrm{H_2} = 496.0\text{ mol} > 214.29\text{ mol}$.
  * Therefore, $\mathrm{N_2}$ is the **limiting reagent** and $\mathrm{H_2}$ is the **excess reagent**.  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for stoichiometric comparison and specifying limiting reagent).*

* **Step 3: Mass of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ Produced**
  * $1\text{ mol }\mathrm{N_2}$ produces $2\text{ mol }\mathrm{NH_3}$.
  * Moles of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ formed:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{NH_3}) = 2 \times 71.43 = 142.86\text{ mol}
$$

  * Mass of $\mathrm{NH_3}$:
    

$$
m(\mathrm{NH_3}) = 142.86\text{ mol} \times 17.03\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 2432.9\text{ g} \approx 2.43 \times 10^3\text{ g} \ (2.43\text{ kg})
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for mass of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ with correct significant figures).*

* **Step 4: Residual Unreacted Reagent**
  * Yes, $\mathrm{H_2}$ remains unreacted.
  * Moles of unreacted $\mathrm{H_2}$:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2})_{\text{excess}} = 496.0\text{ mol} - 214.29\text{ mol} = 281.71\text{ mol}
$$

  * Mass of unreacted $\mathrm{H_2}$:
    

$$
m(\mathrm{H_2}) = 281.71\text{ mol} \times 2.016\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 567.9\text{ g} \approx 5.68 \times 10^2\text{ g}
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for naming $\mathrm{H_2}$, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for final mass: $568\text{ g}$).*

### Question B.2: NCERT Exercise 1.36 (Stoichiometry of Precipitation)
**Tag:** [NCERT Exercise 1.36, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

#### Problem Statement:
Chlorine is prepared in the laboratory by treating manganese dioxide ($\mathrm{MnO_2}$) with aqueous hydrochloric acid according to the reaction:

$$
4\mathrm{HCl(aq)} + \mathrm{MnO_2(s)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} + \mathrm{MnCl_2(aq)} + \mathrm{Cl_2(g)}
$$

How many grams of $\mathrm{HCl}$ react completely with $5.0\text{ g}$ of manganese dioxide? (Atomic mass of $\mathrm{Mn} = 54.94\text{ u}$, $\mathrm{O} = 16.00\text{ u}$, $\mathrm{Cl} = 35.45\text{ u}$, $\mathrm{H} = 1.008\text{ u}$).

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:
* **Step 1: Calculate Molar Masses**
  

$$
M(\mathrm{MnO_2}) = 54.94 + 2(16.00) = 86.94\text{ g mol}^{-1}
$$

  

$$
M(\mathrm{HCl}) = 1.008 + 35.45 = 36.458\text{ g mol}^{-1}
$$

  
  *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for correct molar masses).*

* **Step 2: Molar Stoichiometry Ratio**
  From the balanced chemical equation:
  

$$
1\text{ mol }\mathrm{MnO_2} \equiv 86.94\text{ g}
$$

  

$$
4\text{ mol }\mathrm{HCl} \equiv 4 \times 36.458\text{ g} = 145.832\text{ g}
$$

  
  *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for quantitative molar link between $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ and $\mathrm{HCl}$).*

* **Step 3: Mass Calculation**
  

$$
86.94\text{ g of }\mathrm{MnO_2}\text{ reacts with } 145.832\text{ g of }\mathrm{HCl}
$$

  Therefore, $5.0\text{ g of }\mathrm{MnO_2}$ reacts with:
  

$$
m(\mathrm{HCl}) = \frac{145.832\text{ g}}{86.94\text{ g}} \times 5.0\text{ g} = 8.387\text{ g} \approx 8.4\text{ g}
$$

  
  *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for step calculation, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for final answer: $8.4\text{ g}$ with units).*

### Question B.3: NCERT Exercise 1.8 & 1.12 (Concentration Interconversion)
**Tag:** [NCERT Exercise Combined Standard, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

#### Problem Statement:
An aqueous solution of commercial concentrated nitric acid is labeled as $69\%$ by mass of $\mathrm{HNO_3}$ and has a density of $1.41\text{ g mL}^{-1}$.
1. Calculate the molarity of this nitric acid sample.
2. What volume of this concentrated acid is required to prepare $250.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.500\text{ M }\mathrm{HNO_3}$ solution?

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:
* **Step 1: Molarity Determination**
  * Let mass of solution $= 100\text{ g}$.
  * Mass of solute ($\mathrm{HNO_3}$) $= 69.0\text{ g}$.
  * Molar mass of $\mathrm{HNO_3} = 1.008 + 14.007 + 3(16.00) = 63.015\text{ g mol}^{-1} \approx 63.02\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
  * Moles of $\mathrm{HNO_3}$:
    

$$
n = \frac{69.0\text{ g}}{63.02\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 1.095\text{ mol}
$$

  * Volume of $100\text{ g}$ solution:
    

$$
V = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Density}} = \frac{100\text{ g}}{1.41\text{ g mL}^{-1}} = 70.92\text{ mL} = 0.07092\text{ L}
$$

  * Molarity ($M_1$):
    

$$
M_1 = \frac{n}{V\text{ (L)}} = \frac{1.095\text{ mol}}{0.07092\text{ L}} = 15.44\text{ M} \quad \left(\text{or } M = \frac{\% \times d \times 10}{M_w} = \frac{69 \times 1.41 \times 10}{63.02} = 15.44\text{ M}\right)
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for calculating moles and volume, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for obtaining $15.44\text{ M}$).*

* **Step 2: Dilution Equation**
  * Applying the dilution formula:
    

$$
M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2
$$

  * Where $M_1 = 15.44\text{ M}$, $M_2 = 0.500\text{ M}$, $V_2 = 250.0\text{ mL}$.
  * Solving for $V_1$:
    

$$
V_1 = \frac{M_2 V_2}{M_1} = \frac{0.500\text{ M} \times 250.0\text{ mL}}{15.44\text{ M}} = \frac{125.0}{15.44} = 8.096\text{ mL} \approx 8.10\text{ mL}
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for dilution formula, $1$ Mark for final value: $8.10\text{ mL}$).*

### Question C.1: NCERT Exemplar MCQ (Avogadro’s Number & Particulate Count)
**Tag:** [NCERT Exemplar Multiple Choice, 1 Mark]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

#### Problem Statement:
Which of the following contains the maximum number of atoms?
* (a) $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{Ag(s)}$ [Atomic mass $= 108\text{ u}$]
* (b) $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{Mg(s)}$ [Atomic mass $= 24\text{ u}$]
* (c) $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{O_2(g)}$ [Atomic mass $= 16\text{ u}$]
* (d) $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{Li(s)}$ [Atomic mass $= 7\text{ u}$]

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:
* **Mathematical Calculation:**
  

$$
\text{Number of atoms} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{molar mass}} \times N_A \times \text{atomicity}
$$

  * (a) $\text{Atoms in } 1.0\text{ g Ag} = \frac{1}{108} \times N_A = 0.00926\, N_A$
  * (b) $\text{Atoms in } 1.0\text{ g Mg} = \frac{1}{24} \times N_A = 0.0416\, N_A$
  * (c) $\text{Atoms in } 1.0\text{ g O_2} = \frac{1}{32} \times 2 \times N_A = \frac{1}{16} \times N_A = 0.0625\, N_A$
  * (d) $\text{Atoms in } 1.0\text{ g Li} = \frac{1}{7} \times N_A = 0.1428\, N_A$
* Since lithium has the lowest atomic mass among the monoatomic/diatomic species per gram, it yields the highest number of moles of atoms.
* **Correct Option:** **(d)**  
  *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for formula/atomicity relation, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for selecting option (d)).*

### Question C.2: NCERT Exemplar Subjective Analysis (Isotopic Abundances & Average Atomic Mass)
**Tag:** [NCERT Exemplar Subjective, 3 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐ [Core]

#### Problem Statement:
Naturally occurring boron consists of two stable isotopes: $^{10}\mathrm{B}$ having an accurate isotopic mass of $10.0129\text{ u}$, and $^{11}\mathrm{B}$ having an accurate isotopic mass of $11.0093\text{ u}$. The standard atomic mass of natural boron listed on the periodic table is $10.811\text{ u}$.
1. State the principle used to calculate the average atomic mass of an element existing as multiple isotopes.
2. Determine the fractional and percentage relative abundances of $^{10}\mathrm{B}$ and $^{11}\mathrm{B}$.

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:
* **Step 1: Formula / Principle**
  * Average atomic mass is the weighted average of the atomic masses of its naturally occurring isotopes, weighted by their relative fractional abundances:
    

$$
\bar{A} = \sum (f_i \times A_i) = f_1 A_1 + f_2 A_2
$$

    where $f_1 + f_2 = 1$.  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for principle and algebraic statement).*

* **Step 2: Algebraic Substitution**
  * Let the fractional abundance of $^{10}\mathrm{B}$ be $x$.
  * Then the fractional abundance of $^{11}\mathrm{B}$ is $(1 - x)$.
  * Substituting known values:
    

$$
10.811 = x(10.0129) + (1 - x)(11.0093)
$$

    

$$
10.811 = 10.0129x + 11.0093 - 11.0093x
$$

    

$$
10.811 - 11.0093 = -0.9964x
$$

    

$$
-0.1983 = -0.9964x
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for correct substitution and linear reduction).*

* **Step 3: Solution of Abundance Values**
  

$$
x = \frac{0.1983}{0.9964} = 0.1990
$$

  * Fractional abundance of $^{10}\mathrm{B} = 0.1990 \implies \mathbf{19.90\%}$
  * Fractional abundance of $^{11}\mathrm{B} = 1 - 0.1990 = 0.8010 \implies \mathbf{80.10\%}$  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for $^{10}\mathrm{B} = 19.90\%$, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for $^{11}\mathrm{B} = 80.10\%$).*

### Question C.3: NCERT Exemplar Advanced Analytical Problem
**Tag:** [NCERT Exemplar HOTS / Comprehensive, 5 Marks]  
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

#### Problem Statement:
A solid mixture weighing $5.000\text{ g}$ consisting only of barium oxide ($\mathrm{BaO}$) and calcium oxide ($\mathrm{CaO}$) is dissolved completely in excess hydrochloric acid:

$$
\mathrm{BaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow BaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l)}
$$

$$
\mathrm{CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l)}
$$

The resulting clear solution is subsequently treated with an excess of dilute sulfuric acid ($\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$), precipitating pure white insoluble barium sulfate ($\mathrm{BaSO_4}$), while calcium ions remain entirely in solution under the experimental dilution conditions:

$$
\mathrm{BaCl_2(aq) + H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow BaSO_4(s)\downarrow + 2HCl(aq)}
$$

The precipitate of $\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ is quantitatively filtered, washed thoroughly with deionized water, dried to constant weight, and found to weigh $4.668\text{ g}$.  
*(Molar masses: $\mathrm{Ba} = 137.33\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, $\mathrm{Ca} = 40.08\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, $\mathrm{S} = 32.06\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, $\mathrm{O} = 16.00\text{ g mol}^{-1}$)*.

1. Formulate the gravimetric stoichiometry connecting mass of $\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ to $\mathrm{BaO}$. **(1 Mark)**
2. Calculate the mass and percentage by mass of $\mathrm{BaO}$ in the original binary oxide mixture. **(2 Marks)**
3. Calculate the percentage by mass of $\mathrm{CaO}$ in the mixture. **(1 Mark)**
4. If the residual calcium ions in the filtrate are precipitated as calcium oxalate monohydrate ($\mathrm{CaC_2O_4 \cdot H_2O}$, Molar mass $= 146.11\text{ g mol}^{-1}$), what theoretical mass of oxalate precipitate would be recovered? **(1 Mark)**

#### Model Solution & Marking Scheme:

* **Step 1: Stoichiometric Relationship**
  * According to the reaction sequence:
    

$$
1\text{ mol }\mathrm{BaO} \rightarrow 1\text{ mol }\mathrm{BaCl_2} \rightarrow 1\text{ mol }\mathrm{BaSO_4}
$$

  * Molar mass of $\mathrm{BaO} = 137.33 + 16.00 = 153.33\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
  * Molar mass of $\mathrm{BaSO_4} = 137.33 + 32.06 + 4(16.00) = 233.39\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.  
  *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for molar masses, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for conservation of moles: $n(\mathrm{BaO}) = n(\mathrm{BaSO_4})$).*

* **Step 2: Mass and Percentage of $\mathrm{BaO}$**
  * Moles of $\mathrm{BaSO_4}$ isolated:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{BaSO_4}) = \frac{4.668\text{ g}}{233.39\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.02000\text{ mol}
$$

  * Moles of $\mathrm{BaO}$ present in original sample:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{BaO}) = 0.02000\text{ mol}
$$

  * Mass of $\mathrm{BaO}$:
    

$$
m(\mathrm{BaO}) = 0.02000\text{ mol} \times 153.33\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 3.0666\text{ g} \approx 3.067\text{ g}
$$

  * Mass percentage of $\mathrm{BaO}$:
    

$$
\% \mathrm{BaO} = \frac{3.0666\text{ g}}{5.000\text{ g}} \times 100 = 61.33\%
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $1$ Mark for mass of $\mathrm{BaO} = 3.067\text{ g}$, $1$ Mark for $\% \mathrm{BaO} = 61.33\%$).*

* **Step 3: Mass and Percentage of $\mathrm{CaO}$**
  * Mass of $\mathrm{CaO} = \text{Total mass} - m(\mathrm{BaO})$:
    

$$
m(\mathrm{CaO}) = 5.000\text{ g} - 3.0666\text{ g} = 1.9334\text{ g} \approx 1.933\text{ g}
$$

  * Mass percentage of $\mathrm{CaO}$:
    

$$
\% \mathrm{CaO} = \frac{1.9334\text{ g}}{5.000\text{ g}} \times 100 = 38.67\%
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for mass subtraction, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for $\% \mathrm{CaO} = 38.67\%$).*

* **Step 4: Mass of Calcium Oxalate Monohydrate Recoverable**
  * Molar mass of $\mathrm{CaO} = 40.08 + 16.00 = 56.08\text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
  * Moles of $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ ions from $\mathrm{CaO}$:
    

$$
n(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}) = \frac{1.9334\text{ g}}{56.08\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.03448\text{ mol}
$$

  * Stoichiometry: $1\text{ mol }\mathrm{Ca}^{2+} \equiv 1\text{ mol }\mathrm{CaC_2O_4 \cdot H_2O}$.
  * Mass of precipitate:
    

$$
m(\mathrm{CaC_2O_4 \cdot H_2O}) = 0.03448\text{ mol} \times 146.11\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 5.038\text{ g}
$$

  
    *(**Marking Rubric:** $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for molar balance, $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark for final mass: $5.038\text{ g}$ or $5.04\text{ g}$).*

# Speed Hacks, Examiner Traps, Common Evaluation Mistakes & Golden Points to Remember

## 1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

### 1.1 The Ultimate Mole Map Shortcut
Never write multi-step unitary methods for basic conversions. Memorize the central junction:

$$
\text{Moles } (n) = \frac{\text{Mass } (m \text{ in g})}{M} = \frac{N}{N_A} = \frac{V_{\text{STP}} (\text{in L})}{22.7 \text{ L (or } 22.4 \text{ L)}} = M \times V_{\text{soln}} (\text{in L})
$$

* **Rapid Conversion Multipliers**:
  * Mass to particles in one line: $N = \left(\frac{m}{M}\right) \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}$
  * STP Litres to particles: $N = \left(\frac{V}{22.4}\right) \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \approx V \times 2.69 \times 10^{22}$ particles/L (at old STP $22.4\text{ L}$).

### 1.2 The "Number of Atoms in a Molecule" Formula
For a compound $A_x B_y$ with mass $m$ and molar mass $M$:

$$
\text{Total atoms of } A = \left(\frac{m}{M}\right) \times x \times N_A
$$

$$
\text{Total electrons} = \left(\frac{m}{M}\right) \times (\sum Z) \times N_A
$$

* **Examiner Hack**: If asked for total electrons in $1.6\text{ g}$ of $\text{CH}_4$:
  

$$
M = 16\text{ g/mol}, \quad \text{Electrons per molecule} = 6 + 4(1) = 10
$$

  

$$
\text{Total } e^- = \left(\frac{1.6}{16}\right) \times 10 \times N_A = 1.0 \times N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}
$$

  *(Do not calculate number of molecules first and write a 4-line paragraph; calculate it directly in one compound factor step).*

### 1.3 Rapid Limiting Reagent (L.R.) Identification (The Stoichiometric Ratio Test)
Given a balanced equation:

$$
aA + bB \longrightarrow cC + dD
$$

* Compute the **Mole-to-Coefficient Ratio**:
  

$$
\text{Ratio}_A = \frac{n_A}{a}, \quad \text{Ratio}_B = \frac{n_B}{b}
$$

* **Rule**: Whichever species has the **minimum ratio is strictly the Limiting Reagent**.
* **Shortcut for Product Formed**:
  

$$
n_{\text{product } C} = c \times \min\left(\frac{n_A}{a}, \frac{n_B}{b}\right)
$$

  *(Eliminates guessing or writing two trial cases).*

### 1.4 Direct Conversion Formulas for Concentration Terms
In board examinations, interconverting molarity, molality, mole fraction, and mass percent consumes 6–8 minutes if done from first principles. Use standard interconversion relations:

1. **Molarity ($M$) from Mass Percentage ($x\% \text{ w/w}$) and Solution Density ($d \text{ in g/mL}$)**:
   

$$
M = \frac{10 \times x \times d}{M_{\text{solute}}}
$$

2. **Molality ($m$) from Mass Percentage ($x\% \text{ w/w}$)**:
   

$$
m = \frac{1000 \times x}{(100 - x) \times M_{\text{solute}}}
$$

3. **Molality ($m$) from Molarity ($M$) and Density ($d \text{ in g/mL}$)**:
   

$$
m = \frac{1000 \times M}{1000 \times d - M \times M_{\text{solute}}}
$$

4. **Mole Fraction of Solute ($\chi_B$) from Molality ($m$) in Aqueous Solution**:
   

$$
\chi_B = \frac{m}{m + \frac{1000}{18}} = \frac{m}{m + 55.55}
$$

### 1.5 The Average Atomic Mass Pivot Technique
For an element with isotopes of mass numbers $A_1$ and $A_2$ having average atomic mass $\bar{A}$:

$$
\% \text{ abundance of } A_1 = \left|\frac{\bar{A} - A_2}{A_1 - A_2}\right| \times 100
$$

*(No need to set up two equations with $x$ and $100 - x$ and solve iteratively).*

## 2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

### 2.1 The Critical CBSE Mark-Loss Points
Every year in the CBSE Class 11 annual and board sample evaluation, thousands of students lose 0.5 to 1 full mark per question due to specific recurring errors:

| Concept | The Common Student Trap | The Precise Board Standard | Penalty |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Molar Volume at STP** | Using $22.4\text{ L}$ when modern NCERT/IUPAC standard ($T = 273.15\text{ K}, P = 1\text{ bar}$) is specified. | Standard at $1\text{ bar}$ is **$22.7\text{ L/mol}$**. At $1\text{ atm}$ it is **$22.4\text{ L/mol}$**. If pressure is not stated, specify: *"Assuming $1\text{ atm}$, $V_m = 22.4\text{ L}$"* or use $22.7\text{ L}$ as per revised NCERT. | $-\frac{1}{2}\text{ mark}$ |
| **Units of Concentration** | Writing numeric answer only, e.g., $\text{Molarity} = 0.25$. | Must write unit: $\text{mol L}^{-1}$, $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$, or $\text{M}$. Molality must be $\text{mol kg}^{-1}$ or $\text{m}$. | $-\frac{1}{2}\text{ mark}$ |

## 3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT High-Yield Repertoire)

1. **Matter Classification & Precision**:
   * A mixture having uniform composition throughout is **homogeneous** (e.g., air, brass, aqueous sugar solution).
   * Components of a compound **cannot** be separated by physical methods; they can only be separated by chemical or electrochemical methods.

2. **The 5 Governing Laws of Chemical Combinations**:
   * **Law of Conservation of Mass (Antoine Lavoisier, 1789)**: In all physical and chemical changes, total mass of reactants equals total mass of products. *(Limitation: Nuclear reactions where $E = \Delta m c^2$)*.
   * **Law of Definite Proportions / Composition (Joseph Proust, 1799)**: A given compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by weight, irrespective of the source. *(Fails for non-stoichiometric compounds and isotopes)*.
   * **Law of Multiple Proportions (John Dalton, 1803)**: If two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in the ratio of small whole numbers (e.g., $\text{CO}$ and $\text{CO}_2$; $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$).
   * **Gay-Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes (1808)**: Gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction in a simple ratio by volume, provided all gases are at the same temperature and pressure. *(This is essentially the law of definite proportions by volume for gases)*.
   * **Avogadro’s Law (1811)**: Equal volumes of all gases under identical conditions of temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules. *(Explains Gay-Lussac's law and justifies diatomic nature of gases like $\text{H}_2, \text{O}_2, \text{Cl}_2$)*.

3. **Atomic and Molecular Mass Nuances**:
   * **Standard Reference**: The atomic mass unit ($\text{amu}$ or unified mass $\text{u}$) is defined as exactly $\frac{1}{12}\text{th}$ of the mass of one carbon-12 atom:
     

$$
1\text{ u} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g} = \frac{1}{N_A}\text{ g}
$$

   * **Formula Mass vs. Molecular Mass**: Ionic compounds (like $\text{NaCl}, \text{KNO}_3$) do not contain discrete molecules in the solid state; they exist as 3D crystal lattices. Therefore, term **Formula Mass** is used instead of Molecular Mass.

4. **Rules for Significant Figures (NCERT Section 1.4.2)**:
   * All non-zero digits are significant.
   * Zeros preceding the first non-zero digit are non-significant ($0.0025 \rightarrow 2\text{ sig figs}$).
   * Zeros between two non-zero digits are significant ($2.005 \rightarrow 4\text{ sig figs}$).
   * Zeros at the end or right of a number are significant **only if they are on the right side of the decimal point** ($0.200 \rightarrow 3\text{ sig figs}$, but $100 \rightarrow 1\text{ sig fig}$; write as $1.00 \times 10^2$ for $3\text{ sig figs}$).
   * Exact numbers (counting numbers, e.g., $2$ balls or $20$ eggs) have an **infinite** number of significant figures ($2 = 2.000000...$).
