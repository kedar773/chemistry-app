### 1.1 The Meaning and Etymology of Stoichiometry
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
The word **Stoichiometry** originates from two Greek words:
- **Stoicheion** ($\sigma\tau o\iota\chi\epsilon\tilde{\iota}o\nu$): meaning *element*
- **Metron** ($\mu\acute{\epsilon}\tau\rho o\nu$): meaning *measure*

**Rigorous Definition:**  
> **Stoichiometry** is the quantitative study of the reactants consumed and products formed in a chemical reaction based on the conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions.

The numerical coefficients appearing before the chemical formulas in a balanced chemical equation are called **stoichiometric coefficients**. They represent the relative numbers of moles, molecules, or volumes (for gases at identical $T$ and $P$) participating in the transformation.

***

### 1.2 Fundamental Physical Laws Governing Chemical Equations
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Every balanced chemical reaction satisfies two fundamental conservation principles:

#### 1. Law of Conservation of Mass (Antoine Lavoisier, 1789)
> In any physical change or chemical reaction, total mass is neither created nor destroyed; the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.

$$
\sum m_{\text{reactants}} = \sum m_{\text{products}}
$$

*Molecular interpretation:* Atoms cannot be created or annihilated during ordinary chemical rearrangements; atomic nuclei remain unchanged, and only bonds (electronic configurations) are reorganized.

#### 2. Law of Definite Proportions / Composition (Joseph Proust, 1799)
> A given chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by weight, irrespective of its source or method of preparation.

#### 3. Gay-Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes (1808) & Avogadro’s Hypothesis (1811)
> When gases react, they do so in volumes that bear a simple whole-number ratio to one another and to the volumes of products (if gaseous), provided all measurements are carried out at identical temperature ($T$) and pressure ($P$).

Avogadro's interpretation states:

$$
V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } P)
$$

Hence, the ratio of stoichiometric coefficients of gaseous species directly equals their volume ratio.

### 1.3 Microscopic vs. Macroscopic Interpretations of a Chemical Equation
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Consider the complete combustion of methane:

$$
\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2\,O_2(g) \longrightarrow CO_2(g) + 2\,H_2O(g)}
$$

A balanced chemical equation can be interpreted on four distinct quantitative planes:

| Level of Interpretation | Reactant 1 ($\mathrm{CH_4}$) | Reactant 2 ($\mathrm{O_2}$) | Product 1 ($\mathrm{CO_2}$) | Product 2 ($\mathrm{H_2O}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Molecular level** | $1\text{ molecule}$ | $2\text{ molecules}$ | $1\text{ molecule}$ | $2\text{ molecules}$ |
| **2. Molar level** | $1\text{ mol}$ | $2\text{ mol}$ | $1\text{ mol}$ | $2\text{ mol}$ |
| **3. Mass level** | $16.04\text{ g}$ | $2 \times 32.00 = 64.00\text{ g}$ | $44.01\text{ g}$ | $2 \times 18.02 = 36.04\text{ g}$ |
| **4. Gaseous Volume ($T, P$)** | $1\text{ volume (or } V\text{ L)}$ | $2\text{ volumes (or } 2V\text{ L)}$ | $1\text{ volume (or } V\text{ L)}$ | $2\text{ volumes (or } 2V\text{ L)}$ |

**Verification of Law of Conservation of Mass:**

$$
\text{Mass of Reactants} = 16.04\text{ g} + 64.00\text{ g} = 80.04\text{ g}
$$

$$
\text{Mass of Products} = 44.01\text{ g} + 36.04\text{ g} = 80.05\text{ g} \approx 80.04\text{ g}
$$

> **NCERT Critical Note:** Moles are conserved **only** if the total stoichiometric coefficients happen to be identical on both sides, which is generally not required. Mass and atomic counts are **always strictly conserved**; total moles and total volumes are **not necessarily conserved**.

### 1.4 State Symbols and Thermodynamic/Reaction Notations
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐

Chemical formulas must be accompanied by physical state designators:
- $\mathrm{(s)}$: solid
- $\mathrm{(l)}$: pure liquid
- $\mathrm{(g)}$: gas or vapor
- $\mathrm{(aq)}$: aqueous solution (substance dissolved in excess water where water is the solvent)

Special reaction condition notations placed over or under the reaction arrow:
- $\Delta$: thermal energy applied (heating)
- $h\nu$: photochemical activation (ultraviolet/visible light irradiation)
- $\text{Cat.}$: catalyst present to lower activation energy without stoichiometric consumption
- $\mathrm{P, T}$: explicit operating pressure and temperature

***

### 1.5 The Limiting Reagent (Limiting Reactant) Concept
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In laboratory and industrial chemical syntheses, reactants are rarely mixed in exact stoichiometric proportions.

**Strict NCERT Definition:**
> The reactant which is present in the least stoichiometric amount and gets consumed first in a reaction is called the **Limiting Reagent (L.R.)**. It limits or restricts the maximum amount of product that can be formed.

- **Excess Reagent (E.R.):** The reactant(s) remaining unreacted after the complete consumption of the limiting reagent.
- **Fundamental Rule:** All theoretical yields of products and amounts of excess reactants consumed **must be calculated solely with reference to the Limiting Reagent**.

### 2.1 The Master Stoichiometric Equation (Extent of Reaction)
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Consider the generalized irreversible homogeneous chemical reaction:

$$
a\,\mathrm{A} + b\,\mathrm{B} \longrightarrow c\,\mathrm{C} + d\,\mathrm{D}
$$

Where:
- $\mathrm{A, B}$ = Reactants
- $\mathrm{C, D}$ = Products
- $a, b, c, d$ = Stoichiometric coefficients

Let $n_{\mathrm{A},0}, n_{\mathrm{B},0}$ be the initial moles present at $t = 0$.  
Let $\xi$ (Greek letter *xi*) be the **extent of reaction** (in moles).

At any progress stage or completion:

$$
\Delta n_i = \nu_i \cdot \xi
$$

Where $\nu_i$ is the stoichiometric coefficient with signs assigned by convention:
- $\nu_{\mathrm{reactants}} = -a, -b$ (consumed)
- $\nu_{\mathrm{products}} = +c, +d$ (formed)

Therefore:

$$
n_{\mathrm{A}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{A},0} - a\xi
$$

$$
n_{\mathrm{B}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{B},0} - b\xi
$$

$$
n_{\mathrm{C}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{C},0} + c\xi
$$

$$
n_{\mathrm{D}}(t) = n_{\mathrm{D},0} + d\xi
$$

Equating the extent of reaction $\xi$ across all participating species:

$$
\boxed{\xi = \frac{-\Delta n_{\mathrm{A}}}{a} = \frac{-\Delta n_{\mathrm{B}}}{b} = \frac{\Delta n_{\mathrm{C}}}{c} = \frac{\Delta n_{\mathrm{D}}}{d}}
$$

Or in terms of moles consumed ($n_{\text{consumed}}$) and produced ($n_{\text{produced}}$):

$$
\boxed{\frac{n_{\mathrm{A, consumed}}}{a} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{B, consumed}}}{b} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{C, produced}}}{c} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{D, produced}}}{d}}
$$

***

### 2.2 Mathematical Criterion for Identifying the Limiting Reagent
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

To determine which reactant is the limiting reagent when initial moles $n_{\mathrm{A},0}$ and $n_{\mathrm{B},0}$ are given:

1. Compute the **Stoichiometric Ratio Index ($\mathcal{R}$)** for every reactant:

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{A},0}}{a} = \frac{w_{\mathrm{A}} / M_{\mathrm{A}}}{a}
$$

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{B},0}}{b} = \frac{w_{\mathrm{B}} / M_{\mathrm{B}}}{b}
$$

Where:
- $w$ = mass in grams
- $M$ = molar mass in $\mathrm{g\cdot mol^{-1}}$

2. Compare the indices:
- If $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}} < \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}}$, then **$\mathrm{A}$ is the Limiting Reagent (L.R.)** and **$\mathrm{B}$ is the Excess Reagent (E.R.)**.
- If $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}} < \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}$, then **$\mathrm{B}$ is the Limiting Reagent (L.R.)** and **$\mathrm{A}$ is the Excess Reagent (E.R.)**.
- If $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}} = \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}}$, the reactants are in **stoichiometric proportions**; both are consumed completely simultaneously, leaving no excess reactant.

3. Quantities at reaction termination ($t \to \infty$ for $100\%$ yield):
Assuming $\mathrm{A}$ is the L.R. ($\xi_{\max} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{A},0}}{a}$):

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{A, final}} = 0}
$$

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{B, unreacted}} = n_{\mathrm{B},0} - \left(\frac{b}{a}\right)n_{\mathrm{A},0} = b \cdot (\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{B}} - \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}})}
$$

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{C, formed}} = \left(\frac{c}{a}\right)n_{\mathrm{A},0} = c \cdot \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}
$$

$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{D, formed}} = \left(\frac{d}{a}\right)n_{\mathrm{A},0} = d \cdot \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{A}}}
$$

### 2.3 Conversions Involving Gas Volumes: STP Standards
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Gas volumes must be converted to moles using the Ideal Gas Law:

$$
P \cdot V = n \cdot R \cdot T \implies n = \frac{P \cdot V}{R \cdot T}
$$

#### Critical Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP) Distinctions:

**1. Old IUPAC Convention (Pre-1982, still frequently used in Indian State Boards and older competitive exams):**
- Temperature: $T = 0\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 273.15\text{ K}$
- Pressure: $P = 1.0\text{ atm} = 101.325\text{ kPa} = 760\text{ torr}$

$$
V_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1\text{ mol} \times 0.082057\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \times 273.15\text{ K}}{1.0\text{ atm}} \approx \mathbf{22.414\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 22.4\text{ L}}
$$

**2. Modern IUPAC Standard (Adopted in latest NCERT editions):**
- Temperature: $T = 0\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 273.15\text{ K}$
- Pressure: $P = 1.0\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa} = 100\text{ kPa}$

$$
V_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1\text{ mol} \times 0.083145\text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \times 273.15\text{ K}}{1.0\text{ bar}} \approx \mathbf{22.711\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 22.7\text{ L}}
$$

> **Instruction for Students:** If the question explicitly states $P = 1\text{ bar}$, use $22.7\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$. If it states $1\text{ atm}$ or does not specify, $22.4\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ is commonly accepted, but clarify the assumption in board answers.

### 2.4 Percent Yield and Percent Purity Equations
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

In real chemical transformations, the experimental mass isolated is less than the theoretical mass due to competing side-reactions, incomplete equilibrium, mechanical losses during filtration, crystallization, or washing.

#### 1. Percentage Yield

$$
\boxed{\text{Percentage Yield (\%)} = \frac{\text{Actual Yield (Experimental)}}{\text{Theoretical Yield (Stoichiometric)}} \times 100\%}
$$

*Note:* Both actual and theoretical yields must be in identical units (both in grams, or both in moles).

#### 2. Percentage Purity of Impure Reactant Samples
When an impure sample of reactant $\mathrm{X}$ reacts:

$$
\boxed{\text{Percentage Purity (\%)} = \frac{\text{Pure mass of substance participating in reaction}}{\text{Total mass of impure sample taken}} \times 100\%}
$$

$$
\text{Pure mass of reactant} = \text{Total mass of sample} \times \left(\frac{\%\text{ Purity}}{100}\right)
$$

### Archetype 1: Classic Gravimetric Stoichiometry

#### Problem 1 (NCERT In-Text Adapted)
Calculate the amount of water ($\mathrm{g}$) produced by the complete combustion of $32.00\text{ g}$ of methane.

**Solution:**
- **Step 1: Write the balanced chemical equation:**

$$
\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2\,O_2(g) \longrightarrow CO_2(g) + 2\,H_2O(l)}
$$

- **Step 2: Calculate molar masses:**
  - $M(\mathrm{CH_4}) = 12.011 + 4(1.008) = 16.043\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 16.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - $M(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 2(1.008) + 15.999 = 18.015\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 18.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

- **Step 3: Convert mass of given substance to moles:**

$$
n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = \frac{w}{M} = \frac{32.00\text{ g}}{16.043\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 1.9946\text{ mol} \approx 2.00\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 4: Apply stoichiometric molar ratio:**
  From the balanced equation:
  

$$
1\text{ mol }\mathrm{CH_4} \implies 2\text{ mol }\mathrm{H_2O}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2O})_{\text{produced}} = 2 \times n(\mathrm{CH_4}) = 2 \times 1.9946\text{ mol} = 3.9892\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 5: Convert moles of product to mass in grams:**

$$
w(\mathrm{H_2O}) = n \times M = 3.9892\text{ mol} \times 18.015\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 71.865\text{ g}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Mass of }\mathrm{H_2O} = 72.0\text{ g}\quad (\text{to } 3\text{ significant figures})}
$$

### Archetype 2: Limiting Reagent in the Haber Process

#### Problem 2 (NCERT Exercise 1.24 / Exemplar Archetype)
$50.0\text{ kg}$ of $\mathrm{N_2(g)}$ and $10.0\text{ kg}$ of $\mathrm{H_2(g)}$ are mixed to produce $\mathrm{NH_3(g)}$.
1. Identify the limiting reagent.
2. Calculate the mass of $\mathrm{NH_3(g)}$ formed.
3. Determine the mass of the excess reagent remaining unreacted.

**Reaction Conditions:** $T = 700\text{ K}$, $P = 200\text{ atm}$, Catalyst: $\mathrm{Fe/K_2O/Al_2O_3}$.

$$
\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3\,H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\,NH_3(g)}
$$

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Convert all initial quantities to moles ($n = \frac{w\text{ in grams}}{M}$):**
  - Molar mass of $\mathrm{N_2} = 28.02\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - Molar mass of $\mathrm{H_2} = 2.016\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
  - Molar mass of $\mathrm{NH_3} = 17.03\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

$$
w(\mathrm{N_2}) = 50.0\text{ kg} = 50.0 \times 10^3\text{ g} = 5.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2})_0 = \frac{5.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}}{28.02\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 1784.44\text{ mol} = 1.784 \times 10^3\text{ mol}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{H_2}) = 10.0\text{ kg} = 10.0 \times 10^3\text{ g} = 1.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2})_0 = \frac{1.00 \times 10^4\text{ g}}{2.016\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 4960.32\text{ mol} = 4.960 \times 10^3\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 2: Determine the Limiting Reagent using the Ratio Index ($\mathcal{R} = \frac{n_0}{\text{coefficient}}$):**
  - For $\mathrm{N_2}$ (coefficient $a = 1$):
    

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \frac{1784.44}{1} = 1784.44\text{ mol}
$$

  - For $\mathrm{H_2}$ (coefficient $b = 3$):
    

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \frac{4960.32}{3} = 1653.44\text{ mol}
$$

$$
\because \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{H_2}} < \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{N_2}} \quad (1653.44 < 1784.44)
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{H_2} \text{ is the Limiting Reagent (L.R.)}; \quad \mathbf{N_2} \text{ is the Excess Reagent (E.R.)}}
$$

- **Step 3: Calculate the mass of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ produced:**
  Stoichiometric ratio relates $\mathrm{NH_3}$ directly to the L.R. ($\mathrm{H_2}$):

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{NH_3})_{\text{produced}}}{2} = \frac{n(\mathrm{H_2})_{\text{consumed}}}{3}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{NH_3})_{\text{produced}} = \frac{2}{3} \times n(\mathrm{H_2})_0 = \frac{2}{3} \times 4960.32\text{ mol} = 3306.88\text{ mol}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{NH_3}) = n \times M = 3306.88\text{ mol} \times 17.031\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 56319.47\text{ g} \approx 56.32\text{ kg}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Mass of }\mathrm{NH_3}\text{ formed} = 56.3\text{ kg}}
$$

- **Step 4: Calculate the mass of excess $\mathrm{N_2}$ remaining:**
  Moles of $\mathrm{N_2}$ consumed:

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{consumed}} = \frac{1}{3} \times n(\mathrm{H_2})_{\text{consumed}} = \frac{1}{3} \times 4960.32 = 1653.44\text{ mol}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{remaining}} = n(\mathrm{N_2})_0 - n(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{consumed}} = 1784.44 - 1653.44 = 131.00\text{ mol}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{N_2})_{\text{remaining}} = 131.00\text{ mol} \times 28.02\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 3670.62\text{ g} \approx 3.67\text{ kg}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Mass of excess }\mathrm{N_2}\text{ unreacted} = 3.67\text{ kg}}
$$

### Archetype 3: Gravimetric Analysis with Percent Purity

#### Problem 3 (NCERT Exemplar Advanced Variant)
A sample of impure limestone ($\mathrm{CaCO_3}$) weighing $25.0\text{ g}$ is treated with excess dilute hydrochloric acid ($\mathrm{HCl}$). The liberated carbon dioxide gas occupies $4.92\text{ L}$ measured at $300\text{ K}$ and $1.0\text{ atm}$ pressure. Assuming the impurities are completely inert to acid, calculate the percentage purity of the limestone sample.  
(Given: $R = 0.0821\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, atomic masses: $\mathrm{Ca}=40.0, \mathrm{C}=12.0, \mathrm{O}=16.0$).

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Write the balanced chemical reaction:**

$$
\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) + 2\,HCl(aq) \longrightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)\uparrow}
$$

- **Step 2: Calculate moles of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ liberated using the Ideal Gas Equation:**

$$
n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = \frac{P \cdot V}{R \cdot T} = \frac{1.0\text{ atm} \times 4.92\text{ L}}{0.0821\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \times 300\text{ K}}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = \frac{4.92}{24.63} = 0.19976\text{ mol} \approx 0.200\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 3: Relate moles of $\mathrm{CO_2}$ to moles of pure $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$:**
  From the stoichiometry of the equation:
  

$$
1\text{ mol }\mathrm{CaCO_3} \equiv 1\text{ mol }\mathrm{CO_2}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{CaCO_3})_{\text{pure}} = n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 0.200\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 4: Calculate mass of pure $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$:**
  

$$
M(\mathrm{CaCO_3}) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 100.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{CaCO_3})_{\text{pure}} = 0.200\text{ mol} \times 100.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 20.0\text{ g}
$$

- **Step 5: Compute percentage purity:**

$$
\%\text{ Purity} = \frac{w(\mathrm{CaCO_3})_{\text{pure}}}{w(\text{impure sample})} \times 100 = \frac{20.0\text{ g}}{25.0\text{ g}} \times 100
$$

$$
\boxed{\%\text{ Purity of Limestone} = 80.0\%}
$$

### Archetype 4: Multi-Step Sequential Stoichiometry

#### Problem 4 (JEE Main/Advanced & Chemistry Olympiad Archetype)
Potassium chlorate ($\mathrm{KClO_3}$) decomposes upon heating in the presence of $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ catalyst to produce oxygen gas. This generated oxygen is subsequently reacted with red phosphorus ($\mathrm{P_4}$) to form phosphorus pentoxide ($\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}$). Calculate the minimum mass of $\mathrm{KClO_3}$ required to completely oxidize $6.20\text{ g}$ of $\mathrm{P_4}$.  
(Atomic masses: $\mathrm{K} = 39.1, \mathrm{Cl} = 35.5, \mathrm{O} = 16.0, \mathrm{P} = 31.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Write both individual balanced reactions:**
  - Reaction 1 (Decomposition):
    

$$
2\,\mathrm{KClO_3(s)} \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\mathrm{MnO_2}} 2\,\mathrm{KCl(s)} + 3\,\mathrm{O_2(g)}
$$

  - Reaction 2 (Oxidation):
    

$$
\mathrm{P_4(s)} + 5\,\mathrm{O_2(g)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{P_4O_{10}(s)}
$$

- **Step 2: Method A - Coupling via the common intermediate ($\mathrm{O_2}$):**
  Multiply Reaction 1 by $5$ and Reaction 2 by $3$ to eliminate intermediate $\mathrm{O_2}$:
  

$$
10\,\mathrm{KClO_3} \longrightarrow 10\,\mathrm{KCl} + 15\,\mathrm{O_2}
$$

$$
3\,\mathrm{P_4} + 15\,\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow 3\,\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}
$$

  Summing the coupled net stoichiometric equation:

$$
10\,\mathrm{KClO_3} + 3\,\mathrm{P_4} \longrightarrow 10\,\mathrm{KCl} + 3\,\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}
$$

- **Step 3: Compute moles of $\mathrm{P_4}$:**
  

$$
M(\mathrm{P_4}) = 4 \times 30.974 = 123.90\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{P_4}) = \frac{6.20\text{ g}}{123.90\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 0.05004\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 4: Relate $\mathrm{KClO_3}$ to $\mathrm{P_4}$ using the coupled master ratio:**

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{KClO_3})}{10} = \frac{n(\mathrm{P_4})}{3}
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{KClO_3}) = \frac{10}{3} \times n(\mathrm{P_4}) = \frac{10}{3} \times 0.05004\text{ mol} = 0.1668\text{ mol}
$$

- **Step 5: Calculate mass of $\mathrm{KClO_3}$:**
  

$$
M(\mathrm{KClO_3}) = 39.10 + 35.45 + 3(16.00) = 122.55\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
w(\mathrm{KClO_3}) = 0.1668\text{ mol} \times 122.55\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 20.44\text{ g}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Required Mass of }\mathrm{KClO_3} = 20.4\text{ g}}
$$

### Archetype 5: Reactions in Aqueous Solution (Neutralization Titration)

#### Problem 5
What volume of $0.250\text{ M }\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ solution is required to neutralize completely $50.0\text{ mL}$ of $0.400\text{ M }\mathrm{NaOH}$ solution?

**Solution:**

- **Step 1: Balanced chemical equation:**

$$
\mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq) + 2\,NaOH(aq) \longrightarrow Na_2SO_4(aq) + 2\,H_2O(l)}
$$

- **Step 2: Stoichiometric mole relationship:**

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{H_2SO_4})}{1} = \frac{n(\mathrm{NaOH})}{2}
$$

Recall that $n = M \times V_{\text{litres}}$ or $\text{millimoles} = M \times V_{\mathrm{mL}}$:

$$
M_1 \cdot V_1 = \frac{M_2 \cdot V_2}{2}
$$

Where:
- $M_1 = 0.250\text{ M}$, $V_1 = ?$
- $M_2 = 0.400\text{ M}$, $V_2 = 50.0\text{ mL}$

- **Step 3: Solve for $V_1$:**

$$
0.250 \times V_1 = \frac{0.400 \times 50.0}{2} = \frac{20.0}{2} = 10.0\text{ mmol}
$$

$$
V_1 = \frac{10.0\text{ mmol}}{0.250\text{ mmol}\cdot\mathrm{mL}^{-1}} = 40.0\text{ mL}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Required Volume of }\mathrm{H_2SO_4} = 40.0\text{ mL}}
$$

***

# SECTION 4: EXAMINER TRAPS, PITFALLS & FREQUENT STUDENT ERRORS

***

### Trap 1: Comparing Given Masses Directly Instead of Moles
- **The Trap:** In the reaction $\mathrm{N_2 + 3\,H_2 \to 2\,NH_3}$, if given $5\text{ g of }\mathrm{N_2}$ and $5\text{ g of }\mathrm{H_2}$, students conclude that because the masses are equal, neither is limiting, or they assume the one with the larger stoichiometric coefficient ($\mathrm{H_2}$) is limiting.
- **The Truth:** Stoichiometry operates **exclusively in the currency of moles**, never in grams!
  

$$
n(\mathrm{N_2}) = \frac{5}{28} = 0.178\text{ mol} \implies \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \frac{0.178}{1} = 0.178
$$

  

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2}) = \frac{5}{2} = 2.50\text{ mol} \implies \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \frac{2.50}{3} = 0.833
$$

  Here, $\mathrm{N_2}$ is the Limiting Reagent, even though both had equal starting mass!

***

### Trap 2: Neglecting the Stoichiometric Coefficient when Calculating L.R.
- **The Trap:** Calculating moles of reactants and choosing the one with fewer moles as the Limiting Reagent.
- **The Truth:** Moles must be divided by their stoichiometric coefficients.
  Example: $2\text{ mol of A}$ and $3\text{ mol of B}$ reacting according to:
  

$$
\mathrm{A + 2\,B \longrightarrow C}
$$

  Here, $n(\mathrm{A}) = 2 < n(\mathrm{B}) = 3$. But:
  

$$
\frac{n(\mathrm{A})}{1} = 2.0; \quad \frac{n(\mathrm{B})}{2} = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5
$$

  $\mathrm{B}$ is the Limiting Reagent despite having a higher initial mole count.

***

### Trap 3: STP Volume Confusion ($22.4\text{ L}$ vs $22.7\text{ L}$)
- **The Trap:** Indiscriminately substituting $22.4\text{ L}$ for gaseous conversions when pressure is specified in bar.
- **The Truth:**
  - $1.0\text{ atm}$ at $273.15\text{ K} \implies V_m = \mathbf{22.414\text{ L}}$
  - $1.0\text{ bar}$ ($10^5\text{ Pa}$) at $273.15\text{ K} \implies V_m = \mathbf{22.711\text{ L}}$
  A $1.3\%$ error occurs if interchanged, resulting in lost marks in high-precision board/entrance scoring.

***

### Trap 4: Applying Gay-Lussac’s Volume Law to Liquids or Solids
- **The Trap:** If a question states $\mathrm{2\,H_2(g) + O_2(g) \to 2\,H_2O(l)}$, students frequently assert that $2\text{ volumes of }\mathrm{H_2} + 1\text{ volume of }\mathrm{O_2} \to 2\text{ volumes of water}$.
- **The Truth:** Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes applies **only to gases**! The product here is liquid water ($\mathrm{l}$), whose volume is determined by its density ($\approx 1.0\text{ g}\cdot\mathrm{mL}^{-1}$), not by molar gas volume ($22.4\text{ L}$).

***

### Trap 5: Forgetting to Subtract Consumed Amount for Excess Reactant
- **The Trap:** When asked for the "amount of excess reactant remaining", students often report the amount of excess reactant *consumed*.
- **The Truth:**
  

$$
\text{Amount Remaining} = \text{Initial Amount Present} - \text{Amount Consumed by L.R.}
$$

### 5.1 The Principle of Atom Conservation (POAC)
**Exam Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (The ultimate speed hack for competitive exams)

When reactions involve complex balancing, multi-step mechanisms, or unknown side pathways, balancing every equation is unnecessary if an element passes quantitatively from a reactant into a single specific product.

> **POAC Rule:** For an element $X$ conserved completely from reactant to product:
> 

$$
\text{Total moles of atom } X \text{ in reactants} = \text{Total moles of atom } X \text{ in products}
$$

$$
(\text{Atoms of } X \text{ per molecule of Reactant}) \times n_{\text{Reactant}} = (\text{Atoms of } X \text{ per molecule of Product}) \times n_{\text{Product}}
$$

#### Example Application of POAC:
All carbon in $26.4\text{ g of }\mathrm{CO_2}$ was obtained by burning a sample of an organic compound $\mathrm{C_xH_y}$. How many grams of carbon were present?
- Applying POAC on Carbon ($\mathrm{C}$):

$$
1 \times n(\mathrm{C}) = 1 \times n(\mathrm{CO_2})
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{C}) = \frac{26.4\text{ g}}{44.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 0.60\text{ mol}
$$

$$
\text{Mass of Carbon} = 0.60\text{ mol} \times 12.0\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{7.2\text{ g}}
$$

*(Solved in 15 seconds without writing or balancing any combustion equation!)*

### 5.2 The 3-Line ICE Matrix Table Method
For any reaction with limiting reagent problems, systematically construct the **ICE** table:
- **I** = Initial quantity ($\text{mol}$)
- **C** = Change in quantity ($\Delta n = \nu \cdot \xi$)
- **E** = Equilibrium / End quantity ($n_{\text{final}} = n_0 + \Delta n$)

#### General Matrix:

$$
\begin{array}{lcccc}
\text{Reaction:} & a\,\mathrm{A} & + & b\,\mathrm{B} & \longrightarrow & c\,\mathrm{C} \\
\hline
\mathbf{I}\text{ (Initial)} & n_{\mathrm{A},0} & & n_{\mathrm{B},0} & & 0 \\
\mathbf{C}\text{ (Change)} & -a\xi_{\max} & & -b\xi_{\max} & & +c\xi_{\max} \\
\mathbf{E}\text{ (End)} & n_{\mathrm{A},0} - a\xi_{\max} & & n_{\mathrm{B},0} - b\xi_{\max} & & c\xi_{\max} \\
\end{array}
$$

Where $\xi_{\max} = \min\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{A},0}}{a}, \frac{n_{\mathrm{B},0}}{b}\right)$.

### 5.3 Golden Mnemonics

```
                       ┌─────────────────────────┐
                       │   "M-S-M" Flowchart:    │
                       │  Mass → Moles → Mass    │
                       └────────────┬────────────┘
                                    │
                                    ▼
       Mass of Known (g) ───[ ÷ Molar Mass ]───► Moles of Known
                                                      │
                                                      ▼
                                            [ × Mole Ratio: ]
                                            [ Coeff(Unknown)]
                                            [ ───────────── ]
                                            [  Coeff(Known) ]
                                                      │
                                                      ▼
       Mass of Unknown (g) ◄──[ × Molar Mass ]─── Moles of Unknown
```

#### Memory Mnemonic for Limiting Reagent Steps: **"B-M-D-P"**
1. **B** — **Balance** the chemical equation first.
2. **M** — Convert all quantities to **Moles**.
3. **D** — **Divide** moles by stoichiometric coefficients (lowest quotient is L.R.).
4. **P** — **Proceed** with product calculations using that L.R. quotient only.
