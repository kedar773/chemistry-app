# Module 1: Nature & Classification of Matter

### 1.1 Fundamental Definition of Matter ⭐⭐⭐
Matter is defined as anything that possesses mass, occupies space (volume), and whose presence can be felt by one or more of our five senses.

At the macroscopic level, matter exists primarily in three physical states under ordinary conditions of temperature ($T$) and pressure ($P$):
1. **Solid:** Possesses a definite shape and a definite volume. Intermolecular forces of attraction are significantly stronger than thermal energy. Particles oscillate only about their mean equilibrium positions; constituent particles exhibit minimal translational freedom.
2. **Liquid:** Possesses a definite volume but no definite shape (takes the shape of the container). Intermolecular forces are intermediate—strong enough to hold particles in proximity, yet weak enough to permit translational fluid motion.
3. **Gas:** Possesses neither a definite volume nor a definite shape. Particles exhibit complete translational, rotational, and vibrational freedom. Intermolecular forces of attraction are negligible compared to thermal kinetic energy at ambient temperatures.

#### Interconversion of States of Matter
The physical state of a substance is governed by a balance between two opposing factors:
- **Intermolecular Attractive Forces:** Tend to keep the molecules together.
- **Thermal Energy:** Tend to keep the molecules apart by inducing kinetic motion.

$$
\text{Solid} \underset{\text{Cool}}{\overset{\text{Heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Liquid} \underset{\text{Cool}}{\overset{\text{Heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Gas}
$$

### 1.2 Macroscopic Classification of Matter ⭐⭐⭐⭐

At the macroscopic and chemical level, matter is classified into **Mixtures** and **Pure Substances**:

```
                                  MATTER
                                    │
         ┌──────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                     ▼
     Mixtures                                           Pure Substances
  (Variable Composition;                              (Definite Composition;
 Physical Separation Possible)                      Chemical Separation Only)
         │                                                     │
   ┌─────┴─────┐                                         ┌─────┴─────┐
   ▼           ▼                                         ▼           ▼
Homogeneous  Heterogeneous                           Elements    Compounds
 (Uniform)  (Non-uniform)                           (1 Atom type)(Fixed Ratio)
```

#### 1. Pure Substances
A sample of matter that has a definite (constant) chemical composition and distinct chemical/physical properties throughout.
- **Elements:** 
  - Pure substances consisting of only one type of atom or entity.
  - Cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by ordinary chemical reactions.
  - *Examples:* Monoatomic ($\text{He}, \text{Ne}, \text{Ar}$), Diatomic ($\text{H}_2, \text{N}_2, \text{O}_2$), Polyatomic ($\text{P}_4, \text{S}_8$).
- **Compounds:** 
  - Formed when two or more atoms of different elements combine chemically in a **fixed and definite ratio by mass**.
  - The constituent elements lose their individual chemical identities completely.
  - *Example:* Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$); Hydrogen is combustible and Oxygen is a supporter of combustion, yet their compound, water, acts as a fire extinguisher.
  - Constituents cannot be separated by physical methods (e.g., filtration, distillation, magnetic separation); they require chemical or electrochemical cleavage.

#### 2. Mixtures
Consist of two or more pure substances present in any arbitrary ratio, wherein each constituent retains its individual chemical identity.
- **Homogeneous Mixtures (Solutions):**
  - Uniform composition and properties throughout the entire bulk phase.
  - No boundary of separation is visible under optical microscopy.
  - *Examples:* Pure air, aqueous solution of glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6\text{ (aq)}$), brass ($\text{Cu}-\text{Zn}$ solid solution).
- **Heterogeneous Mixtures:**
  - Non-uniform composition; consists of two or more physically distinct phases separated by phase boundaries.
  - *Examples:* Suspension of sand in water, oil-water emulsions, smoke (solid aerosol).

#### Temperature Scales & Mathematical Interconversions:
Temperature is the measure of the thermal state or degree of hotness of a body.

$$
\boxed{T\text{ (in K)} = T\text{ (in }^\circ\text{C)} + 273.15}
$$

$$
\boxed{^\circ\text{F} = \frac{9}{5}\left(^\circ\text{C}\right) + 32}
$$

$$
\boxed{^\circ\text{C} = \frac{5}{9}\left(^\circ\text{F} - 32\right)}
$$

> **Derivation of the Cross-Over Point:**
> To find the exact temperature where the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales read identical numerical values:
> 

$$
\text{Let } ^\circ\text{F} = ^\circ\text{C} = x
$$

> 

$$
x = \frac{9}{5}x + 32 \implies x - \frac{9}{5}x = 32 \implies -\frac{4}{5}x = 32 \implies x = \frac{32 \times (-5)}{4} = -40
$$

> 

$$
\boxed{-40^\circ\text{C} = -40^\circ\text{F}}
$$

> **Critical Note on Negative Temperatures:**
> The Kelvin scale is thermodynamic and absolute. Since zero Kelvin ($0\text{ K}$, absolute zero) is the theoretical lower limit of temperature where classical molecular kinetic motion ceases:
> 

$$
\boxed{T\text{ (in K)} \ge 0 \quad (\text{Negative Kelvin temperatures do not exist in classical systems})}
$$

### 2.2 Scientific Notation ⭐⭐⭐
Any number is expressed in exponential notation:

$$
N \times 10^n
$$

Where:
- $N$ is a number (known as digit term) such that $1.000\dots \le N < 10$.
- $n$ is an integer exponent (positive, negative, or zero).

*Examples:*
- $0.0000456\text{ g} = 4.56 \times 10^{-5}\text{ g}$
- $602\,200\,000\,000\,000\,000\,000\,000 = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$

### 2.3 Precision versus Accuracy ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Accuracy:** The agreement of a particular value to the true or accepted standard value of the quantity measured.
  

$$
\text{Absolute Error} = |\text{Observed Mean} - \text{True Value}|
$$

- **Precision:** The closeness of various measurements for the identical quantity to one another (reproducibility/repeatability of the measurement technique).

```
   Low Precision, Low Accuracy       High Precision, Low Accuracy      High Precision, High Accuracy
             (Poor)                        (Systematic Error)                    (Ideal)
         ┌─────────────┐                    ┌─────────────┐                 ┌─────────────┐
         │  x          │                    │             │                 │             │
         │       x     │                    │    x x      │                 │     xxx     │
         │    x     x  │                    │    xxx      │                 │     xxx     │
         │             │                    │             │                 │             │
         └─────────────┘                    └─────────────┘                 └─────────────┘
         Target: Center                     Target: Center                  Target: Center
```

*Illustration:* 
Let the true mass of a standard titration sample be **$2.000\text{ g}$**.
- **Analyst A:** $1.95\text{ g}, 1.93\text{ g}$ $\implies$ Average = $1.940\text{ g}$ (Neither accurate nor precise).
- **Analyst B:** $1.940\text{ g}, 1.942\text{ g}$ $\implies$ Average = $1.941\text{ g}$ (High precision, poor accuracy $\rightarrow$ indicates determinate/systematic error).
- **Analyst C:** $2.001\text{ g}, 1.999\text{ g}$ $\implies$ Average = $2.000\text{ g}$ (High precision and high accuracy).

### 2.4 Significant Figures: Rigorous Rules & Operational Arithmetic ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The total number of digits in a reported measurement including the last digit whose value is uncertain is called the number of **Significant Figures**.

$$
\text{Measured Value} = \text{Certain Digits} + \text{One Doubtful (Estimated) Digit}
$$

#### The 6 Core Rules for Determining Significant Figures:
1. **All non-zero digits are significant.**
   - $285\text{ cm} \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
   - $0.25\text{ mL} \rightarrow 2\text{ significant figures}$
2. **Zeros between two non-zero digits are always significant.**
   - $2.005\text{ kg} \rightarrow 4\text{ significant figures}$
   - $50.008\text{ s} \rightarrow 5\text{ significant figures}$
3. **Leading zeros (zeros to the left of the first non-zero digit) are NEVER significant.** They serve only to locate the position of the decimal point.
   - $0.03\text{ g} \rightarrow 1\text{ significant figure}$
   - $0.0052\text{ m} \rightarrow 2\text{ significant figures}$
4. **Trailing zeros (zeros at the end or to the right of a number):**
   - If located **after the decimal point**, they are **significant**.
     - $0.200\text{ g} \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
     - $4.000\text{ mL} \rightarrow 4\text{ significant figures}$
   - If located in a number **without a decimal point**, trailing zeros are ambiguous and not considered significant unless specified by scientific notation or an explicit terminal decimal point.
     - $100 \rightarrow 1\text{ significant figure (ambiguous)}$
     - $100. \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures (terminal decimal indicates precision)}$
     - $1.00 \times 10^2 \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
5. **Counting numbers of exact entities have an INFINITE ($\infty$) number of significant figures**, as they represent exact integers with zero experimental uncertainty.
   - $2\text{ balls} = 2.000000\dots \rightarrow \infty\text{ significant figures}$
   - $20\text{ eggs} = 20.00000\dots \rightarrow \infty\text{ significant figures}$
   - $\pi = 3.14159265\dots \rightarrow \infty\text{ significant figures}$
   - Standard stoichiometry coefficients in balanced equations have infinite precision.
6. **Exponential terms in scientific notation do not influence the count of significant figures.**
   - In $N \times 10^n$, the number of significant figures is dictated entirely by $N$.
   - $4.01 \times 10^2 \rightarrow 3\text{ significant figures}$
   - $8.256 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 4\text{ significant figures}$

***

### 2.5 Rules for Mathematical Operations with Significant Figures

#### Addition and Subtraction
The final calculated result cannot have more digits to the right of the decimal point than any of the original numbers involved in the operation.

$$
\text{Precision limit} = \min(\text{decimal places of operands})
$$

> **Worked Example:**
> Evaluate the sum: $12.11\text{ (2 decimal places)} + 18.0\text{ (1 decimal place)} + 1.012\text{ (3 decimal places)}$
> 

$$
\begin{array}{r@{\quad}l}
> 12.11 & \\
> 18.0\phantom{0} & \leftarrow Limiting operand (1 decimal place) \\
> +\; 1.012 & \\
> \hline
> 31.122 & \xrightarrow{\text{Round to 1 decimal place}} \mathbf{31.1}
> \end{array}
$$

#### Multiplication and Division
The final result must be reported with no more significant figures than are present in the measurement with the **fewest significant figures**.

$$
\text{Precision limit} = \min(\text{total significant figures of operands})
$$

> **Worked Example:**
> Multiply $2.5\text{ (2 significant figures)}$ by $1.25\text{ (3 significant figures)}$:
> 

$$
2.5 \times 1.25 = 3.125
$$

> Limiting term has 2 significant figures. 
> Result rounded to 2 significant figures: $\mathbf{3.1}$.

***

### 2.6 Formal Rules for Rounding Off
1. If the leftmost insignificant digit to be dropped is **greater than 5**, the preceding digit is increased by 1:
   

$$
1.386 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 3 sig. fig.}} 1.39
$$

2. If the leftmost insignificant digit to be dropped is **less than 5**, the preceding digit is left unchanged:
   

$$
4.334 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 3 sig. fig.}} 4.33
$$

3. If the digit to be dropped is **exactly 5** (or $5$ followed exclusively by zeros):
   - **Condition A:** If the preceding digit is **even**, it is left unchanged.
     

$$
6.250 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 2 sig. fig.}} 6.2
$$

   - **Condition B:** If the preceding digit is **odd**, it is increased by 1 (to make it even).
     

$$
3.750 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 2 sig. fig.}} 3.8
$$

   - **Condition C:** If the 5 is followed by any non-zero digit (e.g., $51, 501$), round up unconditionally:
     

$$
6.251 \xrightarrow{\text{round to 2 sig. fig.}} 6.3
$$

### 2.7 Dimensional Analysis (Factor-Label or Unit-Conversion Method) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Every physical quantity is expressed as the product of a numerical value and a physical unit:

$$
\text{Physical Quantity} = \text{Numerical Value} \times \text{Unit}
$$

To convert a quantity from one unit system to another, multiply by an identity fraction termed the **Unit Conversion Factor**:

$$
\text{Conversion Factor} = \frac{\text{Target Unit}}{\text{Given Unit}} = 1
$$

#### Step-by-Step Multi-Stage Dimensional Conversions:

##### Problem A: Convert $3\text{ days}$ into seconds.
1. Identification of fundamental unit equivalences:
   

$$
1\text{ day} = 24\text{ h} \implies \frac{24\text{ h}}{1\text{ day}} = 1
$$

   

$$
1\text{ h} = 60\text{ min} \implies \frac{60\text{ min}}{1\text{ h}} = 1
$$

   

$$
1\text{ min} = 60\text{ s} \implies \frac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}} = 1
$$

2. Dimensional chain multiplication:
   

$$
t = 3\text{ days} \times \left(\frac{24\text{ h}}{1\text{ day}}\right) \times \left(\frac{60\text{ min}}{1\text{ h}}\right) \times \left(\frac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}}\right)
$$

   

$$
t = 3 \times 24 \times 60 \times 60\text{ s} = \mathbf{259\,200\text{ s}} = \mathbf{2.592 \times 10^5\text{ s}}
$$

##### Problem B: Convert density $\rho = 13.6\text{ g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$ into standard SI units ($\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$).
1. Equivalences:
   

$$
1\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ g} = 10^3\text{ g} \implies \left(\frac{1\text{ kg}}{10^3\text{ g}}\right) = 1
$$

   

$$
1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm} = 10^2\text{ cm} \implies \left(\frac{10^2\text{ cm}}{1\text{ m}}\right) = 1 \implies \left(\frac{10^2\text{ cm}}{1\text{ m}}\right)^3 = \frac{10^6\text{ cm}^3}{1\text{ m}^3} = 1
$$

2. Chain multiplication:
   

$$
\rho = 13.6\left(\frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}\right) \times \left(\frac{1\text{ kg}}{10^3\text{ g}}\right) \times \left(\frac{10^6\text{ cm}^3}{1\text{ m}^3}\right)
$$

   

$$
\rho = 13.6 \times \frac{10^6}{10^3}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3} = 13.6 \times 10^3\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3} = \mathbf{1.36 \times 10^4\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}}
$$

### 3.1 Law of Conservation of Mass ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Antoine Lavoisier (1789) — Father of Modern Chemistry.
- **Statement:** 
  > In all physical transformations and chemical reactions, the total mass of the products formed is equal to the total mass of the reactants consumed. Matter can neither be created nor destroyed.
- **Mathematical Form:**
  

$$
\boxed{\sum m_{\text{reactants consumed}} = \sum m_{\text{products formed}}}
$$

  

$$
\Delta m = m_{\text{final}} - m_{\text{initial}} = 0
$$

- **Experimental Verification (Landolt's Tube Experiment):**
  An H-shaped glass vessel (Landolt tube) containing solutions of $\text{AgNO}_3$ in one arm and $\text{NaCl}$ (or $\text{HCl}$) in the other arm was sealed and weighed accurately. The solutions were mixed by inversion to precipitate Silver Chloride:
  

$$
\text{AgNO}_{3\text{ (aq)}} + \text{NaCl}_{\text{(aq)}} \longrightarrow \text{AgCl}_{\text{(s)}} \downarrow (\text{curdy white}) + \text{NaNO}_{3\text{ (aq)}}
$$

  Re-weighing the sealed system showed no detectable change in mass within experimental limits.

- **Modern Nuclear Limitation (Einstein Mass-Energy Equivalence):**
  In nuclear fission and fusion reactions, mass is not conserved independently. Mass ($m$) and energy ($E$) are interconvertible:
  

$$
\Delta E = \Delta m \cdot c^2
$$

  Where:
  - $c = 2.9979 \times 10^8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ (speed of light in vacuum).
  - In conventional chemical reactions, $\Delta E \approx 10^2 - 10^3\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$. The corresponding loss of mass $\Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2} \approx 10^{-11}\text{ kg}$, which is completely imperceptible on analytical balances. Thus, Lavoisier’s law holds with extreme fidelity for all standard chemical processes.

### 3.2 Law of Definite Proportions (or Definite Composition) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Joseph Proust (1799).
- **Statement:** 
  > A given pure chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of its constituent elements by mass, irrespective of its source, method of preparation, or geographical origin.

- **Proust's Classic Experiment on Cupric Carbonate ($\text{CuCO}_3$):**
  Proust analyzed two distinct samples of cupric carbonate—one of natural mineral origin (malachite extract) and one synthesized in the laboratory via wet precipitation:

| Sample Type | $\% \text{ Cu}$ | $\% \text{ C}$ | $\% \text{ O}$ |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Natural Mineral Sample** | $51.35\%$ | $9.74\%$ | $38.91\%$ |
| **Synthetic Laboratory Sample** | $51.35\%$ | $9.74\%$ | $38.91\%$ |

  The exact matching of mass percentages verified that composition is fixed.

- **Modern Limitations & Failures:**
  1. **Existence of Isotopes:** 
     If a compound is prepared using different isotopes of an element, the mass ratio changes.
     - Carbon dioxide synthesized from $^{12}\text{C}$:
       

$$
\text{Mass ratio } (m_{\text{C}} : m_{\text{O}}) = 12 : 32 = 3 : 8 = 1 : 2.667
$$

     - Carbon dioxide synthesized from $^{14}\text{C}$:
       

$$
\text{Mass ratio } (m_{\text{C}} : m_{\text{O}}) = 14 : 32 = 7 : 16 = 1 : 2.286
$$

     The mass ratio is not invariant when isotopic abundance varies.
  2. **Non-Stoichiometric Compounds (Berthollides):**
     Transition metal oxides, sulfides, and hydrides often exhibit crystal lattice defects resulting in variable composition.
     - *Example:* Wüstite (Ferrous Oxide), represented stoichiometrically as $\text{FeO}$, actually exists as $\text{Fe}_{x}\text{O}$ where $x$ varies continuously from $0.93$ to $0.96$ due to missing $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ lattice sites compensated by $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ions:
       

$$
\text{Composition} = \text{Fe}_{0.93}\text{O} \text{ to } \text{Fe}_{0.96}\text{O}
$$

     These do not adhere to fixed, integer mass ratios.

***

### 3.3 Law of Multiple Proportions ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** John Dalton (1803).
- **Statement:** 
  > When two elements combine to form more than one chemical compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in the ratio of small whole numbers.

- **Mathematical Formulation:**
  Let element $A$ and element $B$ combine to form $k$ distinct compounds: $\text{Comp}_1, \text{Comp}_2, \dots, \text{Comp}_k$.
  Fix the mass of element $A$ at a constant quantity $m_{A0}$.
  If the corresponding combining masses of element $B$ are $m_{B1}, m_{B2}, \dots, m_{Bk}$, then:
  

$$
\boxed{m_{B1} : m_{B2} : \dots : m_{Bk} = a_1 : a_2 : \dots : a_k \quad (a_i \in \mathbb{Z}^+)}
$$

#### Textbook Examples with Tabular Proofs:

##### Example 1: Oxides of Nitrogen ($\text{N}$ and $\text{O}$)
Nitrogen forms five stable oxides: Nitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), Nitric oxide ($\text{NO}$ or $\text{N}_2\text{O}_2$), Dinitrogen trioxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$), Dinitrogen tetroxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$), and Dinitrogen pentoxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$).
Fixing the mass of Nitrogen at $28.00\text{ g}$:

| Compound | Formula | Mass of $\text{N}$ ($\text{g}$) | Mass of $\text{O}$ ($\text{g}$) | Mass of $\text{O}$ combining with fixed $14.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{N}$ ($\text{g}$) | Ratio of Oxygen Masses |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Nitrous oxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ | $28.0$ | $16.0$ | $8.0$ | **1** |
| Nitric oxide | $\text{NO}$ | $14.0$ | $16.0$ | $16.0$ | **2** |
| Dinitrogen trioxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$ | $28.0$ | $48.0$ | $24.0$ | **3** |
| Dinitrogen tetroxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$ | $28.0$ | $64.0$ | $32.0$ | **4** |
| Dinitrogen pentoxide | $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ | $28.0$ | $80.0$ | $40.0$ | **5** |

$$
\text{Ratio of Oxygen masses} = 8.0 : 16.0 : 24.0 : 32.0 : 40.0 = \mathbf{1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5}
$$

This is an integer ratio, verifying the law.

##### Example 2: Oxides of Carbon ($\text{CO}$ and $\text{CO}_2$)
- Carbon Monoxide ($\text{CO}$): $12\text{ g}$ Carbon combines with $16\text{ g}$ Oxygen.
- Carbon Dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$): $12\text{ g}$ Carbon combines with $32\text{ g}$ Oxygen.
- For a fixed mass of Carbon ($12\text{ g}$), the ratio of masses of Oxygen is:
  

$$
\frac{m_{\text{O in CO}_2}}{m_{\text{O in CO}}} = \frac{32\text{ g}}{16\text{ g}} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{1} = 2 : 1}
$$

### 3.4 Law of Reciprocal Proportions (Law of Equivalent Proportions) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Jeremias Richter (1792).
- **Statement:** 
  > The ratio of the masses of two elements $A$ and $B$ which combine separately with a fixed mass of a third element $C$ is either the same as, or a simple whole-number multiple of, the ratio of the masses in which $A$ and $B$ combine directly with each other.

- **Mathematical Formulation:**
  Let element $A$ combine with element $C$ to form compound $AC$, where mass ratio is:
  

$$
\left(\frac{m_A}{m_C}\right)_1
$$

  Let element $B$ combine with element $C$ to form compound $BC$, where mass ratio is:
  

$$
\left(\frac{m_B}{m_C}\right)_2
$$

  Normalize the mass of $C$ to a common value. The resulting ratio of masses of $A$ and $B$ is:
  

$$
R_1 = \frac{m_A}{m_B}
$$

  Now, let $A$ and $B$ combine directly to form compound $A_x B_y$, where their direct combining mass ratio is:
  

$$
R_2 = \left(\frac{m_A}{m_B}\right)_{\text{direct}}
$$

  Then, according to Richter's Law:
  

$$
\boxed{\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{p}{q} \quad (p, q \in \mathbb{Z}^+)}
$$

```
                                Element C
                              (e.g., Oxygen)
                                ╱        ╲
                               ╱          ╲
                              ╱            ╲
                             ╱              ╲
                    Compound AC            Compound BC
                    (e.g., SO2)            (e.g., H2O)
                           ╱                  ╲
                          ╱                    ╲
                         ▼                      ▼
                   Element A ════════════════ Element B
                  (e.g., Sulfur) Compound AB  (e.g., Hydrogen)
                                 (e.g., H2S)
```

#### Step-by-Step Proof via Triangle System ($\text{C} = \text{Oxygen}$, $\text{A} = \text{Sulfur}$, $\text{B} = \text{Hydrogen}$):
1. **Reaction 1 (A with C):** $\text{S}$ combines with $\text{O}$ to form $\text{SO}_2$.
   - In $\text{SO}_2$: $32\text{ g}$ of Sulfur combines with $32\text{ g}$ of Oxygen.
   - Ratio: $\frac{m_{\text{S}}}{m_{\text{O}}} = \frac{32}{32} = \frac{1}{1}$.
   - Mass of $\text{S}$ combining with $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{O} = \mathbf{1.0\text{ g}}$.

2. **Reaction 2 (B with C):** $\text{H}$ combines with $\text{O}$ to form $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
   - In $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $2\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen combines with $16\text{ g}$ of Oxygen.
   - Mass of $\text{H}$ combining with $1.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{O} = \frac{2}{16}\text{ g} = \frac{1}{8}\text{ g} = \mathbf{0.125\text{ g}}$.

3. **Ratio $R_1$ for a fixed mass ($1.0\text{ g}$) of Oxygen:**
   

$$
R_1 = \frac{m_{\text{S}}}{m_{\text{H}}} = \frac{1.0}{\frac{1}{8}} = \frac{8}{1} = 8 : 1
$$

4. **Reaction 3 (A with B directly):** $\text{S}$ combines with $\text{H}$ to form $\text{H}_2\text{S}$.
   - In $\text{H}_2\text{S}$: $32\text{ g}$ of Sulfur combines with $2\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen.
   - Direct Ratio $R_2$:
     

$$
R_2 = \left(\frac{m_{\text{S}}}{m_{\text{H}}}\right)_{\text{direct}} = \frac{32}{2} = \frac{16}{1} = 16 : 1
$$

5. **Comparison of $R_1$ and $R_2$:**
   

$$
\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{\frac{8}{1}}{\frac{16}{1}} = \frac{8}{16} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}
$$

   

$$
\mathbf{R_1 : R_2 = 1 : 2}
$$

   Since $1:2$ is a simple whole-number ratio, the Law of Reciprocal Proportions is validated.

### 3.5 Gay-Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1808).
- **Statement:** 
  > When gases react together, they always do so in volumes which bear a simple whole-number ratio to one another and to the volumes of the gaseous products, provided all measurements are carried out under identical conditions of temperature and pressure.

- **Governing Condition:** 
  Temperature ($T$) and Pressure ($P$) must remain strictly constant throughout the volumetric measurement:
  

$$
\boxed{V_1 : V_2 : V_3 : \dots = n_1 : n_2 : n_3 : \dots \quad (\text{at constant } T, P)}
$$

#### Solved Case Studies:

##### Case A: Synthesis of Gaseous Water

$$
\begin{array}{cccccc}
2\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & \text{O}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(g)}} & \quad (T > 373.15\text{ K}) \\
2\text{ volumes} & : & 1\text{ volume} & : & 2\text{ volumes} & \\
100\text{ mL} & & 50\text{ mL} & \longrightarrow & 100\text{ mL} &
\end{array}
$$

Volume ratio:

$$
\mathbf{V_{\text{H}_2} : V_{\text{O}_2} : V_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = 2 : 1 : 2}
$$

##### Case B: Synthesis of Gaseous Hydrogen Chloride

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & \text{Cl}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{HCl}_{\text{(g)}} \\
1\text{ volume} & : & 1\text{ volume} & : & 2\text{ volumes}
\end{array}
$$

Volume ratio:

$$
\mathbf{V_{\text{H}_2} : V_{\text{Cl}_2} : V_{\text{HCl}} = 1 : 1 : 2}
$$

##### Case C: Industrial Haber-Bosch Ammonia Synthesis

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\text{N}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & 3\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \xrightarrow[\text{Fe catalyst}]{\Delta, P} & 2\text{NH}_{3\text{ (g)}} \\
1\text{ volume} & : & 3\text{ volumes} & : & 2\text{ volumes}
\end{array}
$$

Volume ratio:

$$
\mathbf{V_{\text{N}_2} : V_{\text{H}_2} : V_{\text{NH}_3} = 1 : 3 : 2}
$$

### 3.6 Avogadro’s Law (Avogadro's Hypothesis) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Formulated by:** Amedeo Avogadro (1811).
- **Statement:** 
  > Equal volumes of all gases under identical conditions of temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules.

- **Mathematical Expression:**
  

$$
\boxed{V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } P)}
$$

  

$$
\frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2}
$$

  Where $n$ represents the amount of substance (moles), and the total number of individual molecules $N = n \cdot N_{\text{A}}$.

#### Crucial Historical Distinction: Berzelius Hypothesis vs. Avogadro’s Law
- **Berzelius Hypothesis (1811):** Proposed that equal volumes of all gases contain an equal number of *atoms* at the same temperature and pressure.
  - Applying this to the formation of $\text{HCl}$ gas:
    

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
    \text{Hydrogen gas} & + & \text{Chlorine gas} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{ Compound gas (HCl)} \\
    1\text{ volume} & & 1\text{ volume} & & 2\text{ volumes} \\
    n\text{ atoms} & & n\text{ atoms} & & 2n\text{ compound atoms} \\
    1\text{ atom} & & 1\text{ atom} & & 2\text{ compound atoms} \\
    \frac{1}{2}\text{ atom} & + & \frac{1}{2}\text{ atom} & \longrightarrow & 1\text{ compound atom of HCl}
    \end{array}
$$

  - *The Fatal Flaw:* This required Dalton's fundamental indivisible atom to split into halves ($\frac{1}{2}\text{ atom}$), violating the foundational postulate of Dalton's atomic theory. Consequently, Berzelius's hypothesis was rejected.
- **Avogadro’s Resolution:** 
  Avogadro introduced the clear distinction between an **atom** (the smallest particle of an element that participates in a chemical reaction, which may or may not have independent existence) and a **molecule** (the smallest particle of an element or compound capable of independent existence).
  

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
  \text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} & + & \text{Cl}_{2\text{ (g)}} & \longrightarrow & 2\text{HCl}_{\text{(g)}} \\
  1\text{ molecule} & & 1\text{ molecule} & & 2\text{ molecules} \\
  \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule} & + & \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule} & \longrightarrow & 1\text{ molecule of HCl}
  \end{array}
$$

  Since a molecule of hydrogen can be diatomic ($\text{H}_2$), $\frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule} = 1\text{ atom of H}$. This resolved the paradox without splitting any atoms.

***

### 3.7 Mathematical Derivations Based on Avogadro's Hypothesis

#### Derivation 1: Deduction of the Diatomic Nature of Elementary Gases ($\text{H}_2, \text{O}_2, \text{Cl}_2$)
Consider the volumetric combination of hydrogen and oxygen producing water vapour:

$$
2\text{H}_{2\text{ (g)}} + \text{O}_{2\text{ (g)}} \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(g)}} \quad (\text{all at constant } T, P)
$$

By experimental gas analysis:

$$
2\text{ volumes of hydrogen} + 1\text{ volume of oxygen} \longrightarrow 2\text{ volumes of water vapour}
$$

Applying Avogadro's Law ($V \propto N$):

$$
2N\text{ molecules of hydrogen} + N\text{ molecules of oxygen} \longrightarrow 2N\text{ molecules of water vapour}
$$

Divide by $2N$:

$$
1\text{ molecule of hydrogen} + \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule of oxygen} \longrightarrow 1\text{ molecule of water vapour}
$$

Experimental analysis shows that 1 molecule of water contains at least 1 atom of oxygen.

$$
\text{Therefore: } \frac{1}{2}\text{ molecule of oxygen} \ge 1\text{ atom of oxygen}
$$

$$
\implies \mathbf{1\text{ molecule of oxygen}} \ge \mathbf{2\text{ atoms of oxygen}}
$$

Thus, elementary oxygen cannot be monoatomic; it must be at least diatomic ($\text{O}_2$).

***

#### Derivation 2: Relationship Between Vapour Density (V.D.) and Molar Mass ($M$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

##### Definition:
**Vapour Density (V.D.)** is defined as the ratio of the mass of a certain volume of a gas or vapour to the mass of the same volume of hydrogen gas measured under identical conditions of temperature and pressure.

##### Step-by-Step Derivation:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } V \text{ volume of gas at temperature } T \text{ and pressure } P}{\text{Mass of } V \text{ volume of } \text{H}_2\text{ gas at same } T \text{ and } P}
$$

According to Avogadro’s Law, under identical conditions of $T$ and $P$, $V$ volume contains $N$ molecules:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of } \text{H}_2\text{ gas}}
$$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $N$:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of } \text{H}_2\text{ gas}}
$$

Since Hydrogen is a diatomic gas ($\text{H}_2$ molecule contains $2$ atoms of $\text{H}$):

$$
\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of } \text{H}_2 = 2 \times \text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}
$$

Substitute this relation into the expression:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{2 \times \text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}}
$$

Rewrite the equation:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{1}{2} \times \left( \frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}} \right)
$$

By definition, the term $\frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of the gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ atom of } \text{H}}$ is the **Relative Molecular Mass ($M$)** of the gas:

$$
\text{V.D.} = \frac{1}{2} \times M
$$

Rearranging for Relative Molar Mass ($M$):

$$
\boxed{M = 2 \times \text{V.D.}}
$$

***

#### Derivation 3: Molar Volume of an Ideal Gas at STP ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The Ideal Gas Equation is:

$$
P \cdot V = n \cdot R \cdot T
$$

Where:
- $n = 1\text{ mol}$
- $R = 8.314\text{ J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 0.082057\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} = 0.08314\text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$

##### Case 1: Old IUPAC Definition of STP
- $T = 273.15\text{ K}\ (0^\circ\text{C})$
- $P = 1.0\text{ atm} = 101\,325\text{ Pa}$

$$
V_{\text{m}} = \frac{n R T}{P} = \frac{(1\text{ mol}) \times (0.082057\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (273.15\text{ K})}{1.0\text{ atm}} = \mathbf{22.414\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

$$
\boxed{V_{\text{m (old STP)}} \approx 22.4\text{ L} = 22\,400\text{ mL}}
$$

##### Case 2: Modern IUPAC Standard (since 1982)
- $T = 273.15\text{ K}\ (0^\circ\text{C})$
- $P = 1.0\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa}$

$$
V_{\text{m}} = \frac{n R T}{P} = \frac{(1\text{ mol}) \times (0.08314\text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (273.15\text{ K})}{1.0\text{ bar}} = \mathbf{22.711\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}}
$$

$$
\boxed{V_{\text{m (modern STP)}} \approx 22.7\text{ L} = 22\,700\text{ mL}}
$$

# Module 4: Dalton’s Atomic Theory

### 4.1 Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808) ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Published in *A New System of Chemical Philosophy*, Dalton proposed:
1. Matter consists of indivisible, indestructible particles called **atoms** (derived from Greek *atomos*, meaning uncuttable).
2. All atoms of a given element are **identical in every respect**—they possess identical mass, size, and chemical properties.
3. Atoms of different elements possess **different masses, sizes, and chemical behaviors**.
4. Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in **fixed, simple whole-number numerical ratios** (e.g., $1:1, 1:2, 2:3$).
5. Chemical reactions involve only the **reorganization, combination, or separation of atoms**. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

***

### 4.2 Explanatory Success of Dalton’s Postulates

| Law Explained | Operating Dalton Postulate |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Conservation of Mass** | Chemical reactions involve only rearrangement; atoms cannot be created or destroyed (Postulate 5). |
| **Definite Proportions** | A compound consists of atoms of different elements combined in fixed numerical ratios, with constant atomic masses (Postulates 2 and 4). |
| **Multiple Proportions** | Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios ($1:1, 1:2$, etc.). If element $A$ is fixed, varying whole numbers of atom $B$ combine with it (Postulate 4). |

***

### 4.3 Failures & Critical Limitations of Dalton's Atomic Theory ⭐⭐⭐⭐
1. **Subatomic Particles:** The discovery of electrons (J.J. Thomson), protons (Goldstein/Rutherford), and neutrons (Chadwick) invalidated the postulate that the atom is indivisible.
2. **Discovery of Isotopes:** Postulate 2 asserts that all atoms of an element have identical masses. Isotopes ($^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $^{37}\text{Cl}$; $^{1}\text{H}, ^{2}\text{H}, ^{3}\text{H}$) possess identical atomic numbers but different masses, disproving this postulate.
3. **Discovery of Isobars:** Postulate 3 asserts that atoms of different elements must have different masses. Isobars are atoms of different elements with identical mass numbers ($^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$ and $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$), disproving this postulate.
4. **Complex Organic Molecules:** In macromolecules and complex organic polymers (e.g., sucrose $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$, starch $(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{10}\text{O}_5)_n$, proteins), the ratio of atoms is not simple whole numbers like $1:1$ or $1:2$, but rather large structural networks.
5. **Inability to Explain Gay-Lussac’s Law:** Dalton could not account for why gases combine in simple integer ratios by *volume*. He rejected Avogadro’s premise because he refused to believe that elementary gaseous particles could exist as diatomic molecules ($\text{H}_2, \text{O}_2$).
6. **Allotropy:** Dalton's theory failed to explain why substances composed of the exact same atoms (e.g., diamond and graphite, both composed purely of carbon) exhibit vastly different physical and chemical properties.

### Problem 1: Law of Definite Proportions
**Question:** In an experiment, $2.16\text{ g}$ of copper metal was treated with concentrated nitric acid. The resulting copper nitrate was ignited to yield $2.70\text{ g}$ of copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$). In a second experiment, $1.15\text{ g}$ of copper oxide was completely reduced under a stream of pure hydrogen gas to yield $0.92\text{ g}$ of metallic copper. Demonstrate that these experimental findings validate the Law of Definite Proportions.

**Solution:**
- **Experiment 1:**
  - Mass of metallic copper ($m_{\text{Cu}}$) = $2.16\text{ g}$
  - Mass of copper oxide formed ($m_{\text{oxide}}$) = $2.70\text{ g}$
  - Mass of oxygen combined ($m_{\text{O}}$) = $2.70\text{ g} - 2.16\text{ g} = 0.54\text{ g}$
  

$$
\% \text{ of Cu in Experiment 1} = \left(\frac{2.16\text{ g}}{2.70\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{80.0\%}
$$

  

$$
\% \text{ of O in Experiment 1} = \left(\frac{0.54\text{ g}}{2.70\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{20.0\%}
$$

- **Experiment 2:**
  - Mass of copper oxide taken ($m_{\text{oxide}}$) = $1.15\text{ g}$
  - Mass of metallic copper recovered ($m_{\text{Cu}}$) = $0.92\text{ g}$
  - Mass of oxygen lost ($m_{\text{O}}$) = $1.15\text{ g} - 0.92\text{ g} = 0.23\text{ g}$
  

$$
\% \text{ of Cu in Experiment 2} = \left(\frac{0.92\text{ g}}{1.15\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{80.0\%}
$$

  

$$
\% \text{ of O in Experiment 2} = \left(\frac{0.23\text{ g}}{1.15\text{ g}}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{20.0\%}
$$

- **Conclusion:**
  

$$
\% \text{ Cu} : \% \text{ O} = 80.0\% : 20.0\% = 4 : 1 \quad (\text{Identical in both experiments})
$$

  Because the mass composition of copper oxide is constant irrespective of the chemical pathway, the **Law of Definite Proportions** is experimentally validated.

### Problem 2: Law of Multiple Proportions (NCERT Exemplar Archetype)
**Question:** Carbon and oxygen form two distinct gaseous compounds, designated $X$ and $Y$. Quantitative micro-analysis shows:
- Compound $X$ contains $42.9\%$ carbon and $57.1\%$ oxygen.
- Compound $Y$ contains $27.3\%$ carbon and $72.7\%$ oxygen.
Show that these data illustrate the Law of Multiple Proportions.

**Solution:**
1. **Analyze Compound $X$:**
   In $100\text{ g}$ of compound $X$:
   - Mass of Carbon = $42.9\text{ g}$
   - Mass of Oxygen = $57.1\text{ g}$
   

$$
\text{Mass of Oxygen combining with } 1.00\text{ g of Carbon} = \frac{57.1\text{ g}}{42.9\text{ g}} = \mathbf{1.33\text{ g}}
$$

2. **Analyze Compound $Y$:**
   In $100\text{ g}$ of compound $Y$:
   - Mass of Carbon = $27.3\text{ g}$
   - Mass of Oxygen = $72.7\text{ g}$
   

$$
\text{Mass of Oxygen combining with } 1.00\text{ g of Carbon} = \frac{72.7\text{ g}}{27.3\text{ g}} = \mathbf{2.66\text{ g}}
$$

3. **Compare Oxygen Masses for a Fixed Mass ($1.00\text{ g}$) of Carbon:**
   

$$
\text{Ratio} = \frac{m_{\text{O in } Y}}{m_{\text{O in } X}} = \frac{2.66\text{ g}}{1.33\text{ g}} = \frac{2}{1} = \mathbf{2 : 1}
$$

- **Conclusion:**
  The ratio of masses of oxygen combining with a fixed mass of carbon is $1 : 2$, an integer ratio. Thus, the **Law of Multiple Proportions** is confirmed. *(Compound $X = \text{CO}$; Compound $Y = \text{CO}_2$)*.

### Problem 3: Law of Reciprocal Proportions
**Question:** Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) contains $82.35\%$ nitrogen and $17.65\%$ hydrogen. Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) contains $88.90\%$ oxygen and $11.10\%$ hydrogen. Nitrogen trioxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$) contains $63.15\%$ oxygen and $36.85\%$ nitrogen. Prove that these quantitative values satisfy the Law of Reciprocal Proportions.

**Solution:**
- **Step 1: In $\text{NH}_3$ (Element $A = \text{N}$, Element $C = \text{H}$):**
  $17.65\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen combines with $82.35\text{ g}$ of Nitrogen.
  

$$
\text{Mass of Nitrogen combining with } 1.0\text{ g of Hydrogen} = \frac{82.35}{17.65} = \mathbf{4.666\text{ g}}
$$

- **Step 2: In $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Element $B = \text{O}$, Element $C = \text{H}$):**
  $11.10\text{ g}$ of Hydrogen combines with $88.90\text{ g}$ of Oxygen.
  

$$
\text{Mass of Oxygen combining with } 1.0\text{ g of Hydrogen} = \frac{88.90}{11.10} = \mathbf{8.009\text{ g}}
$$

- **Step 3: Calculated Ratio ($R_1$) between $\text{N}$ and $\text{O}$ with fixed mass ($1.0\text{ g}$) of $\text{H}$:**
  

$$
R_1 = \frac{m_{\text{N}}}{m_{\text{O}}} = \frac{4.666}{8.009} = \mathbf{0.5826}
$$

- **Step 4: Direct Combination in $\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$:**
  Mass of Nitrogen = $36.85\text{ g}$; Mass of Oxygen = $63.15\text{ g}$.
  

$$
R_2 = \left(\frac{m_{\text{N}}}{m_{\text{O}}}\right)_{\text{direct}} = \frac{36.85}{63.15} = \mathbf{0.5835}
$$

- **Step 5: Determine the Ratio of $R_1$ to $R_2$:**
  

$$
\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{0.5826}{0.5835} \approx \mathbf{1.00} = \mathbf{1 : 1}
$$

- **Conclusion:**
  Since the ratio $\frac{R_1}{R_2} = 1:1$, a simple integer ratio, the **Law of Reciprocal Proportions** is satisfied.

### Problem 4: Gay-Lussac’s Law with Excess Reactant
**Question:** $500\text{ mL}$ of gaseous propane ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$) is completely combusted in $3000\text{ mL}$ of pure oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$) at $100^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\text{ atm}$. Calculate the total volume and composition of the resulting gas mixture cooled to $25^\circ\text{C}$ at $1\text{ atm}$.

**Solution:**
1. **Balanced Chemical Equation:**
   

$$
\text{C}_3\text{H}_{8\text{ (g)}} + 5\text{O}_{2\text{ (g)}} \longrightarrow 3\text{CO}_{2\text{ (g)}} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}}
$$

   *(Note: Water is in the liquid state at $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\text{ atm}$; its volume is negligible compared to gases).*

2. **Stoichiometric Volume Relationships (Gay-Lussac's Law):**
   

$$
1\text{ volume of } \text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{ volumes of } \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 3\text{ volumes of } \text{CO}_2
$$

3. **Identify the Limiting Reactant:**
   - Volume of $\text{O}_2$ required for $500\text{ mL}$ of $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 = 500\text{ mL} \times 5 = 2500\text{ mL}$.
   - Available volume of $\text{O}_2 = 3000\text{ mL}$.
   - Since $2500\text{ mL} < 3000\text{ mL}$, **$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$ is the Limiting Reactant** and $\text{O}_2$ is in excess.

4. **Quantities After Reaction:**
   - $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$ consumed = $500\text{ mL} \implies \text{Residual } \text{C}_3\text{H}_8 = \mathbf{0\text{ mL}}$
   - $\text{O}_2$ consumed = $2500\text{ mL} \implies \text{Residual } \text{O}_2 = 3000\text{ mL} - 2500\text{ mL} = \mathbf{500\text{ mL}}$
   - $\text{CO}_2$ produced = $500\text{ mL} \times 3 = \mathbf{1500\text{ mL}}$
   - $\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}}$ volume $\approx 0\text{ mL (gas phase)}$

5. **Total Final Gaseous Volume:**
   

$$
V_{\text{final}} = V_{\text{residual } \text{O}_2} + V_{\text{formed } \text{CO}_2} = 500\text{ mL} + 1500\text{ mL} = \mathbf{2000\text{ mL}}
$$

### Problem 5: Significant Figures in Arithmetic

$$
\text{Ans} = \frac{0.02856 \times 298.15 \times 0.112}{0.5785}

   - $0.02856 \implies 4\text{ significant figures}$
   - $298.15 \implies 5\text{ significant figures}$
   - $0.112 \implies 3\text{ significant figures}$ $\leftarrow$ **Limiting operand**
   - $0.5785 \implies 4\text{ significant figures}$

$$
\text{Numerator} = 0.02856 \times 298.15 \times 0.112 = 0.953696544
$$

   

$$
\text{Ans} = \frac{0.953696544}{0.5785} = 1.64856792\dots
$$

3. Round to the limiting precision ($3\text{ significant figures}$):
   

$$
\mathbf{\text{Final Answer} = 1.65}

1. **Kelvin Notation Errors:**
   - *Error:* Writing temperatures as $273^\circ\text{K}$.
   - *Correction:* The Kelvin scale is thermodynamic; it does not take a degree symbol ($^\circ$). Write **$273\text{ K}$**, never $273^\circ\text{K}$. The degree symbol is reserved for $^\circ\text{C}$, $^\circ\text{F}$, and $^\circ\text{R}$.

2. **Liquid Phase in Gay-Lussac Calculations:**
   - *Error:* Including water volume when hydrocarbons combust at room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$).
   - *Correction:* Gay-Lussac’s Law applies strictly to **gaseous volumes**. At $25^\circ\text{C}$, water condenses to liquid form ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_{\text{(l)}}$). Its molar volume in the liquid phase is only $\approx 18\text{ mL}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, which is negligible compared to gas molar volume ($22\,400\text{ mL}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$). Always verify the state symbols at the specified reaction temperature!

3. **Molar Volume: $22.4\text{ L}$ versus $22.7\text{ L}$:**
   - *Error:* Using $22.4\text{ L}$ when conditions are specified at $1\text{ bar}$.
   - *Correction:* 
     - At $T = 273.15\text{ K}$ and $P = 1\text{ atm}$, $V_{\text{m}} = 22.414\text{ L}$.
     - At $T = 273.15\text{ K}$ and $P = 1\text{ bar}$, $V_{\text{m}} = 22.711\text{ L}$.
     - If an exam question specifies **STP with $1\text{ bar}$**, use **$22.7\text{ L}$**. If it does not specify pressure, clarify the standard used.

4. **Law of Multiple Proportions vs. Reciprocal Proportions:**
   - *Error:* Confusing the two laws when two elements form multiple compounds.
   - *Rule of Thumb:*
     - **Multiple Proportions:** Involves **two** elements only (e.g., $\text{CO}$ and $\text{CO}_2$; $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{SO}_3$).
     - **Reciprocal Proportions:** Involves **three** elements forming three distinct binary compounds in a triangular relationship (e.g., $\text{C}, \text{H}, \text{O}$ forming $\text{CH}_4, \text{CO}_2, \text{H}_2\text{O}$).

5. **Rounding at Intermediate Steps:**
   - *Error:* Rounding off intermediate numbers during multi-step calculations causes compounding errors in the final digit.
   - *Correction:* Retain at least two extra guard digits throughout intermediate arithmetic steps. Apply significant figure rounding **only once**, at the final reported answer.

# Module 7: Speed Hacks, Mental Math & Golden Points

### 7.1 Golden Rules for Rapid Problem-Solving
1. **The Vapour Density Anchor:**
   

$$
\boxed{\text{Molar Mass} = 2 \times \text{V.D.}}
$$

   *Instant Check:* If an unknown gas has a Vapour Density of $16$, its molar mass is $32\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ ($\text{O}_2$ or $\text{N}_2\text{H}_4$). If $\text{V.D.} = 14$, $M = 28\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ ($\text{N}_2$ or $\text{CO}$).

2. **Density of Air Conversion:**
   Dry air has an effective average molar mass of $M_{\text{air}} \approx 28.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.
   

$$
\text{V.D. of dry air} \approx \frac{28.96}{2} \approx 14.48
$$

   - Any gas with $M < 28.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (e.g., $\text{CH}_4, \text{NH}_3$) is lighter than air and collected via **downward displacement of air**.
   - Any gas with $M > 28.96\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ (e.g., $\text{CO}_2, \text{SO}_2, \text{Cl}_2$) is heavier than air and collected via **upward displacement of air**.

3. **Rounding Rules Rapid-Selector:**
   ```
   Is the digit to be dropped equal to 5?
   ├── If followed by non-zero digits (e.g., 51, 52) ──► ALWAYS ROUND UP (+1)
   └── If followed by zeros only (or terminates)
       ├── Preceding digit is ODD  ─────────────────────► ROUND UP (+1 to make even)
       └── Preceding digit is EVEN ─────────────────────► LEAVE UNCHANGED (keep even)
   ```

4. **Gas Volumetric Shortcuts at STP:**
   

$$
\text{Moles of gas } (n) = \frac{\text{Volume in mL at old STP}}{22\,400\text{ mL}} = \frac{\text{Volume in L at old STP}}{22.4\text{ L}}
$$

   

$$
1\text{ millimole of any ideal gas at old STP} = 22.4\text{ mL}
$$

   

$$
1\text{ micromole of any ideal gas at old STP} = 22.4\text{ }\mu\text{L}
$$

5. **Temperature Inversion Trick:**
   For fast mental sanity checks:
   - $0^\circ\text{C} = 32^\circ\text{F}$
   - $10^\circ\text{C} = 50^\circ\text{F}$
   - $20^\circ\text{C} = 68^\circ\text{F}$
   - $30^\circ\text{C} = 86^\circ\text{F}$
   - $100^\circ\text{C} = 212^\circ\text{F}$
   

$$
\Delta(1.0^\circ\text{C}) = \Delta(1.8^\circ\text{F})
$$

   *Every $5^\circ\text{C}$ increase corresponds to an exact $9^\circ\text{F}$ increase.*
