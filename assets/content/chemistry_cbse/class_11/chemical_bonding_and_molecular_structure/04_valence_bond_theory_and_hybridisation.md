## 1. Valence Bond Theory (VBT) & Orbital Overlap Concept

### 1.1 Historical Context & Fundamental Postulates ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Valence Bond Theory was introduced by **Heitler and London (1927)** based on quantum mechanical principles and later developed extensively by **Linus Pauling and J.C. Slater (1931)** to explain directional properties of covalent bonds.

#### Core Postulates:
1. **Covalent Bond Formation:** A covalent bond is formed by the partial merging or **overlapping** of two half-filled atomic orbitals belonging to the valence shell of participating atoms.
2. **Spin Pairing Requirement:** The overlapping atomic orbitals must possess unpaired electrons with **antiparallel (opposite) spins**:
   

$$
\uparrow\; (\text{Atom A}) + \downarrow\; (\text{Atom B}) \longrightarrow (\uparrow\downarrow)\; (\text{Covalent Bond})
$$

3. **Electron Density Localization:** Overlapping leads to a localized concentration of electronic charge density in the internuclear region between the two bonded nuclei, minimizing electron-nuclear electrostatic potential energy.
4. **Directional Characteristics:** Since atomic orbitals (except $s$-orbitals) have definite directional orientations in space, covalent bonds formed via orbital overlap are strictly **directional**, conferring definite geometry to polyatomic molecules.
5. **Principle of Maximum Overlap:** The strength of a covalent bond is directly proportional to the **extent of overlap** of the atomic orbitals:
   

$$
\text{Bond Strength} \propto \text{Extent of Overlap} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Bond Length}}
$$

***

### 1.2 Potential Energy Diagram for the Formation of $\text{H}_2$ Molecule ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Consider two isolated hydrogen atoms, $\text{H}_A$ and $\text{H}_B$, each with a nucleus ($N_A, N_B$) and an electron ($e_A, e_B$).

```
Isolated State:        H_A (N_A, e_A)  ·········  (r = ∞)  ·········  H_B (N_B, e_B)
```

#### Forces Operative as Atoms Approach:
1. **Attractive Forces ($F_{\text{att}}$):**
   - Nucleus of $\text{H}_A$ attracting its own electron: $N_A \leftrightarrow e_A$ (Old)
   - Nucleus of $\text{H}_B$ attracting its own electron: $N_B \leftrightarrow e_B$ (Old)
   - Nucleus of $\text{H}_A$ attracting electron of $\text{H}_B$: $N_A \leftrightarrow e_B$ (New)
   - Nucleus of $\text{H}_B$ attracting electron of $\text{H}_A$: $N_B \leftrightarrow e_A$ (New)
2. **Repulsive Forces ($F_{\text{rep}}$):**
   - Repulsion between nuclei: $N_A \leftrightarrow N_B$ (New)
   - Repulsion between electrons: $e_A \leftrightarrow e_B$ (New)

#### The Energetic Profile:
- **At large internuclear distance ($r \to \infty$):** Potential energy $V(r) \to 0$. No interaction.
- **As $r$ decreases:** New attractive forces overpower new repulsive forces. Potential energy decreases ($V(r) < 0$), releasing energy (exothermic stabilization).
- **At Equilibrium Internuclear Distance ($r = r_0 = 74\text{ pm}$):**
  Attractive and repulsive forces balance precisely:
  

$$
\frac{dV(r)}{dr} = 0 \quad \text{at} \quad r = r_0
$$

  The system reaches minimum potential energy. A stable covalent bond forms.
- **For $r < r_0$:** Strong nuclear-nuclear repulsion dominates sharply. Potential energy rises steeply, destabilizing the system.

$$
\boxed{r_0(\text{H}-\text{H}) = 74\text{ pm} = 0.74\text{ Å}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^\ominus = -435.8\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \quad (\text{Bond Dissociation Enthalpy})}
$$

```
Potential Energy (kJ/mol)
       ^
       |          / (Strong internuclear repulsion when r < 74 pm)
       |         /
     0 +--------/------------------------- r -> ∞  (Zero PE)
       |       / \
       |      /   \  Attractive forces dominate
       |     /     \
-435.8 +----+       '--------------------> Internuclear Distance r (pm)
       |    r = 74 pm (Equilibrium bond length)
```

### 1.3 Types of Overlapping & Nature of Covalent Bonds ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Sign/Phase of Orbitals:
Wavefunctions $\psi$ have mathematical signs ($+$ or $-$ phases). Overlap depends on the symmetry and sign of the overlapping lobes.

| Overlap Type | Phase Condition | Physical Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positive Overlap (In-phase)** | $(+) \text{ with } (+)$ or $(-)$ with $(-)$ | Maximum electron density between nuclei; **Bond formation occurs**. |
| **Negative Overlap (Out-of-phase)** | $(+) \text{ with } (-)$ | Nodal plane formed between nuclei; electron density cancels; **No bond formation** (Antibonding/Repulsion). |
| **Zero Overlap** | Orthogonal axes or equal positive & negative interactions | Net overlap integral equals zero: $\int \psi_A \psi_B \, d\tau = 0$; **No bond formed**. |

## 2. Sigma ($\sigma$) vs Pi ($\pi$) Bonds

### 2.1 The Sigma ($\sigma$) Bond ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Formed by **end-to-end (head-on / axial)** overlap of atomic orbitals along the **internuclear axis** (standard convention: $z$-axis).

#### Types of Axial Overlap:
1. **$s-s$ Overlap:**
   - Overlap of two half-filled spherical $s$-orbitals.
   - Spherically symmetrical along the internuclear axis.
   

$$
\text{Example: } \text{H}_2 \text{ molecule } (1s^1 - 1s^1)
$$

2. **$s-p_z$ Overlap:**
   - Overlap of a half-filled spherical $s$-orbital with one lobe of a half-filled $p_z$-orbital along the $z$-axis.
   

$$
\text{Example: } \text{H}-\text{F} \text{ or } \text{H}-\text{Cl} \text{ molecule } (1s^1 - 2p_z^1)
$$

3. **$p_z-p_z$ Overlap (Axial):**
   - Co-axial overlap of two half-filled $p$-orbitals pointing along the internuclear axis.
   

$$
\text{Example: } \text{F}_2 \text{ molecule } (2p_z^1 - 2p_z^1)
$$

***

### 2.2 The Pi ($\pi$) Bond ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Formed by **lateral (sideways)** overlap of atomic orbitals oriented perpendicular to the internuclear axis.

#### Characteristics:
- The atomic orbitals remain parallel to each other and perpendicular to the internuclear axis ($z$-axis): $p_x-p_x$ or $p_y-p_y$ overlap.
- The electron density consists of two sausage-shaped clouds: one **above** and one **below** the plane of the participating nuclei.
- Contains an intrinsic **nodal plane** passing through the internuclear axis where electron density is zero.

$$
\text{Nodal Plane of } \pi(p_x-p_x) \text{ bond } = yz\text{-plane} \quad (\text{for internuclear axis } = z)
$$

$$
\text{Nodal Plane of } \pi(p_y-p_y) \text{ bond } = xz\text{-plane} \quad (\text{for internuclear axis } = z)
$$

***

### 2.3 Comprehensive Comparison: $\sigma$-Bond vs $\pi$-Bond

| Property | Sigma ($\sigma$) Bond | Pi ($\pi$) Bond |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Type of Overlap** | Axial / Head-on / End-to-end | Lateral / Sideways / Parallel |
| **Overlapping Orbitals** | $s-s$, $s-p$, $p-p$, hybridized orbitals | Unhybridized pure $p-p$, $p-d$, $d-d$ orbitals |
| **Extent of Overlap** | **Large** (high orbital penetration) | **Small** (limited lateral contact) |
| **Bond Strength** | Stronger bond; high dissociation energy | Weaker bond; low dissociation energy |
| **Electron Cloud Symmetry** | Cylindrically symmetrical about internuclear axis | Discontinuous; two lobes (above & below internuclear axis) |
| **Free Rotation** | Permitted (rotation does not alter overlap extent) | **Restricted / Hindered** (rotation breaks parallel overlap) |
| **Independent Existence**| Can exist independently (single bond is always $\sigma$) | Cannot exist independently; forms only after a $\sigma$-bond is formed |
| **Molecular Geometry** | Dictates hybridization and molecular shape | Modifies bond lengths/angles slightly; does not alter primary geometry |

***

### 2.4 Relative Overlap Strength Hierarchy ⭐⭐⭐⭐
For orbitals with the same principal quantum number $n$:

$$
\boxed{p-p\; (\text{axial}) > s-p\; (\text{axial}) > s-s\; (\text{axial}) > p-p\; (\text{sideways, } \pi)}
$$

*Reason:* $p$-orbitals are directionally concentrated along their lobes, permitting deeper interpenetration along the bond axis than non-directional, diffuse $s$-orbitals.

For different shell levels:

$$
\boxed{1s-1s > 2p-2p > 2s-2p > 2s-2s > 3p-3p}
$$

*Reason:* As principal quantum number $n$ increases, orbital size increases, diffuseness increases, and overlap efficiency drops drastically.

## 3. The Concept of Hybridisation

### 3.1 Limitations of Pure Valence Bond Overlap ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Consider methane ($\text{CH}_4$):
- Ground state of Carbon: $1s^2\, 2s^2\, 2p_x^1\, 2p_y^1\, 2p_z^0$ (2 unpaired electrons $\implies$ should form $\text{CH}_2$).
- Excited state of Carbon: $1s^2\, 2s^1\, 2p_x^1\, 2p_y^1\, 2p_z^1$ (4 unpaired electrons).
- If Carbon were to bond using pure atomic orbitals:
  - Three $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bonds would involve $2p-1s$ overlap along $x, y, z$ axes at mutual angles of $90^\circ$.
  - One $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bond would involve $2s-1s$ overlap (non-directional, differing bond length and strength).
- **Experimental Reality:** All four $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bonds are completely **identical** in bond length ($109\text{ pm}$), bond energy ($414\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$), and all $\text{H}-\text{C}-\text{H}$ bond angles are precisely $109^\circ 28'$.
- This necessitates the concept of **hybridisation** (Pauling, 1931).

***

### 3.2 Definition & Salient Features of Hybridisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> **Definition:** The phenomenon of intermixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies belonging to the same isolated atom, followed by a redistribution of energy to produce an entirely new set of equivalent orbitals having identical shapes, degenerate energies, and definite spatial orientations.

#### Salient Rules:
1. The number of hybrid orbitals formed is **strictly equal** to the number of atomic orbitals that undergo hybridisation:
   

$$
N_{\text{hybrid orbitals}} = N_{\text{atomic orbitals intermixed}}
$$

2. Hybridized orbitals are always equivalent in energy and shape.
3. Hybrid orbitals form **stronger and more directed** bonds than pure atomic orbitals because their directional lobes are larger on one side of the nucleus:
   ```
   Pure p-orbital:      (+) | (-)          (Symmetrical lobes)
   Hybrid orbital:     ( +++ ) . (-)       (One large major lobe, one tiny minor lobe)
   ```
4. **Promotion is not an essential condition:** Excitation of an electron to a higher subshell is not mandatory prior to hybridisation; empty, half-filled, or fully-filled valence orbitals can participate.
5. **Hybrid orbitals form only $\sigma$-bonds.** Unhybridized pure $p$- or $d$-orbitals are utilized for lateral $\pi$-bonding.

***

### 3.3 Steric Number Rule: Predicting Hybridisation State ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$
\boxed{\text{Steric Number (SN)} = \sigma + \text{LP} = \frac{1}{2}\Big[ V + M - C + A \Big]}
$$

Where:
- $V =$ Number of valence electrons of the central atom
- $M =$ Number of monovalent surrounding atoms ($\text{H, F, Cl, Br, I}$)
- $C =$ Positive charge on the species (cation charge)
- $A =$ Magnitude of negative charge on the species (anion charge)

| Steric Number (SN) | Hybridisation Scheme | Parent Geometry | Ideal Bond Angle |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **2** | $sp$ | Linear | $180^\circ$ |
| **3** | $sp^2$ | Trigonal Planar | $120^\circ$ |
| **4** | $sp^3$ | Tetrahedral | $109^\circ 28' \approx 109.5^\circ$ |
| **5** | $sp^3d$ | Trigonal Bipyramidal (TBP) | $120^\circ \text{ (eq)}, 90^\circ \text{ (ax)}, 180^\circ$ |
| **6** | $sp^3d^2$ | Octahedral / Square Bipyramidal | $90^\circ, 180^\circ$ |
| **7** | $sp^3d^3$ | Pentagonal Bipyramidal (PBP) | $72^\circ \text{ (eq)}, 90^\circ \text{ (ax)}$ |

### 4.1 $sp$ Hybridisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Orbitals Mixed:** One $s$ + One $p$ orbital (typically $p_z$, taking $z$ as internuclear axis).
- **Percentage Character:** $50\% \; s\text{-character}, 50\% \; p\text{-character}$.
- **Geometry & Angle:** Linear, with bond angle = $180^\circ$.

#### Exemplar Case 1: Beryllium Chloride ($\text{BeCl}_2$ in vapour phase)
- Ground State of $\text{Be } (Z=4)$: $[ \text{He} ] \, 2s^2 \, 2p^0$
- Excited State of $\text{Be}$: $[ \text{He} ] \, 2s^1 \, 2p_z^1$
- Intermixing: One $2s$ + One $2p_z \longrightarrow$ Two degenerate $sp$ hybrid orbitals oriented at $180^\circ$.
- Bond Formation: Each $sp$ hybrid orbital overlaps axially with a singly occupied $3p_z$ orbital of Chlorine:
  

$$
2 \times \sigma(sp_{\text{Be}} - 3p_{z,\text{Cl}})
$$

- Net Dipole Moment: $\mu = 0\text{ D}$ due to vector cancellation.

#### Exemplar Case 2: Ethyne ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$)
- Excited state Carbon: $2s^1 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$.
- Carbon undergoes $sp$ hybridisation ($2s + 2p_z$), leaving $2p_x$ and $2p_y$ unhybridized.
- Overlaps per molecule:
  - One $\text{C}-\text{C}$ axial overlap: $\sigma(sp - sp)$
  - Two $\text{C}-\text{H}$ axial overlaps: $2 \times \sigma(sp - 1s)$
  - Two mutually perpendicular lateral overlaps: $\pi(2p_x - 2p_x)$ and $\pi(2p_y - 2p_y)$
- **Net Bond Count:** $3\sigma + 2\pi$ bonds.

***

### 4.2 $sp^2$ Hybridisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Orbitals Mixed:** One $s$ + Two $p$ orbitals (typically $s + p_x + p_y$ lying in $xy$-plane).
- **Percentage Character:** $33.33\% \; s\text{-character}, 66.67\% \; p\text{-character}$.
- **Geometry & Angle:** Trigonal Planar, with bond angle = $120^\circ$.

#### Exemplar Case 1: Boron Trichloride ($\text{BCl}_3$)
- Ground State of $\text{B } (Z=5)$: $[ \text{He} ] \, 2s^2 \, 2p_x^1 \, 2p_y^0 \, 2p_z^0$
- Excited State of $\text{B}$: $[ \text{He} ] \, 2s^1 \, 2p_x^1 \, 2p_y^1 \, 2p_z^0$
- Intermixing: $2s + 2p_x + 2p_y \longrightarrow$ Three equivalent $sp^2$ hybrid orbitals pointing to corners of an equilateral triangle.
- Bonding: Three $\sigma(sp^2_{\text{B}} - 3p_{z,\text{Cl}})$ bonds.
- Geometry: Pure Trigonal Planar, $\angle \text{Cl}-\text{B}-\text{Cl} = 120^\circ$, $\mu_{\text{net}} = 0\text{ D}$.

#### Exemplar Case 2: Ethene ($\text{H}_2\text{C}=\text{CH}_2$)
- Excited Carbon undergoes $sp^2$ hybridisation ($2s, 2p_x, 2p_y$).
- The unhybridized $2p_z$ orbital remains oriented perpendicular to the nodal molecular plane ($xy$-plane).
- Overlaps:
  - One $\text{C}-\text{C}$ axial overlap: $\sigma(sp^2 - sp^2)$
  - Four $\text{C}-\text{H}$ axial overlaps: $4 \times \sigma(sp^2 - 1s)$
  - One lateral overlap: $\pi(2p_z - 2p_z)$
- **Net Bond Count:** $5\sigma + 1\pi$ bond.

***

### 4.3 $sp^3$ Hybridisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Orbitals Mixed:** One $s$ + Three $p$ orbitals ($s + p_x + p_y + p_z$).
- **Percentage Character:** $25\% \; s\text{-character}, 75\% \; p\text{-character}$.
- **Geometry & Angle:** Regular Tetrahedral, with bond angle = $109^\circ 28' \approx 109.5^\circ$.

#### Exemplar Case 1: Methane ($\text{CH}_4$)
- Ground State $\text{C}$: $[ \text{He} ] \, 2s^2 \, 2p_x^1 \, 2p_y^1$
- Excited State $\text{C}$: $[ \text{He} ] \, 2s^1 \, 2p_x^1 \, 2p_y^1 \, 2p_z^1$
- Intermixing: Four equivalent $sp^3$ orbitals pointing towards four corners of a regular tetrahedron.
- Bonding: $4 \times \sigma(sp^3_{\text{C}} - 1s_{\text{H}})$. All four bond lengths = $109\text{ pm}$, bond angles = $109.5^\circ$.

#### Effect of Lone Pairs on $sp^3$ Hybridized Systems (VSEPR Integration):
When non-bonding electron pairs (lone pairs) occupy hybrid orbitals, repulsive hierarchy alters geometry:

$$
\boxed{\text{LP}-\text{LP} > \text{LP}-\text{BP} > \text{BP}-\text{BP}}
$$

1. **Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$):**
   - Central N: $\text{SN} = 3\sigma + 1\text{LP} = 4 \implies sp^3$.
   - The lone pair in one $sp^3$ orbital exerts greater repulsion on bonding pairs.
   - Geometry: **Trigonal Pyramidal**.
   - Bond angle contracts from $109.5^\circ$ to **$107^\circ$**.
2. **Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$):**
   - Central O: $\text{SN} = 2\sigma + 2\text{LP} = 4 \implies sp^3$.
   - Two lone pairs exert intense $\text{LP}-\text{LP}$ and $\text{LP}-\text{BP}$ repulsions.
   - Geometry: **Bent / V-shaped / Angular**.
   - Bond angle contracts further to **$104.5^\circ$**.

***

### 4.4 $sp^3d$ Hybridisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Orbitals Mixed:** One $s$ + Three $p$ ($p_x, p_y, p_z$) + One $d_{z^2}$ orbital.
- **Why specifically $d_{z^2}$?** Because the trigonal bipyramidal geometry requires axial symmetry along the $z$-axis.
- **Decomposition of $sp^3d$:**
  

$$
sp^3d \equiv \underbrace{(s + p_x + p_y)}_{\text{Trigonal Planar } (sp^2)} + \underbrace{(p_z + d_{z^2})}_{\text{Linear Axial } (pd)}
$$

- **Geometry:** Trigonal Bipyramidal (TBP).

#### Exemplar Case: Phosphorus Pentachloride ($\text{PCl}_5$)
- Ground State of $\text{P } (Z=15)$: $[ \text{Ne} ] \, 3s^2 \, 3p_x^1 \, 3p_y^1 \, 3p_z^1 \, 3d^0$
- Excited State of $\text{P}$: $[ \text{Ne} ] \, 3s^1 \, 3p_x^1 \, 3p_y^1 \, 3p_z^1 \, 3d_{z^2}^1$
- Intermixing: Five $sp^3d$ hybrid orbitals directed towards the vertices of a trigonal bipyramid.

```
                  Cl (Axial)
                  |
                  |  240 pm (Longer, weaker)
                  P --- Cl (Equatorial, 202 pm)
                 / \
                /   \
   Cl (Equatorial)   Cl (Equatorial)
                  |
                  |
                  Cl (Axial)
```

#### The Axial vs Equatorial Bond Asymmetry (High-Yield):
In $\text{PCl}_5$, the 5 bonds are **NOT equivalent**:
- **3 Equatorial Bonds ($\text{P}-\text{Cl}_{\text{eq}}$):** Lie in the horizontal plane at mutual angles of $120^\circ$. Each equatorial bond experiences repulsion from only two axial bonds at $90^\circ$.
- **2 Axial Bonds ($\text{P}-\text{Cl}_{\text{ax}}$):** Lie perpendicular to the equatorial plane at $180^\circ$ to each other. Each axial bond experiences repulsion from **three equatorial bonds at $90^\circ$**.
- Because repulsive forces are far stronger at $90^\circ$ than at $120^\circ$:
  

$$
\boxed{\text{Repulsion experienced by Axial bonds} > \text{Repulsion experienced by Equatorial bonds}}
$$

- To minimize repulsion, axial bonds are pushed outward:
  

$$
\boxed{r(\text{P}-\text{Cl}_{\text{axial}}) = 240\text{ pm} > r(\text{P}-\text{Cl}_{\text{equatorial}}) = 202\text{ pm}}
$$

- **Chemical Consequence:** $\text{PCl}_5$ is thermally unstable. On gentle heating, the two weaker axial bonds break preferentially:
  

$$
\text{PCl}_5(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{PCl}_3(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g)
$$

***

### 4.5 $sp^3d^2$ Hybridisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
- **Orbitals Mixed:** One $s$ + Three $p$ ($p_x, p_y, p_z$) + Two $d$ orbitals ($d_{x^2-y^2} \text{ and } d_{z^2}$).
- **Why $d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$?** Both orbitals have lobes pointing directly along the Cartesian axes ($x, y, z$), which matches the octahedral coordinate frame.
- **Decomposition:**
  

$$
sp^3d^2 \equiv \underbrace{(s + p_x + p_y + d_{x^2-y^2})}_{\text{Square Planar } (sp^2d)} + \underbrace{(p_z + d_{z^2})}_{\text{Linear Axial } (pd)}
$$

- **Geometry:** Regular Octahedral (Square Bipyramidal).
- **Bond Angles:** All adjacent angles are strictly $90^\circ$ (12 such angles); trans angles are $180^\circ$ (3 such pairs).
- **Equivalence:** Unlike $\text{PCl}_5$, all 6 positions in a regular octahedron are **strictly equivalent**.

#### Exemplar Case: Sulfur Hexafluoride ($\text{SF}_6$)
- Ground State of $\text{S } (Z=16)$: $[ \text{Ne} ] \, 3s^2 \, 3p_x^2 \, 3p_y^1 \, 3p_z^1 \, 3d^0$
- Excited State of $\text{S}$ (promotes 2 electrons): $[ \text{Ne} ] \, 3s^1 \, 3p_x^1 \, 3p_y^1 \, 3p_z^1 \, 3d_{z^2}^1 \, 3d_{x^2-y^2}^1$
- Intermixing: Six equivalent $sp^3d^2$ hybrid orbitals directed to the six corners of a regular octahedron.
- Bonding: $6 \times \sigma(sp^3d^2_{\text{S}} - 2p_{z,\text{F}})$.
- Bond length: All six $\text{S}-\text{F}$ bonds are completely identical ($156.4\text{ pm}$).
- Extreme chemical inertness: Kinetic stability due to complete steric shielding of the central Sulfur atom by 6 surrounding Fluorine atoms.

## 5. Quantitative Parameters: Bond Angles, $s$-Character & Electronegativity

### 5.1 Bent's Rule & Cosine Relationship ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Mathematical Formulation:
For an orbital $i$ having hybridisation $sp^{n_i}$, let the fractional $s$-character be $S_i$ and fractional $p$-character be $P_i$ such that $S_i + P_i = 1$. The interorbital angle $\theta_{ij}$ between two hybrid orbitals $i$ and $j$ is governed by:

$$
\boxed{1 + \lambda_i \lambda_j \cos\theta_{ij} = 0}
$$

Where $\lambda^2 = n$ (the mixing coefficient ratio $p/s$).  
For equivalent hybrid orbitals ($\lambda_i = \lambda_j = \lambda$):

$$
\boxed{\cos\theta = -\frac{S}{1 - S} = -\frac{1}{n} = -\frac{S}{P}}
$$

*Verification:*
- For $sp$ ($S = 0.5, P = 0.5, n = 1$): $\cos\theta = -\frac{0.5}{1-0.5} = -1 \implies \theta = 180^\circ$.
- For $sp^2$ ($S = \frac{1}{3}, P = \frac{2}{3}, n = 2$): $\cos\theta = -\frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = 120^\circ$.
- For $sp^3$ ($S = \frac{1}{4}, P = \frac{3}{4}, n = 3$): $\cos\theta = -\frac{1}{3} \approx -0.3333 \implies \theta = 109^\circ 28'$.

#### Bent's Rule (Formal Statement):
> **"More electronegative substituent atoms prefer to bond to hybrid orbitals having less $s$-character (more $p$-character), whereas lone pairs and more electropositive substituents prefer hybrid orbitals having greater $s$-character."**

#### Practical Consequences of Bent's Rule:
1. **In Trigonal Bipyramidal ($\text{PCl}_3\text{F}_2$):**
   - Equatorial orbitals have $sp^2$ character ($33.3\% \; s$).
   - Axial orbitals have $pd$ character ($0\% \; s$).
   - More electronegative Fluorine atoms exclusively occupy the **axial** positions (lower $s$-character).
   - Less electronegative Chlorine atoms occupy the **equatorial** positions.
2. **In Fluoromethanes ($\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ vs $\text{CF}_4$):**
   - As electronegative $\text{F}$ pulls electron density away, the $\text{C}-\text{F}$ bond orbital acquires more $p$-character.
   - Consequently, the $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bonds acquire more $s$-character, causing the $\angle \text{H}-\text{C}-\text{H}$ angle in $\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ to expand ($> 109.5^\circ$) while $\angle \text{F}-\text{C}-\text{F}$ contracts ($< 109.5^\circ$).

### 5.2 $s$-Character, Electronegativity, and Acidity Correlation ⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$
\boxed{\% \; s\text{-character}: \quad sp\; (50\%) > sp^2\; (33.3\%) > sp^3\; (25\%)}
$$

#### Direct Consequences:
1. **Electronegativity of Carbon:**
   Since $s$-orbitals penetrate closer to the positive nucleus than $p$-orbitals, an increase in $s$-character pulls the electron cloud closer to the carbon nucleus:
   

$$
\boxed{\text{Electronegativity}: \quad \text{C}_{sp} \ (3.29) > \text{C}_{sp^2} \ (2.75) > \text{C}_{sp^3} \ (2.50)}
$$

2. **Carbon-Hydrogen ($\text{C}-\text{H}$) Bond Length:**
   Greater $s$-character produces a shorter, tighter, and stronger orbital:
   

$$
\boxed{\text{Bond Length}: \quad \text{C}_{sp^3}-\text{H} \ (1.10\text{ Å}) > \text{C}_{sp^2}-\text{H} \ (1.08\text{ Å}) > \text{C}_{sp}-\text{H} \ (1.06\text{ Å})}
$$

3. **Acidic Strength of Hydrocarbons:**
   A conjugate base formed by deprotonating an $sp$-hybridized carbon ($\text{H}-\text{C}\equiv\text{C}^-$) holds the conjugate lone pair in an orbital with $50\% \; s$-character, stabilizing the negative charge:
   

$$
\boxed{K_a: \quad \text{H}-\text{C}\equiv\text{C}-\text{H} \ (pK_a \approx 25) \gg \text{H}_2\text{C}=\text{CH}_2 \ (pK_a \approx 44) \gg \text{CH}_3-\text{CH}_3 \ (pK_a \approx 50)}
$$

## 6. High-Yield Solved Problems & Exemplar Archetypes

### Problem 1: Step-by-Step Counting of $\sigma$ and $\pi$ Bonds in Complex Molecules ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem:** Determine the total number of $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonds and the hybridisation of each carbon atom in:
1. Tetracyanoethylene: $(\text{NC})_2\text{C}=\text{C}(\text{CN})_2$
2. Vinylacetylene: $\text{H}_2\text{C}=\text{CH}-\text{C}\equiv\text{CH}$

#### Solution:
**Part 1: Tetracyanoethylene**
- Expand the structural formula showing all multiple bonds:
  ```
  N ≡ C       C ≡ N
       \     /
        C = C
       /     \
  N ≡ C       C ≡ N
  ```
- Analyzing bonds:
  - $\text{C}=\text{C}$ double bond: $1\sigma + 1\pi$
  - $4 \times (\text{C}-\text{C})$ single bonds connecting nitrile groups: $4\sigma$
  - $4 \times (\text{C}\equiv\text{N})$ triple bonds: $4 \times (1\sigma + 2\pi) = 4\sigma + 8\pi$
- **Total $\sigma$ bonds:** $1 + 4 + 4 = 9\sigma$
- **Total $\pi$ bonds:** $1 + 8 = 9\pi$
- **Hybridisations:**
  - Central 2 Carbons (each with $3\sigma, 0\text{LP}$): **$sp^2$**
  - Nitrile 4 Carbons (each with $2\sigma, 0\text{LP}$): **$sp$**
  - Nitrile 4 Nitrogens (each with $1\sigma, 1\text{LP}$): **$sp$**

$$
\boxed{\text{Answer: } 9\sigma\text{ bonds}, \; 9\pi\text{ bonds}}
$$

**Part 2: Vinylacetylene**
- Numbering carbons:
  

$$
\overset{1}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 = \overset{2}{\text{C}}\text{H} - \overset{3}{\text{C}} \equiv \overset{4}{\text{C}}\text{H}
$$

- Bond counting:
  - $\text{C}_1=\text{C}_2$: $1\sigma + 1\pi$
  - $\text{C}_1-\text{H}$ (two bonds): $2\sigma$
  - $\text{C}_2-\text{H}$ (one bond): $1\sigma$
  - $\text{C}_2-\text{C}_3$: $1\sigma$
  - $\text{C}_3\equiv\text{C}_4$: $1\sigma + 2\pi$
  - $\text{C}_4-\text{H}$: $1\sigma$
- **Total $\sigma$ bonds:** $1 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 7\sigma$
- **Total $\pi$ bonds:** $1 + 2 = 3\pi$
- **Hybridisation:**
  

$$
\text{C}_1: sp^2, \quad \text{C}_2: sp^2, \quad \text{C}_3: sp, \quad \text{C}_4: sp
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Answer: } 7\sigma\text{ bonds}, \; 3\pi\text{ bonds}}
$$

### Problem 2: Solid State Dissociation & Structural Transformation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem (NCERT Exemplar):** 
1. Explain why $\text{PCl}_5$ exists as an ionic solid in the crystalline state. Give the hybridisation and geometry of the ionic species involved.
2. What happens when solid $\text{PBr}_5$ crystallizes?

#### Solution:
**1. Solid $\text{PCl}_5$:**
In the gas/liquid phase, $\text{PCl}_5$ exists as discrete trigonal bipyramidal covalent molecules. However, in the solid state, it undergoes auto-ionization to minimize steric overcrowding and lattice strain:

$$
\boxed{2\text{PCl}_5(s) \rightleftharpoons [\text{PCl}_4]^+ [\text{PCl}_6]^-}
$$

- **Cation: $[\text{PCl}_4]^+$**
  - $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}[5 + 4 - 1] = 4 \implies \mathbf{sp^3}$ **Hybridisation**
  - Geometry: **Regular Tetrahedral** ($\angle \text{Cl}-\text{P}-\text{Cl} = 109.5^\circ$)
- **Anion: $[\text{PCl}_6]^-$**
  - $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}[5 + 6 + 1] = 6 \implies \mathbf{sp^3d^2}$ **Hybridisation**
  - Geometry: **Regular Octahedral** ($\angle \text{Cl}-\text{P}-\text{Cl} = 90^\circ, 180^\circ$)

**2. Solid $\text{PBr}_5$:**
Because Bromine atoms are larger than Chlorine atoms, six Bromine atoms cannot pack octahedrally around a single Phosphorus atom due to extreme steric hindrance. Hence, $\text{PBr}_5$ crystallizes differently:

$$
\boxed{\text{PBr}_5(s) \rightleftharpoons [\text{PBr}_4]^+ [\text{Br}]^-}
$$

- Cation: $[\text{PBr}_4]^+$ is **Tetrahedral ($sp^3$)**.
- Counter-ion: Simple bromide anion $[\text{Br}]^-$.

### Problem 3: Hybrid Orbitals Involved in $d$-Hybridisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem:** Identify the exact $d$-orbitals involved in:
1. $sp^3d$ in $\text{PCl}_5$
2. $sp^3d^2$ in $\text{SF}_6$
3. $sp^3d$ in $\text{BrF}_3$ (T-shaped)
4. $dsp^2$ in $[\text{Ni}(\text{CN})_4]^{2-}$

#### Solution:
1. **$\text{PCl}_5$ ($sp^3d$, Trigonal Bipyramidal):**
   The axial bonds lie strictly along the $z$-axis. Thus, the participating $d$-orbital must have directional lobes along the $z$-axis:
   

$$
\boxed{d\text{-orbital used} = d_{z^2}}
$$

2. **$\text{SF}_6$ ($sp^3d^2$, Octahedral):**
   Lobe axes point along Cartesian coordinates $x, y, z$. The two matching $e_g$ set orbitals are:
   

$$
\boxed{d\text{-orbitals used} = d_{z^2} \text{ and } d_{x^2-y^2}}
$$

3. **$\text{BrF}_3$ ($sp^3d$, T-shaped):**
   Bromine has $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}[7+3] = 5$ (3 bonding pairs + 2 lone pairs). Lone pairs prefer equatorial positions ($xy$-plane), and axial positions are on the $z$-axis:
   

$$
\boxed{d\text{-orbital used} = d_{z^2}}
$$

4. **$[\text{Ni}(\text{CN})_4]^{2-}$ ($dsp^2$, Square Planar):**
   The four ligands lie in the $xy$-plane pointing directly along the $x$ and $y$ axes:
   

$$
\boxed{d\text{-orbital used} = d_{x^2-y^2}}
$$

### Problem 4: Bond Angle Alterations & Isoelectronic Comparisons ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Problem:** Arrange the following sets in decreasing order of bond angle and justify using hybridisation and electron pair repulsion:
1. $\text{NO}_2^+$, $\text{NO}_2$, $\text{NO}_2^-$
2. $\text{NH}_4^+$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{NF}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$

#### Solution:
**Set 1: Nitrogen Oxides/Ions**
- $\mathbf{\text{NO}_2^+}$ (Nitronium cation):
  

$$
\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}[5 - 1] = 2 \implies sp \text{ hybridisation} \implies \text{Linear}, \; \angle\text{O}-\text{N}-\text{O} = \mathbf{180^\circ}
$$

- $\mathbf{\text{NO}_2}$ (Neutral radical):
  $sp^2$ hybridised with a **single unpaired electron** in one hybrid orbital. The repulsive force of an odd electron is smaller than that of a complete lone pair:
  

$$
\angle\text{O}-\text{N}-\text{O} \approx \mathbf{134^\circ}
$$

- $\mathbf{\text{NO}_2^-}$ (Nitrite anion):
  $sp^2$ hybridised with a **full non-bonding lone pair**. The lone pair exerts full $\text{LP}-\text{BP}$ repulsion, compressing the bond angle:
  

$$
\angle\text{O}-\text{N}-\text{O} \approx \mathbf{115^\circ}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Order: } \text{NO}_2^+ (180^\circ) > \text{NO}_2 (134^\circ) > \text{NO}_2^- (115^\circ)}
$$

**Set 2: Hydrides and Halides**
- All central atoms are based on an $sp^3$ steric framework:
  - $\mathbf{\text{NH}_4^+}$: $4\text{BP} + 0\text{LP} \implies$ Regular tetrahedral $\implies \mathbf{109.5^\circ}$
  - $\mathbf{\text{NH}_3}$: $3\text{BP} + 1\text{LP} \implies \text{LP}-\text{BP}$ repulsion compresses angle $\implies \mathbf{107^\circ}$
  - $\mathbf{\text{NF}_3}$: Highly electronegative $\text{F}$ atoms pull shared pairs towards themselves. The bonding pairs reside further from Nitrogen, decreasing $\text{BP}-\text{BP}$ repulsion. The lone pair on Nitrogen compresses the bonds more effectively:
    

$$
\angle\text{F}-\text{N}-\text{F} \approx \mathbf{102.4^\circ}
$$

  - $\mathbf{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$: $2\text{BP} + 2\text{LP} \implies$ Strong $\text{LP}-\text{LP}$ repulsion compresses angle $\implies \mathbf{104.5^\circ}$

$$
\boxed{\text{Order: } \text{NH}_4^+ (109.5^\circ) > \text{NH}_3 (107^\circ) > \text{H}_2\text{O} (104.5^\circ) > \text{NF}_3 (102.4^\circ)}
$$

## 7. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

### Trap 1: Confusing Nodal Planes of $\sigma$ vs $\pi$ Bonds
- **Error:** Writing that a $\sigma$-bond has a nodal plane between the nuclei.
- **Correction:** A bonding $\sigma$-orbital has **zero** nodal planes perpendicular to the internuclear axis; its electron density is maximal between the nuclei. A $\pi$-bond formed by $p_x-p_x$ (internuclear axis = $z$) has **one** nodal plane, which is the entire $yz$ coordinate plane.

***

### Trap 2: Counting Double Bonds as Two $\sigma$-Bonds
- **Error:** Treating a double bond as $2\sigma$ or a triple bond as $3\sigma$.
- **Correction:**
  

$$
\text{Single Bond} = 1\sigma
$$

  

$$
\text{Double Bond} = 1\sigma + 1\pi
$$

  

$$
\text{Triple Bond} = 1\sigma + 2\pi
$$

  *Any multiple covalent link contains one and only one $\sigma$-bond; all additional bonds are $\pi$-bonds.*

***

### Trap 3: Axial vs Equatorial Bond Length Inversion in $\text{PCl}_5$
- **Error:** Stating that equatorial bonds are longer because there are three of them.
- **Correction:** Axial bonds are **longer** ($240\text{ pm}$) and equatorial bonds are **shorter** ($202\text{ pm}$). Axial bonds face three $90^\circ$ repulsions; equatorial bonds face only two $90^\circ$ repulsions.

***

### Trap 4: Attempting to Hybridize Terminal Monovalent Atoms
- **Error:** Assigning complex hybridisation states to terminal hydrogen or halogen atoms (e.g., calling $\text{Cl}$ in $\text{HCl}$ $sp^3$ hybridized).
- **Correction:** In standard NCERT/board conventions, terminal atoms forming a single bond are treated using their pure, unhybridized half-filled atomic orbitals (e.g., $1s$ for $\text{H}$, $3p_z$ for $\text{Cl}$).

***

### Trap 5: Forgetting that $d$-Orbitals in $sp^3d$ and $sp^3d^2$ Are Not Arbitrary
- **Error:** Writing $d_{xy}$ or $d_{yz}$ as the mixing orbital in $\text{PCl}_5$.
- **Correction:** 
  - For $sp^3d$ (TBP) $\implies$ strictly **$d_{z^2}$**.
  - For $sp^3d^2$ (Octahedral) $\implies$ strictly **$d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$**.

***

### Trap 6: Assuming Lone Pairs Do Not Participate in Hybridisation
- **Error:** Calculating Steric Number by counting only $\sigma$-bonds.
- **Correction:** Localized lone pairs occupy hybridized orbitals!
  

$$
\text{SN} = \text{Number of } \sigma\text{-bonds} + \text{Number of localized lone pairs}
$$

  *(Exception: Delocalized lone pairs involved in resonance/aromaticity remain in pure $p$-orbitals).*

***

## 8. Speed Hacks & Golden Rules

### Hack 1: The Fast Hybridisation Formula
For any neutral molecule or polyatomic ion:

$$
\boxed{\text{Steric Number} = \frac{V + M - C + A}{2}}
$$

```
                      Is there a Lone Pair?
                              |
              +---------------+---------------+
              |                               |
              No                              Yes
              |                               |
       Geometry = Shape               Shape ≠ Geometry
    (Regular, Ideal Angles)        (Distorted by LP Repulsion)
```

- $\text{SN} = 2 \implies sp \implies \text{Linear}$
- $\text{SN} = 3 \implies sp^2 \implies \text{Trigonal Planar}$
- $\text{SN} = 4 \implies sp^3 \implies \text{Tetrahedral}$
- $\text{SN} = 5 \implies sp^3d \implies \text{Trigonal Bipyramidal}$
- $\text{SN} = 6 \implies sp^3d^2 \implies \text{Octahedral}$

***

### Hack 2: Rapid Determination of Hybridisation in Organic Frameworks
Count only the $\pi$-bonds attached directly to the carbon:

$$
\boxed{\text{0 } \pi\text{-bonds} \implies sp^3}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{1 } \pi\text{-bond} \implies sp^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{2 } \pi\text{-bonds} \implies sp \quad (\text{either one } -\text{C}\equiv\text{C}- \text{ or two cumulative } =\text{C}=)}
$$

*Example:* Allene ($\text{H}_2\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{CH}_2$):
- Terminal Carbons have one $\pi$-bond each $\implies \mathbf{sp^2}$
- Central Carbon has two $\pi$-bonds $\implies \mathbf{sp}$

### Hack 3: Bent's Rule at a Glance

$$
\boxed{\text{"Equatorial likes bulky/electropositive/lone pairs; Axial likes skinny/electronegative atoms."}}
$$

*(Applicable to Trigonal Bipyramidal systems where axial positions have zero $s$-character and equatorial positions have $33.3\% \; s$-character).*

### Hack 4: The Number of Non-Crossing Angles in Octahedral Geometry
In an $sp^3d^2$ regular octahedral molecule like $\text{SF}_6$:
- Number of $90^\circ$ bond angles = **$12$**
- Number of $180^\circ$ bond angles (trans pairs) = **$3$**
