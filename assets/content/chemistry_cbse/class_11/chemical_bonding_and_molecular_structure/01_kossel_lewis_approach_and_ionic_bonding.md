## 1. The Kössel-Lewis Approach to Chemical Bonding

### 1.1 Historical Context & The Driving Force of Bonding ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Prior to the modern electronic theory of valence, chemical affinity lacked a quantitative electronic explanation. In 1916, **Walther Kössel** and **Gilbert N. Lewis** independently formulated logical explanations of valence based on the inertness of noble gases.

* **Lewis's Conception:** Lewis envisioned the atom in terms of a positively charged **"Kernel"** (the nucleus plus the inner, completely filled electron shells) and an **outer shell** that could accommodate a maximum of eight electrons. He postulated that atoms attain a stable cubic arrangement where the eight valence electrons occupy the eight corners of a cube surrounding the kernel.
* **Kössel's Postulates:**
  1. In the periodic table, the highly electronegative halogens and the highly electropositive alkali metals are separated by the noble gases.
  2. The formation of a negative ion from a halogen atom and a positive ion from an alkali metal atom involves the gain and loss of electrons by the respective atoms.
  3. The negative and positive ions attain stable noble gas electronic configurations ($ns^2 np^6$, except Helium which attains a stable duplet $1s^2$).
  4. The electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions holds them together in a three-dimensional crystalline lattice, forming an **electrovalent** (ionic) bond.

***

### 1.2 Lewis Symbols (Electron Dot Structures) ⭐⭐⭐
To represent the valence electrons of an atom, simple notation called **Lewis Dot Symbols** is employed. The inner core electrons are represented by the chemical symbol of the element, while the outermost valence electrons are represented by dots placed symmetrically around the symbol.

$$
\text{Group Valence} = \text{Number of dots (if } \le 4 \text{)} \quad \text{or} \quad 8 - (\text{Number of dots})
$$

#### Representation of the Second Period:
* **Lithium ($1s^2 2s^1$):** $\cdot \text{Li}$ (Valence = $1$)
* **Beryllium ($1s^2 2s^2$):** $\cdot \text{Be} \cdot$ (Valence = $2$)
* **Boron ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^1$):** $\cdot \dot{\text{B}} \cdot$ (Valence = $3$)
* **Carbon ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$):** $\cdot \dot{\underset{\cdot}{\text{C}}} \cdot$ (Valence = $4$)
* **Nitrogen ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^3$):** $:\dot{\underset{\cdot}{\text{N}}} \cdot$ (Valence = $8 - 5 = 3$)
* **Oxygen ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$):** $:\ddot{\underset{\cdot}{\text{O}}} \cdot$ (Valence = $8 - 6 = 2$)
* **Fluorine ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^5$):** $:\ddot{\underset{\cdot}{\text{F}}} :$ (Valence = $8 - 7 = 1$)
* **Neon ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$):** $:\ddot{\underset{\cdot\cdot}{\text{Ne}}} :$ (Valence = $8 - 8 = 0$)

### 1.3 The Octet Rule ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
The fundamental premise developed by Lewis and Kössel is termed the **Electronic Theory of Valence**:
> *"Atoms of various elements combine with one another either by transfer of valence electrons from one atom to another (gain or loss) or by sharing of valence electrons in order to acquire a stable octet (eight electrons) in their outermost shells."*

#### Systematic Writing of Lewis Dot Structures:
1. **Calculate the total number of valence electrons ($n_1$):** Sum the valence electrons of all combining atoms. Add $1$ electron for each unit of negative charge; subtract $1$ electron for each unit of positive charge.
2. **Determine the central atom:** The central atom is generally the least electronegative element (excluding Hydrogen and Fluorine, which occupy terminal positions).
3. **Distribute a single shared pair of electrons** between every adjacent bonded pair of atoms to form single skeletal bonds.
4. **Distribute the remaining electron pairs** to satisfy the duplet of hydrogen and the octet of electronegative terminal atoms first.
5. **Form multiple bonds (double/triple bonds)** if the central atom lacks an octet by shifting unshared pairs (lone pairs) from neighboring atoms.

***

### 1.4 Formal Charge: Quantitative Evaluation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
In polyatomic ions and molecules, the net charge is possessed by the ion as a whole and not by an individual atom. However, for bookkeeping purposes and to determine the lowest-energy (most stable) Lewis structure, a **formal charge (F.C.)** is assigned to each individual atom.

#### Definition & Governing Formula:
Formal charge is the difference between the number of valence electrons in an isolated (free) neutral atom and the number of electrons assigned to that atom in a Lewis structure.

$$
\boxed{\text{F.C.} = V - L - \frac{1}{2}S}
$$

* $V = \text{Total number of valence electrons in the free neutral atom}$
* $L = \text{Total number of non-bonding electrons (lone pair electrons)}$
* $S = \text{Total number of shared bonding electrons (bonding pair electrons)}$

#### Rigorous Demonstration 1: Ozone ($\text{O}_3$)
Total valence electrons = $3 \times 6 = 18\ e^-$.  
Skeletal connectivity: $\text{O}_1 - \text{O}_2 = \text{O}_3$ or written as:

$$
\ddot{\text{O}}_1 = \dot{\text{O}}_2^+ - \ddot{\text{O}}_3:^-
$$

* **For Central Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$):**
  * $V = 6$
  * $L = 2$ (one lone pair)
  * $S = 6$ (three covalent bonds: one double bond, one single bond)
  

$$
\text{F.C.}(\text{O}_2) = 6 - 2 - \frac{1}{2}(6) = +1
$$

* **For Double-bonded Terminal Oxygen ($\text{O}_1$):**
  * $V = 6$
  * $L = 4$ (two lone pairs)
  * $S = 4$ (two bonds)
  

$$
\text{F.C.}(\text{O}_1) = 6 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = 0
$$

* **For Single-bonded Terminal Oxygen ($\text{O}_3$):**
  * $V = 6$
  * $L = 6$ (three lone pairs)
  * $S = 2$ (one single bond)
  

$$
\text{F.C.}(\text{O}_3) = 6 - 6 - \frac{1}{2}(2) = -1
$$

* **Check:** Sum of formal charges = $0 + 1 + (-1) = 0$ (matches neutral $\text{O}_3$).

#### Rigorous Demonstration 2: Carbonate Ion ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$)
Total valence electrons = $4(\text{C}) + 3 \times 6(\text{O}) + 2(\text{charge}) = 24\ e^-$.
* Central Carbon forms one double bond with one Oxygen ($\text{O}_a$) and single bonds with two Oxygens ($\text{O}_b, \text{O}_c$).
* $\text{F.C.}(\text{C}) = 4 - 0 - \frac{1}{2}(8) = 0$
* $\text{F.C.}(\text{O}_a) = 6 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = 0$
* $\text{F.C.}(\text{O}_b) = \text{F.C.}(\text{O}_c) = 6 - 6 - \frac{1}{2}(2) = -1$
* **Check:** $\sum \text{F.C.} = 0 + 0 + (-1) + (-1) = -2$.

### 1.5 Limitations and Failures of the Octet Rule ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Despite its wide utility, the Octet Rule is not universal and breaks down across three distinct classes of stable chemical species:

#### 1. Incomplete Octet of the Central Atom (Hypovalent Molecules / Electron-Deficient Species)
Elements of Groups 1, 2, and 13 having fewer than four valence electrons form stable compounds wherein the central atom possesses fewer than eight electrons:
* $\text{LiCl}$: $\text{Li}$ shares an electron pair with $\text{Cl}$ ($2\ e^-$ around $\text{Li}$).
* $\text{BeH}_2, \text{BeCl}_2(\text{g})$: Beryllium possesses only $4$ valence electrons.
* $\text{BCl}_3, \text{BF}_3, \text{AlCl}_3$: Boron/Aluminium possesses only $6$ valence electrons ($3$ bond pairs, $0$ lone pairs).

#### 2. Odd-Electron Molecules
Molecules containing an odd total count of valence electrons cannot satisfy the octet rule for all constituent atoms:
* **Nitric Oxide ($\text{NO}$):** Total valence electrons = $5(\text{N}) + 6(\text{O}) = 11\ e^-$.
  

$$
\dot{\text{N}} = \ddot{\text{O}} \quad (\text{Nitrogen has only } 7\ e^-)
$$

* **Nitrogen Dioxide ($\text{NO}_2$):** Total valence electrons = $5(\text{N}) + 2 \times 6(\text{O}) = 17\ e^-$. Nitrogen retains a solitary unpaired electron. Paramagnetic in nature; readily dimerizes to diamagnetic $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$ ($17 + 17 = 34\ e^-$) to achieve octet stability.

#### 3. Expanded Octet (Hypervalent Molecules / Super-Octet Species)
Elements belonging to Period 3 and beyond possess unfilled $d$-orbitals ($3d, 4d$, etc.) in addition to $s$ and $p$ orbitals. Consequently, they can accommodate more than eight electrons around the central atom:
* $\text{PF}_5$: Phosphorus is surrounded by $5$ single bonds $\implies 10$ valence electrons.
* $\text{SF}_6$: Sulfur is surrounded by $6$ single bonds $\implies 12$ valence electrons.
* $\text{IF}_7$: Iodine is surrounded by $7$ single bonds $\implies 14$ valence electrons.
* $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$: Sulfur forms four $\sigma$-bonds and two $\pi$-bonds $\implies 12$ valence electrons.

#### 4. Additional Drawbacks of the Octet Theory
* **Inert Gas Reactivity:** The octet rule presumes noble gases are completely inert. However, heavier noble gases like Xenon and Krypton form stable fluorides and oxides ($\text{XeF}_2, \text{XeF}_4, \text{XeF}_6, \text{XeOF}_4, \text{KrF}_2$).
* **Geometry:** The octet model cannot predict the shape, bond angles, or three-dimensional stereochemistry of molecules.
* **Thermodynamics:** It provides no quantitative assessment of bond dissociation energy or molecular stability.

## 2. Ionic (Electrovalent) Bonding

### 2.1 Thermodynamic Prerequisites for Ionic Bond Formation ⭐⭐⭐⭐
An ionic bond is defined as the electrostatic force of attraction holding together oppositely charged ions formed by the complete transfer of one or more electrons from an electropositive atom to an electronegative atom.

The formation of an ionic solid $M^+X^-(\text{s})$ from its neutral gaseous constituents involves three energetic factors:

1. **Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H^\circ$):**
   

$$
M(\text{g}) \longrightarrow M^+(\text{g}) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H^\circ > 0, \text{ Endothermic})
$$

   * *Criterion:* Lower ionization enthalpy of the metallic element facilitates rapid cation formation.

2. **Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg} H^\circ$):**
   

$$
X(\text{g}) + e^- \longrightarrow X^-(\text{g}) \quad (\Delta_{eg} H^\circ < 0, \text{ Exothermic for halogens})
$$

   * *Criterion:* More negative electron gain enthalpy of the non-metallic element favors anion formation.

3. **Lattice Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ$):**
   

$$
M^+(\text{g}) + X^-(\text{g}) \longrightarrow MX(\text{s}) \quad (\Delta H < 0, \text{ Highly Exothermic})
$$

   * *Criterion:* Maximally negative energy release upon crystal packing stabilizes the ionic compound.

$$
\boxed{\text{Thermodynamic Condition for Spontaneous Ionic Bonding: } |\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ| + |\Delta_{eg} H^\circ| > \Delta_i H^\circ}
$$

> **NCERT Key Concept:** A stable ionic crystal can form even if the ionization enthalpy is positive and numerically larger than the electron gain enthalpy, provided the magnitude of the **lattice enthalpy** is sufficiently high to render the overall net enthalpy change ($\Delta_f H^\circ$) negative.

## 3. Lattice Enthalpy and The Born-Haber Cycle

### 3.1 Strict IUPAC Definition ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
> **Lattice Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ$):** The enthalpy change required to completely separate one mole of a solid ionic compound into its constituent gaseous ions to an infinite distance apart.

$$
MX(\text{s}) \longrightarrow M^+(\text{g}) + X^-(\text{g}) \quad (\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ > 0, \text{ Endothermic by dissociation convention})
$$

*(Note: Lattice Formation Enthalpy is the reverse process, where $\Delta H_{\text{formation}} = -\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ < 0$. Always verify whether the question specifies dissociation or formation).*

***

### 3.2 Step-by-Step Derivation: Born-Haber Cycle for $\text{NaCl}(\text{s})$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Because lattice enthalpy cannot be measured directly by experiment, it is calculated indirectly using **Hess’s Law of Constant Heat Summation** via the **Born-Haber Cycle**.

```
                   Δf H°
        Na(s) + 1/2 Cl2(g) --------> NaCl(s)
          |           |                 ^
   ΔsubH° |           | 1/2 ΔbondH°     |
          v           v                 | -Δ_lattice H°
        Na(g)       Cl(g)               |
          |           |                 |
   Δi H°  |           | ΔegH°           |
          v           v                 |
        Na+(g)  +   Cl-(g) -------------+
```

#### Stepwise Thermodynamic Breakdown:
1. **Sublimation of solid sodium:**
   

$$
\text{Na}(\text{s}) \longrightarrow \text{Na}(\text{g}) \quad [\Delta_{\text{sub}} H^\circ]
$$

2. **Ionization of gaseous sodium atoms:**
   

$$
\text{Na}(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{Na}^+(\text{g}) + e^- \quad [\Delta_i H^\circ]
$$

3. **Dissociation of chlorine molecules:**
   

$$
\frac{1}{2}\text{Cl}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{Cl}(\text{g}) \quad \left[\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\circ\right]
$$

4. **Electron gain by gaseous chlorine atoms:**
   

$$
\text{Cl}(\text{g}) + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cl}^-(\text{g}) \quad [\Delta_{eg} H^\circ]
$$

5. **Condensation into ionic crystal lattice:**
   

$$
\text{Na}^+(\text{g}) + \text{Cl}^-(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(\text{s}) \quad [-\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ]
$$

#### Direct Path:
Standard Enthalpy of Formation:

$$
\text{Na}(\text{s}) + \frac{1}{2}\text{Cl}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(\text{s}) \quad [\Delta_f H^\circ]
$$

#### Applying Hess's Law:

$$
\Delta_f H^\circ = \Delta_{\text{sub}} H^\circ + \Delta_i H^\circ + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\circ + \Delta_{eg} H^\circ - \Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ
$$

Rearranging to isolate the **Lattice Dissociation Enthalpy**:

$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ = \Delta_{\text{sub}} H^\circ + \Delta_i H^\circ + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\circ + \Delta_{eg} H^\circ - \Delta_f H^\circ}
$$

***

### 3.3 Quantitative Factors Influencing Lattice Enthalpy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Electrostatic potential energy of an ionic lattice can be modeled by the **Born-Landé Equation**:

$$
U_0 = -\frac{N_A M z^+ z^- e^2}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r_0} \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)
$$

* $N_A = \text{Avogadro's constant}$
* $M = \text{Madelung constant (dependent on crystal geometry)}$
* $z^+, z^- = \text{Magnitudes of ionic charges}$
* $r_0 = r_+ + r_- = \text{Equilibrium internuclear distance}$
* $n = \text{Born exponent (repulsion factor, typically between } 5 \text{ and } 12)$

#### Operational Rules for Exam Problems:
1. **Charge Factor ($z^+ \cdot z^-$):** $\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ \propto |z^+ \cdot z^-|$. Higher product of ionic charges creates dramatically greater electrostatic attraction. (Dominant factor).
   

$$
\text{Lattice Enthalpy: } \text{MgO} (|(+2)(-2)| = 4) \gg \text{NaCl} (|(+1)(-1)| = 1)
$$

2. **Size Factor ($r_0 = r_+ + r_-$):** $\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ \propto \frac{1}{r_+ + r_-}$. Smaller internuclear separation produces stronger electrostatic binding.
   

$$
\text{Lattice Enthalpy: } \text{LiF} > \text{LiCl} > \text{LiBr} > \text{LiI}
$$

## 4. Bond Parameters

### 4.1 Bond Length ⭐⭐⭐⭐
> **Definition:** The equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms in a molecule.

#### Components:
1. **Covalent Radius ($r_{\text{cov}}$):** Measured approximately as half of the internuclear distance between two identically bonded atoms in a homonuclear diatomic molecule:
   

$$
r_{\text{cov}} = \frac{d_{A-A}}{2}
$$

   For a heteronuclear molecule $A-B$ with electronegativity difference $\Delta \chi = |\chi_A - \chi_B|$, the **Stevenson-Schomaker Equation** gives:
   

$$
d_{A-B} = r_A + r_B - 0.09\,|\chi_A - \chi_B| \quad (\text{in Å})
$$

2. **van der Waals Radius ($r_{\text{vdW}}$):** Represents half of the distance between two non-bonded atoms of adjacent molecules in the solid state.
   

$$
\boxed{r_{\text{vdW}} > r_{\text{cov}}}
$$

   *Physical Reason:* van der Waals forces are weak dispersion interactions without electron orbital overlap, whereas covalent bonds involve physical interpenetration and overlap of atomic orbitals.

```
      |<------- Covalent Diameter ------->|
      (    (  Nucleus A  ) (  Nucleus B  )    )
           \------- r_cov -------/

      |<---------------- van der Waals Diameter ---------------->|
      ( Molecule 1 : Atom A )     [Void]     ( Molecule 2 : Atom A' )
           \---------------------- r_vdW ----------------------/
```

#### Trends:
* **Multiplicity:** Bond length decreases as bond order increases:
  

$$
d_{\text{C}\equiv\text{C}}\,(120\text{ pm}) < d_{\text{C}=\text{C}}\,(134\text{ pm}) < d_{\text{C}-\text{C}}\,(154\text{ pm})
$$

* **Size:** Increases down a group:
  

$$
\text{H}-\text{F}\,(92\text{ pm}) < \text{H}-\text{Cl}\,(127\text{ pm}) < \text{H}-\text{Br}\,(141\text{ pm}) < \text{H}-\text{I}\,(160\text{ pm})
$$

***

### 4.2 Bond Angle ⭐⭐⭐
> **Definition:** The angle subtended between the orbitals containing bonding electron pairs around the central atom in a molecule/complex ion.

* Expressed in degrees, minutes, and seconds ($^\circ, \,', \,''$).
* Determined experimentally via X-ray crystallography, neutron diffraction, and spectroscopic methods.
* **Classic Examples:**
  * $\text{CH}_4$: Regular Tetrahedron $\implies 109^\circ 28'$
  * $\text{NH}_3$: Trigonal Pyramidal ($1$ lone pair) $\implies 107^\circ$
  * $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: Bent/V-shaped ($2$ lone pairs) $\implies 104.5^\circ$

***

### 4.3 Bond Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\circ$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
> **Definition:** The amount of energy required to break one mole of bonds of a particular type between two atoms in a gaseous state.

#### 1. Diatomic Molecules:
In diatomic molecules, bond dissociation enthalpy equals bond enthalpy:

$$
\text{H}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow 2\text{H}(\text{g}) \quad \Delta_{\text{H}-\text{H}} H^\circ = +435.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\text{O}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow 2\text{O}(\text{g}) \quad \Delta_{\text{O}=\text{O}} H^\circ = +498\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\text{N}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow 2\text{N}(\text{g}) \quad \Delta_{\text{N}\equiv\text{N}} H^\circ = +946.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

#### 2. Polyatomic Molecules:
In polyatomic molecules, the energy required to break identical chemical bonds depends on the molecular environment. Thus, we use the **Mean (Average) Bond Enthalpy**.

##### Case Study: Dissociation of Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)

$$
\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{H}(\text{g}) + \text{OH}(\text{g}) \quad \Delta_a H_1^\circ = +502\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\text{OH}(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{H}(\text{g}) + \text{O}(\text{g}) \quad \Delta_a H_2^\circ = +427\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

Because the chemical environment changes after cleavage of the first $\text{O}-\text{H}$ bond, $\Delta_a H_1^\circ \ne \Delta_a H_2^\circ$.

$$
\boxed{\text{Mean Bond Enthalpy of } \text{O}-\text{H} = \frac{\Delta_a H_1^\circ + \Delta_a H_2^\circ}{2} = \frac{502 + 427}{2} = +464.5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}
$$

***

### 4.4 Bond Order ⭐⭐⭐⭐
> **Definition:** In the Lewis description of a covalent bond, the Bond Order is given by the number of shared electron pairs between two bonded atoms.

* Single bond ($\text{H}_2, \text{Cl}_2$): $\text{Bond Order} = 1$
* Double bond ($\text{O}_2, \text{CO}_2$): $\text{Bond Order} = 2$
* Triple bond ($\text{N}_2, \text{CO}$): $\text{Bond Order} = 3$
* **Isoelectronic Species Rule:** Molecules or ions having identical numbers of electrons exhibit identical bond orders:
  * $\text{N}_2\,(14\ e^-), \, \text{CO}\,(14\ e^-), \, \text{NO}^+\,(14\ e^-), \, \text{CN}^-\,(14\ e^-) \implies \text{Bond Order} = 3$
  * $\text{O}_2^{2-}(18\ e^-), \, \text{F}_2\,(18\ e^-) \implies \text{Bond Order} = 1$

#### Master Correlation of Bond Parameters:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bond Order} \propto \text{Bond Enthalpy} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Bond Length}} \propto \text{Stability}}
$$

***

### 4.5 Resonance Structures ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Often, a single Lewis structure cannot adequately explain all experimentally observed properties of a molecule. In such cases, the molecule is represented by two or more contributing canonical (resonance) structures. The actual molecule is a **resonance hybrid** of these canonical forms.

#### Thermodynamic Criteria:
1. **Resonance Hybrid:** The actual molecule does not oscillate between canonical structures; it has a single, static electronic structure that is an unweighted/weighted quantum mechanical superposition.
2. **Resonance Energy:** The difference in energy between the actual resonance hybrid and the most stable canonical structure:
   

$$
\boxed{E_{\text{resonance}} = E_{\text{hybrid}} - E_{\text{most stable canonical structure}} < 0}
$$

#### Classic Case Study 1: Ozone ($\text{O}_3$)
* Experimentally, both $\text{O}-\text{O}$ bonds in ozone are completely identical:
  

$$
d_{\text{O}-\text{O}} = 128\text{ pm}
$$

* Pure single bond: $d_{\text{O}-\text{O}} = 148\text{ pm}$
* Pure double bond: $d_{\text{O}=\text{O}} = 121\text{ pm}$
* Since $121\text{ pm} < 128\text{ pm} < 148\text{ pm}$, both bonds have a partial double bond character:
  

$$
\text{Bond Order} = \frac{\text{Total number of bonding pairs participating}}{\text{Total number of resonating positions}} = \frac{1 + 2}{2} = 1.5
$$

#### Classic Case Study 2: Carbonate Ion ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$)
All three $\text{C}-\text{O}$ bonds are identical with an intermediate bond length ($129\text{ pm}$), between a $\text{C}=\text{O}$ double bond ($122\text{ pm}$) and a $\text{C}-\text{O}$ single bond ($143\text{ pm}$).

$$
\text{Bond Order}(\text{C}-\text{O}) = \frac{\text{Total bonds between C and O}}{\text{Number of oxygen atoms}} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33
$$

## 5. Solved Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

### Problem 1: Born-Haber Cycle Calculation (Rigorous Numerical)
**Question:** Calculate the standard lattice enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ$) of magnesium chloride ($\text{MgCl}_2(\text{s})$) from the following thermochemical data:
* $\Delta_f H^\circ [\text{MgCl}_2(\text{s})] = -641.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
* Sublimation enthalpy of $\text{Mg}$: $\Delta_{\text{sub}} H^\circ = +147.7\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
* First ionization enthalpy of $\text{Mg}$: $\Delta_{i1} H^\circ = +737.7\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
* Second ionization enthalpy of $\text{Mg}$: $\Delta_{i2} H^\circ = +1450.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
* Bond dissociation enthalpy of $\text{Cl}_2(\text{g})$: $\Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\circ = +242.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
* Electron gain enthalpy of $\text{Cl}(\text{g})$: $\Delta_{eg} H^\circ = -348.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$

#### Solution:
Write out the thermochemical steps:
1. $\text{Mg}(\text{s}) \longrightarrow \text{Mg}(\text{g}) \quad \Delta H_1 = +147.7\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
2. $\text{Mg}(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+}(\text{g}) + 2e^- \quad \Delta H_2 = \Delta_{i1} H^\circ + \Delta_{i2} H^\circ = 737.7 + 1450.6 = +2188.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
3. $\text{Cl}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow 2\text{Cl}(\text{g}) \quad \Delta H_3 = \Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\circ = +242.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$ (full bond broken to yield $2\text{Cl}$)
4. $2\text{Cl}(\text{g}) + 2e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Cl}^-(\text{g}) \quad \Delta H_4 = 2 \times \Delta_{eg} H^\circ = 2(-348.6) = -697.2\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$
5. $\text{Mg}^{2+}(\text{g}) + 2\text{Cl}^-(\text{g}) \longrightarrow \text{MgCl}_2(\text{s}) \quad \Delta H_5 = -\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ$

By Hess's Law:

$$
\Delta_f H^\circ = \Delta_{\text{sub}} H^\circ + (\Delta_{i1} H^\circ + \Delta_{i2} H^\circ) + \Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\circ + 2\Delta_{eg} H^\circ - \Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ
$$

Substitute the values:

$$
-641.6 = 147.7 + 2188.3 + 242.6 + (-697.2) - \Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ
$$

$$
-641.6 = 1881.4 - \Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ
$$

$$
\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ = 1881.4 - (-641.6) = +2523.0\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\text{lattice}} H^\circ(\text{MgCl}_2) = +2523\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}
$$

### Problem 2: Formal Charge Analysis
**Question:** Draw the Lewis structure of the nitrite ion ($\text{NO}_2^-$) and calculate the formal charge on each atom.

#### Solution:
1. **Total valence electrons:**
   

$$
V_{\text{total}} = 5(\text{N}) + 2 \times 6(\text{O}) + 1(\text{charge}) = 18\ e^-
$$

2. **Lewis Structure:**
   

$$
[:\ddot{\text{O}}_A = \ddot{\text{N}} - \ddot{\text{O}}_B:]^-
$$

3. **Formal Charge Calculations:**
   * **Nitrogen:**
     

$$
V = 5, \quad L = 2, \quad S = 6 \implies \text{F.C.} = 5 - 2 - \frac{1}{2}(6) = 0
$$

   * **Oxygen $A$ (double-bonded):**
     

$$
V = 6, \quad L = 4, \quad S = 4 \implies \text{F.C.} = 6 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = 0
$$

   * **Oxygen $B$ (single-bonded):**
     

$$
V = 6, \quad L = 6, \quad S = 2 \implies \text{F.C.} = 6 - 6 - \frac{1}{2}(2) = -1
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{F.C.}(\text{N}) = 0, \quad \text{F.C.}(\text{O}_A) = 0, \quad \text{F.C.}(\text{O}_B) = -1}
$$

### Problem 3: Lattice Enthalpy and Melting Point Order
**Question:** Arrange the following sets in increasing order of lattice enthalpy and justify based on ionic parameters:  
(a) $\text{NaF}, \text{NaCl}, \text{NaBr}, \text{NaI}$  
(b) $\text{NaCl}, \text{MgCl}_2, \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{MgO}$

#### Solution:
**(a)** Cation is constant ($\text{Na}^+$). Anions belong to Group 17:

$$
\text{Ionic radius: } r(\text{F}^-) < r(\text{Cl}^-) < r(\text{Br}^-) < r(\text{I}^-)
$$

Since lattice enthalpy is inversely proportional to internuclear distance ($r_0 = r_+ + r_-$):

$$
\boxed{\text{NaI} < \text{NaBr} < \text{NaCl} < \text{NaF}}
$$

**(b)** Analyze charge product $|z^+ z^-|$:
* $\text{NaCl}$: $|(+1)(-1)| = 1$
* $\text{MgCl}_2$: $|(+2)(-1)| = 2$
* $\text{MgO}$: $|(+2)(-2)| = 4$
* $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$: $|(+3)(-2)| = 6$

Because electrostatic charge has a larger impact on lattice energy than ionic radii differences:

$$
\boxed{\text{NaCl} < \text{MgCl}_2 < \text{MgO} < \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3}
$$

## 7. Speed Hacks & Golden Rules

### Quick Calculation Shortcuts:
1. **Direct Resonance Bond Order Shortcut:**
   

$$
\text{Bond Order} = 1 + \frac{\text{Number of } \pi\text{-bonds}}{\text{Number of resonating } \sigma\text{-positions}}
$$

   * For $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$: $1 + \frac{1}{3} = 1.33$
   * For $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$: $1 + \frac{2}{4} = 1.50$
   * For $\text{ClO}_4^-$: $1 + \frac{3}{4} = 1.75$
   * For $\text{PO}_4^{3-}$: $1 + \frac{1}{4} = 1.25$

2. **Odd-Electron Molecule Identification:**
   Count total valence electrons ($V_{\text{total}}$). If $V_{\text{total}}$ is an odd number, the molecule:
   * Must violate the octet rule.
   * Is paramagnetic due to an unpaired electron.
   * Typically dimerizes at lower temperatures (e.g., $2\text{NO}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{N}_2\text{O}_4$).

3. **Lattice Energy Dominance Rule:**
   

$$
\text{Product of Charges } |z^+ z^-| \gg \text{Sum of Radii } (r_+ + r_-)
$$

   Always evaluate the product of ionic charges first. Only compare ionic radii when charge products are identical (e.g., compare $\text{NaCl}$ with $\text{KCl}$, where both have $|z^+ z^-| = 1$).
