### 1.1 Definition & Position in the Periodic Table
**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Definitional Criterion (NCERT Standard)
A **transition element** is defined as an element which has an **incompletely filled $(n-1)d$ subshell** either in its ground state or in any of its common (stable) oxidation states.

$$
\text{Criterion: } (n-1)d^{1 \text{ to } 9} \quad (\text{in atom or stable ion})
$$

#### 2. Position in the Modern Periodic Table
* **Block:** $d$-block (flanked between electropositive $s$-block and electronegative $p$-block elements).
* **Groups:** Group 3 through Group 12 ($10$ vertical columns).
* **Periods:** Period 4 ($3d$-series), Period 5 ($4d$-series), Period 6 ($5d$-series), and Period 7 ($6d$-series, incomplete/actinoid related).
* **Name Origin:** The term "transition" was originally applied because their chemical and physical characteristics represent a transitional bridge between the highly electropositive $s$-block alkali/alkaline earth metals and the highly electronegative $p$-block reactive non-metals.

#### 3. The Non-Typical Transition Elements: $\mathrm{Zn}$, $\mathrm{Cd}$, $\mathrm{Hg}$
* Electronic configuration of Group 12:
  

$$
\mathrm{Zn}\,(Z=30): [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}\,4s^2 \implies \mathrm{Zn}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Cd}\,(Z=48): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10}\,5s^2 \implies \mathrm{Cd}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10}
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Hg}\,(Z=80): [\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14}\,5d^{10}\,6s^2 \implies \mathrm{Hg}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14}\,5d^{10}
$$

* **Verdict:** Because their $d$-subshell remains completely filled ($d^{10}$) both in their ground elemental state and in their common stable $+2$ oxidation state, $\mathrm{Zn}$, $\mathrm{Cd}$, and $\mathrm{Hg}$ are **not regarded as transition metals** under the strict definition. However, they are studied together with $d$-block elements because their chemistry forms an extension of the general trends.

#### 4. The Case of Copper ($\mathrm{Cu}$), Silver ($\mathrm{Ag}$), and Gold ($\mathrm{Au}$) (Group 11)
* Although ground state configurations are $d^{10}$:
  

$$
\mathrm{Cu}\,(Z=29): [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}\,4s^1
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Ag}\,(Z=47): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10}\,5s^1
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Au}\,(Z=79): [\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14}\,5d^{10}\,6s^1
$$

* They form stable ions with partly filled $d$-orbitals:
  

$$
\mathrm{Cu}^{2+} \implies [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^9 \quad (d\text{-subshell incomplete})
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Ag}^{2+} \implies [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^9 \quad (\text{e.g., in }\mathrm{AgF}_2)
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{Au}^{3+} \implies [\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14}\,5d^8
$$

* **Verdict:** $\mathrm{Cu}$, $\mathrm{Ag}$, and $\mathrm{Au}$ **are transition elements**.

**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$
(n-1)d^{1-10}\,ns^{1-2}
$$

where:
* $n = 4$ for the first transition series ($3d$-series).
* $(n-1) = 3$ (the penultimate shell).

#### 3. Thermodynamic Justification for Anomalous Configurations of $\mathrm{Cr}$ and $\mathrm{Cu}$
The anomalies in $\mathrm{Cr}$ ($3d^5\,4s^1$ instead of $3d^4\,4s^2$) and $\mathrm{Cu}$ ($3d^{10}\,4s^1$ instead of $3d^9\,4s^2$) are governed by two principal quantum-mechanical energetic components:

##### A. Symmetrical Distribution of Electron Density
Half-filled ($d^5$) and completely filled ($d^{10}$) subshells possess spherical symmetry. Symmetrical charge distribution minimizes inter-electronic Coulombic repulsions within the degenerate set of five $d$-orbitals, providing extra stability.

##### B. Maximum Exchange Energy ($\Delta H_{\mathrm{ex}}$)
Electrons with parallel spins in degenerate orbitals can continuously exchange their positions. Each possible pairwise exchange releases a stabilization energy known as the **Exchange Energy**.
* For $n$ electrons of parallel spin, the total number of possible exchange pairs ($K$) is given by:
  

$$
K = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_2
$$

###### Case 1: Chromium ($\mathrm{Cr}$)
* If configuration were $3d^4\,4s^2$:
  

$$
K_{d^4} = \frac{4(4-1)}{2} = \frac{12}{2} = 6 \text{ exchanges}
$$

* For actual configuration $3d^5\,4s^1$:
  

$$
K_{d^5} = \frac{5(5-1)}{2} = \frac{20}{2} = 10 \text{ exchanges}
$$

* Net gain $= 10 - 6 = 4$ additional exchanges. The large magnitude of released exchange energy far exceeds the small energy required to promote an electron from $4s$ to $3d$ ($\Delta E_{4s \to 3d} \approx 0$ due to close energy proximity).

###### Case 2: Copper ($\mathrm{Cu}$)
* In $3d^{10}$, there are two sets of $5$ electrons with mutually opposing spins ($\alpha$ spin and $\beta$ spin):
  

$$
K_{\mathrm{total}} = K_{\text{spin-up}} + K_{\text{spin-down}} = \frac{5(4)}{2} + \frac{5(4)}{2} = 10 + 10 = 20 \text{ exchanges}
$$

* For $3d^9\,4s^2$:
  

$$
K_{\text{spin-up}} = \frac{5(4)}{2} = 10; \quad K_{\text{spin-down}} = \frac{4(3)}{2} = 6 \implies K_{\mathrm{total}} = 16 \text{ exchanges}
$$

* Net gain $= 20 - 16 = 4$ additional exchanges, stabilizing $3d^{10}\,4s^1$.

### 1.3 Atomic and Ionic Radii Trends & Lanthanoid Contraction
**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

```
Atomic Radius Trend across 3d Series:
   Sc (164 pm) 
      \
       Ti > V > Cr > Mn (Decreasing: Z_eff dominates)
                         \
                          Fe ≈ Co ≈ Ni (Nearly Constant: Z_eff ≈ Shielding)
                                       \
                                        Cu < Zn (Slight Increase: e-e repulsion)
```

#### 1. Across a Period ($3d$-Series: $\mathrm{Sc} \to \mathrm{Zn}$)
The atomic radii variation across the $3d$-series exhibits three distinct zones governed by the interplay between **Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$)** and the **Screening/Shielding Effect ($\sigma$)** of the intervening penultimate $(n-1)d$ electrons:

$$
Z_{\mathrm{eff}} = Z - \sigma
$$

1. **Scandium to Manganese (Decrease in Radius):**
   * As $Z$ increases by $+1$ unit per step, the differentiating electron enters the penultimate $3d$ orbital.
   * Diffuse $3d$ orbitals exhibit poor screening towards outer $4s$ electrons.
   * Rate of increase of nuclear charge exceeds screening:
     

$$
\Delta Z > \Delta \sigma \implies Z_{\mathrm{eff}} \uparrow \implies \text{Atomic/Ionic Radius } \downarrow
$$

2. **Iron to Nickel (Nearly Constant Radius):**
   * Pairing of electrons begins in $3d$ orbitals ($\mathrm{Fe}: 3d^6 \to \mathrm{Ni}: 3d^8$).
   * Increased screening power balances the increase in nuclear charge:
     

$$
\Delta Z_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx \Delta \sigma \implies Z_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx \text{constant} \implies r_{\mathrm{Fe}} \approx r_{\mathrm{Co}} \approx r_{\mathrm{Ni}} \approx 125\text{--}126\text{ pm}
$$

3. **Copper to Zinc (Slight Increase in Radius):**
   * In $\mathrm{Cu}$ ($3d^{10}\,4s^1$) and $\mathrm{Zn}$ ($3d^{10}\,4s^2$), the $3d$-subshell is fully populated with $10$ electrons.
   * Intense inter-electronic repulsions within the crowded $3d^{10}$ subshell overcome the nuclear pull, causing the electron cloud to expand slightly:
     

$$
r_{\mathrm{Zn}} (137\text{ pm}) > r_{\mathrm{Cu}} (128\text{ pm})
$$

#### 2. Down a Group: Lanthanoid Contraction
* **Group 3 ($3d \to 4d \to 5d$):**
  

$$
\mathrm{Sc}\,(164\text{ pm}) \to \mathrm{Y}\,(180\text{ pm}) \to \mathrm{La}\,(187\text{ pm}) \quad (\text{Normal increase due to extra shells})
$$

* **Groups 4 to 12 ($4d \to 5d$):**
  

$$
\text{Group 4: } r(\mathrm{Zr}_{4d}) = 160\text{ pm} \approx r(\mathrm{Hf}_{5d}) = 159\text{ pm}
$$

  

$$
\text{Group 5: } r(\mathrm{Nb}_{4d}) = 146\text{ pm} \approx r(\mathrm{Ta}_{5d}) = 146\text{ pm}
$$

  

$$
\text{Group 6: } r(\mathrm{Mo}_{4d}) = 139\text{ pm} \approx r(\mathrm{W}_{5d}) = 139\text{ pm}
$$

##### Mechanism & Cause of Lanthanoid Contraction
* Between $\mathrm{La}$ ($Z=57$) and $\mathrm{Hf}$ ($Z=72$), the $14$ **lanthanoid elements** ($\mathrm{Ce}$ to $\mathrm{Lu}$) intervene, where filling of the deep $4f$-subshell takes place.
* The $4f$-orbitals possess highly diffuse, complex shapes. Consequently, their screening efficiency is extremely poor:
  

$$
\text{Screening Efficiency: } s > p > d > f
$$

* As nuclear charge surges by $+14$ units with essentially negligible shielding from the $4f^{14}$ core, outer electrons experience an unusually powerful electrostatic attraction:
  

$$
Z_{\mathrm{eff}} \text{ surges dramatically} \implies \text{Progressive contraction of atom}
$$

* **Net Result:** The expected expansion caused by the addition of a principal quantum shell ($n=5 \to n=6$) is canceled out by the **Lanthanoid Contraction**.

##### Chemical Consequences of Lanthanoid Contraction
1. **Identical Radii of $4d$ and $5d$ Congeners:** Pairs like $\mathrm{Zr}/\mathrm{Hf}$, $\mathrm{Nb}/\mathrm{Ta}$, and $\mathrm{Mo}/\mathrm{W}$ are termed **chemical twins**.
2. **Extreme Difficulty in Separation:** Because their covalent/ionic radii, coordination geometries, and electronegativities are identical, their chemical reactivity and solubilities are virtually indistinguishable. They require fractional crystallization or ion-exchange chromatography for separation.
3. **Abnormally High Densities of $5d$ Elements:** Mass doubles ($\Delta M \approx \times 2$) while volume remains roughly constant ($\Delta V \approx 0$).
   

$$
\rho = \frac{M}{V} \implies \rho(5d) \approx 2 \times \rho(4d)
$$

   * For example, $\rho(\mathrm{Os}) = 22.59\text{ g/cm}^3$ and $\rho(\mathrm{Ir}) = 22.61\text{ g/cm}^3$.

### 1.4 Physical Properties
**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Metallic Character & Lattice Structures
* All transition elements are lustrous, malleable, ductile, hard solids with high tensile strength and high thermal/electrical conductivity (except $\mathrm{Hg}$, which is liquid at $298\text{ K}$).
* They crystallize in close-packed metallic structures:
  * **$\text{hcp}$ (hexagonal close-packed):** $\mathrm{Sc}, \mathrm{Ti}, \mathrm{Co}, \mathrm{Zn}$
  * **$\text{ccp/fcc}$ (cubic close-packed):** $\mathrm{Ni}, \mathrm{Cu}$
  * **$\text{bcc}$ (body-centered cubic):** $\mathrm{V}, \mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Mn}, \mathrm{Fe}$

#### 2. Melting and Boiling Points
* **Trend:** Across any transition series, melting point first rises to a maximum around Group 6 ($\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Mo}, \mathrm{W}$) and then falls steadily toward Group 12.
* **Tungsten ($\mathrm{W}$):** Possesses the highest melting point among all metals ($T_{\mathrm{mp}} = 3695\text{ K}$).
* **Mechanism:** Transition metal atoms are held together by a combination of:
  1. Conventional metallic bonding (delocalization of $ns$ electrons).
  2. Covalent bonding due to overlap of unpaired $(n-1)d$ orbitals.
  

$$
\text{Bond Strength} \propto \text{Number of Unpaired } d\text{-Electrons}
$$

* **The Manganese ($\mathrm{Mn}$) and Technetium ($\mathrm{Tc}$) Dip:**
  * $\mathrm{Mn}$ has a stable half-filled $3d^5$ configuration paired with $4s^2$.
  * Its five $d$-electrons are tightly localized and held firmly by the nucleus. Delocalization into the metallic conduction band is hindered, resulting in weaker metallic bonding and an anomalously low melting point ($1519\text{ K}$) compared to $\mathrm{Cr}$ ($2180\text{ K}$) and $\mathrm{Fe}$ ($1811\text{ K}$).

### 2.1 Enthalpy of Atomization ($\Delta_a H^\circ$)
**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Definition
The standard enthalpy of atomization is the enthalpy change accompanying the complete dissociation of one mole of a solid metallic lattice into discrete gaseous atoms:

$$
\mathrm{M}_{(\mathrm{s})} \xrightarrow{\Delta_a H^\circ} \mathrm{M}_{(\mathrm{g})}
$$

```
Number of Unpaired d-Electrons vs Δ_a H°:
d^1 (Sc) -> d^2 (Ti) -> d^3 (V) -> d^5 (Cr: Peak) -> d^5 (Mn: Dip) -> d^6 (Fe) -> ... -> d^10 (Zn: Lowest)
Higher unpaired electrons = stronger covalent metal-metal bonding = higher Δ_a H°
```

#### 2. Underlying Mathematical & Structural Origin
Transition metals possess high enthalpies of atomization due to strong interatomic metallic-covalent bonding involving both $ns$ and $(n-1)d$ electrons:

$$
\Delta_a H^\circ \approx E_{\mathrm{metallic\,(ns)}} + E_{\mathrm{covalent\,((n-1)d)}}
$$

* Because $5d$ elements have larger radial distributions and experience stronger orbital overlap (boosted by Lanthanoid Contraction and relativistic contraction of $6s$), $5d$ elements have higher enthalpies of atomization than their corresponding $3d$ and $4d$ congeners:
  

$$
\Delta_a H^\circ\,(5d) > \Delta_a H^\circ\,(4d) > \Delta_a H^\circ\,(3d)
$$

  * This is why heavy transition metals ($\mathrm{Pt}, \mathrm{Ir}, \mathrm{W}, \mathrm{Au}$) readily form strong direct Metal-Metal ($\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{M}$) bonds (e.g., $\mathrm{W}_2\mathrm{Cl}_8^{4-}$, $\mathrm{Re}_2\mathrm{Cl}_8^{2-}$), whereas $3d$ metals rarely do under standard aqueous conditions.
* **Zinc Exception:**
  

$$
\mathrm{Zn}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}\,4s^2
$$

  No unpaired $d$-electrons are available for interatomic covalent bonding. Metallic bonding involves only the outer $4s^2$ electrons.
  

$$
\Delta_a H^\circ(\mathrm{Zn}) = 126\text{ kJ/mol} \quad (\text{Lowest in } 3d\text{-series; volatile metal})
$$

### 2.2 Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$) Mechanics
**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. General Principles
* As we move along the $3d$-series, the first ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1$) generally increases, but the increase is significantly less steep than in representative $s$- and $p$-block elements.
* **Reason:** The added electron enters an inner shell ($3d$), shielding the outer $4s$ valence electrons from the increasing nuclear charge. Thus, $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ rises gradually.

#### 2. The $4s$ Ionization Sequence Rule
Although the $4s$ orbital has lower energy than $3d$ during Aufbau filling of isolated neutral atoms, the moment electrons populate the $3d$ subshell, the $3d$ electrons experience higher effective nuclear shielding, and the energy of $3d$ drops below $4s$:

$$
E(3d) < E(4s) \quad (\text{for } Z \ge 21)
$$

Therefore, upon ionization, **$4s$ electrons are always removed before $(n-1)d$ electrons**:

$$
\mathrm{M}_{(\mathrm{g})} \xrightarrow{-e^-} \mathrm{M}^+_{(\mathrm{g})} \, (4s^1\,3d^n) \xrightarrow{-e^-} \mathrm{M}^{2+}_{(\mathrm{g})} \, (3d^n)
$$

#### 3. Step-by-Step Mathematical/Energetic Breakdown

```
[M(s)]
   |
   +-- (1) Sublimation: +Δ_a H°
   v
[M(g)]
   |
   +-- (2) First Ionization: +IE_1 (removes 4s electron)
   v
[M+(g)]
   |
   +-- (3) Second Ionization: +IE_2 (removes second 4s electron)
   v
[M2+(g)]
   |
   +-- (4) Third Ionization: +IE_3 (removes 3d electron)
   v
[M3+(g)]
```

##### Step 1: Sublimation & First Ionization

$$
\mathrm{M}_{(\mathrm{s})} \xrightarrow{\Delta_a H^\circ} \mathrm{M}_{(\mathrm{g})} \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_1^\circ} \mathrm{M}^+_{(\mathrm{g})} + e^-
$$

##### Step 2: Second Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_2^\circ$)

$$
\mathrm{M}^+_{(\mathrm{g})} \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_2^\circ} \mathrm{M}^{2+}_{(\mathrm{g})} + e^-
$$

* **Why is $\Delta_i H_2$ for $\mathrm{Cr}$ and $\mathrm{Cu}$ exceptionally high?**
  * $\mathrm{Cr}^+: 3d^5 \xrightarrow{-e^-} \mathrm{Cr}^{2+}: 3d^4$ (Disrupts stable, half-filled $d^5$ subshell).
  * $\mathrm{Cu}^+: 3d^{10} \xrightarrow{-e^-} \mathrm{Cu}^{2+}: 3d^9$ (Disrupts stable, completely filled $d^{10}$ subshell).

##### Step 3: Third Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_3^\circ$)

$$
\mathrm{M}^{2+}_{(\mathrm{g})} \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_3^\circ} \mathrm{M}^{3+}_{(\mathrm{g})} + e^-
$$

* **Why is $\Delta_i H_3$ for $\mathrm{Mn}$ and $\mathrm{Zn}$ extraordinarily high?**
  * $\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 \xrightarrow{-e^-} \mathrm{Mn}^{3+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^4$ (Requires breaking stable $d^5$ half-shell; $\Delta_i H_3 = 3260\text{ kJ/mol}$).
  * $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} \xrightarrow{-e^-} \mathrm{Zn}^{3+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^9$ (Requires removing an electron from a stable filled $d^{10}$ core; $\Delta_i H_3 = 3833\text{ kJ/mol}$).
* **Why is $\Delta_i H_3$ for $\mathrm{Fe}$ markedly low?**
  * $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6 \xrightarrow{-e^-} \mathrm{Fe}^{3+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5$ (Forms the stable half-filled $d^5$ state).
  * Hence, oxidation of $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ to $\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}$ is thermodynamically favorable.

### 2.3 Magnetic Properties: Spin-Only Formula
**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Fundamental Derivation
Paramagnetism originates from the presence of permanent magnetic dipole moments generated by unpaired electrons. The total angular momentum of an electron contains two components:
1. Orbital angular momentum ($L$)
2. Spin angular momentum ($S$)

In the first transition series ($3d$), the $3d$-orbitals are external and interact with the electric fields of surrounding ligands/ions. This interaction quenches the orbital angular momentum ($L \approx 0$).

Thus, the magnetic moment ($\mu$) is determined almost entirely by electron spin:

$$
\mu_s = g\sqrt{S(S+1)} \quad \text{Bohr Magnetons (B.M.)}
$$

where:
* $g \approx 2.0023 \approx 2$ (gyromagnetic ratio of electron spin)
* $S = n \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{n}{2}$ ($n =$ number of unpaired electrons)

Substitute $S = \frac{n}{2}$ and $g = 2$:

$$
\mu_s = 2\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right)} = 2\sqrt{\frac{n(n+2)}{4}} = \frac{2}{2}\sqrt{n(n+2)}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mu_s = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}} \quad (\text{or B.M.})}
$$

$$
\text{SI Definition of Bohr Magneton: } 1\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = \frac{eh}{4\pi m_e} = 9.274 \times 10^{-24}\text{ A}\cdot\text{m}^2 \text{ (or J/T)}
$$

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

### 2.4 Electrode Potential ($\mathrm{E}^\circ_{\mathrm{M}^{2+}/\mathrm{M}}$) Thermochemical Cycle
**Star Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The standard electrode potential represents the redox equilibrium:

$$
\mathrm{M}^{2+}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{M}_{(\mathrm{s})}
$$

By IUPAC convention, the standard potential measures the ease of reduction:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ
$$

The total standard free energy change of the reduction/oxidation process is derived from the **Born-Haber-Fajans Solvation Cycle**:

```
                  ΔH°_total
      M(s) ------------------------> M²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
       |                               ^
       | +Δ_a H°                       |
       v                               | +Δ_hyd H°
      M(g)                             |
       |                               |
       | +[IE₁ + IE₂]                  |
       v                               |
    M²⁺(g) + 2e⁻ ----------------------+
```

$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{total}}^\circ = \Delta_a H^\circ + \Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ + \Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}} H^\circ(\mathrm{M}^{2+})
$$

#### Thermochemical Factors Governing $E^\circ$
1. **Sublimation / Atomization ($\Delta_a H^\circ$):** Always endothermic ($>0$). Requires energy input to break the metal lattice.
2. **Ionization ($\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ$):** Always endothermic ($>0$). Requires energy input to strip valence electrons.
3. **Hydration ($\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}} H^\circ$):** Highly exothermic ($<0$). Releases energy when gaseous cations interact with polar water molecules.

$$
\text{Stability of cation in aqueous solution} \iff \lvert \Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}} H^\circ \rvert > \left(\Delta_a H^\circ + \sum \Delta_i H^\circ\right)
$$

#### The Unique Anomaly of Copper ($E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}/\mathrm{Cu}} = +0.34\text{ V}$)
Copper is the **only metal in the $3d$-series** with a positive $E^\circ_{\mathrm{M}^{2+}/\mathrm{M}}$ value. It cannot reduce $\mathrm{H}^+$ ions from dilute non-oxidizing acids ($\mathrm{HCl}, \text{dil. }\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4$) to liberate hydrogen gas ($\mathrm{H}_2$).

```
Energy balance for Cu(s) -> Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻:
   +Δ_a H°              :  +339 kJ/mol
   +IE₁ + IE₂           : +2703 kJ/mol
   Sum of endothermic   : +3042 kJ/mol
   -Δ_hyd H° (released) : -2121 kJ/mol
   -----------------------------------
   Net ΔH°              :  +921 kJ/mol (Unfavorable / Endothermic!)
Because hydration cannot compensate for high (Δ_a H° + IE₁ + IE₂), Cu prefers Cu(s).
```

* **Quantitative Breakdown:**
  * High Enthalpy of Atomization: $\Delta_a H^\circ(\mathrm{Cu}) = +339\text{ kJ/mol}$
  * High Sum of Ionization Enthalpies: $\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ = +745 + 1958 = +2703\text{ kJ/mol}$
  * Total endothermic energy requirement $= 339 + 2703 = \mathbf{+3042\text{ kJ/mol}}$
  * Hydration Enthalpy released: $\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}} H^\circ(\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}) = \mathbf{-2121\text{ kJ/mol}}$
* **Conclusion:** The hydration enthalpy of $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ is insufficient to compensate for the large sum of its enthalpy of atomization and its first two ionization enthalpies. Hence, $\Delta H_{\mathrm{oxidation}} > 0$, making $E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}/\mathrm{Cu}}$ positive ($+0.34\text{ V}$).

**(a)** Why do zirconium ($\mathrm{Zr}$, $Z=40$) and hafnium ($\mathrm{Hf}$, $Z=72$) exhibit almost identical physical and chemical properties?  
**(b)** Zinc is comparatively soft and has a low melting point ($420^\circ\mathrm{C}$) compared to other transition metals. Give reason.

**(a)**
* $\mathrm{Zr}$ belongs to the $4d$-series, and $\mathrm{Hf}$ belongs to the $5d$-series.
* Between $\mathrm{La}$ ($Z=57$) and $\mathrm{Hf}$ ($Z=72$), the $14$ lanthanoids intervene, where electrons fill the inner $4f$ subshell.

* Consequently, $\mathrm{Zr}$ ($160\text{ pm}$) and $\mathrm{Hf}$ ($159\text{ pm}$) have virtually identical atomic and ionic radii, giving them nearly identical chemical behavior. **[1 Mark]**

* There are **no unpaired electrons available** to form covalent $\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{M}$ bonds across the lattice. Metallic bonding is weak and mediated only by outer $4s^2$ electrons.
* Therefore, the lattice energy is low, making $\mathrm{Zn}$ soft with a low melting point. **[1 Mark]**

### Question 5 [CBSE 2020, 2 Marks]
**(a)** Why are transition elements known to act as effective heterogeneous catalysts? Mention two reasons.  
**(b)** Why is $\mathrm{Sc}^{3+}$ ($Z=21$) diamagnetic and colorless, whereas $\mathrm{Ti}^{3+}$ ($Z=22$) is paramagnetic and purple-colored?

#### Step-by-Step Marking Scheme Solution:
**(a)**
Transition metals serve as effective catalysts due to:
1. **Variable Oxidation States:** They can alternate between multiple oxidation states to form reactive intermediates, providing alternative pathways with lower activation energies (e.g., $\mathrm{V}_2\mathrm{O}_5$ in the Contact Process; $\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}$ in peroxodisulphate-iodide reactions).

\mathrm{Sc}\,(Z=21): [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^1\,4s^2 \implies \mathrm{Sc}^{3+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^0
$$

  Since the $3d$ subshell is empty ($n=0$):
  * There are no unpaired electrons, so it is **diamagnetic** ($\mu_s = 0$).
  * No $d\text{--}d$ electron transitions can occur, making it **colorless**.
* **Titanium:**
  

$$
\mathrm{Ti}\,(Z=22): [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^2\,4s^2 \implies \mathrm{Ti}^{3+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^1
$$

  With one unpaired electron ($n=1$):
  * It is **paramagnetic** ($\mu_s = \sqrt{1(3)} = 1.73\text{ B.M.}$).
  * Absorption of yellow-green light promotes the single $3d$ electron from $t_{2g}$ to $e_g$ ($d\text{--}d$ transition), transmitting purple light. **[1 Mark]**

# SECTION 4: EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

***

### Trap 1: The "Scandium vs Zinc" Transition Status Justification
* **Error:** Stating that "Zinc is not a transition metal because it is unreactive" or "Scandium is a transition metal because it has an unpaired electron in its $\mathrm{Sc}^{3+}$ state."
* **Correction:** State ground state AND oxidation state configurations explicitly:
  * $\mathrm{Sc}$ ($Z=21$): Ground state is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^1\,4s^2$. It is a transition element because its atom contains an **incompletely filled $d$-subshell ($3d^1$)**.
  * $\mathrm{Zn}$ ($Z=30$): Ground state is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}\,4s^2$, and $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}$. It is **not** a transition element because its $d$-subshell is completely filled in both its ground state and common oxidation state.

### Trap 2: Incorrect Electron Stripping Order During Ionization
* **Error:** Writing $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ as $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^4\,4s^2$ by stripping electrons from the inner $3d$ subshell.
* **Correction:** When forming cations, electrons are **always removed from the outermost $ns$ orbital first**:
  

$$
\mathrm{Fe}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6\,4s^2 \xrightarrow{-2e^-} \mathrm{Fe}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6\,4s^0 \xrightarrow{-1e^-} \mathrm{Fe}^{3+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5
$$

### Trap 3: Magnetic Moment Units and Diamagnetic Misidentifications
* **Error:** Omitting units for magnetic moment, or writing $\mathrm{Cu}^+$ as paramagnetic.
* **Correction:**
  * Always append **$\text{B.M.}$** (Bohr Magnetons).
  * Check for $d^{10}$ or $d^0$ configurations:
    

$$
\mathrm{Cu}^+: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} \implies n = 0 \implies \text{Diamagnetic } (\mu_s = 0\text{ B.M.})
$$

    

$$
\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}: [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^9 \implies n = 1 \implies \text{Paramagnetic } (\mu_s = 1.73\text{ B.M.})
$$

### Trap 4: Confusing Atomic Radius Equality Reason
* **Error:** Writing that $\mathrm{Zr}$ and $\mathrm{Hf}$ have identical radii due to "the shielding effect" or "identical valence configurations."
* **Correction:** The specific NCERT keyword is **Lanthanoid Contraction**, caused by the **poor shielding of the intervening $4f^{14}$ electrons**.

### Trap 5: Misinterpreting the Disproportionation of $\mathrm{Cu}^+$
* **Error:** Arguing that $\mathrm{Cu}^+$ is more stable than $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ in water because $\mathrm{Cu}^+$ has a filled $d^{10}$ configuration.
* **Correction:** While $d^{10}$ is stable in dry/crystalline conditions, in aqueous solution the hydration enthalpy of $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ is large enough to overcome the second ionization enthalpy of copper:
  

$$
2\mathrm{Cu}^+_{(\mathrm{aq})} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Cu}^{2+}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{Cu}_{(\mathrm{s})}
$$

## 1. IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & CORE CONCEPTS

***

### Module 1.1: Ionisation Enthalpies ($\Delta_i H$) of Transition Elements
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
**NCERT Core Premise:** Along a transition series ($3d$), as nuclear charge increases ($Z$), the successive electrons enter the penultimate $(n-1)d$ subshell rather than the valence $ns$ subshell.

```
       Nucleus (Z+)  <--- Shielding by (n-1)d electrons ---> Outer ns^2 electrons
```

#### 1. Fundamental Principles & Trends
* **General Variation:** Along the $3d$ series, $\Delta_i H$ increases from left to right, but the increase is **not as steep** as observed in $s$- and $p$-block elements.
* **Underlying Mechanism:** 
  

$$
\Delta Z_{\text{eff}} = \Delta Z - \Delta \sigma
$$

  As protons are added to the nucleus, electrons enter inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals. The screening effect ($\sigma$) of $d$-electrons on valence $ns$ electrons is imperfect ($\sigma_{s} > \sigma_{p} > \sigma_{d} > \sigma_{f}$). Hence, $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases gradually across the period, contracting the radius and pulling the valence $4s$ electrons inward.

#### 2. Fine Structure of Successive Ionisation Enthalpies
1. **First Ionisation Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1$):**
   * Corresponds to the removal of the first electron from the $4s$ orbital:
     

$$
\text{M}(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_1} \text{M}^+(g) + e^-
$$

   * General slight upward trend, with local maxima at $\text{Cr}$ ($3d^5 4s^1$) and $\text{Cu}$ ($3d^{10} 4s^1$) due to extra stability of half-filled and completely-filled $d$-orbitals, and an abrupt peak at $\text{Zn}$ ($3d^{10} 4s^2$) where an electron must be pulled from a stable, completely filled $4s$ orbital.
2. **Second Ionisation Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_2$):**
   * Corresponds to:
     

$$
\text{M}^+(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_2} \text{M}^{2+}(g) + e^-
$$

   * **Anomalously High Values for $\text{Cr}$ and $\text{Cu}$:**
     * $\text{Cr}^+: [Ar]\,3d^5$: Removal of the second electron disrupts the stable half-filled $d^5$ configuration with high exchange energy ($10K$).
     * $\text{Cu}^+: [Ar]\,3d^{10}$: Disruption of the symmetrical, fully filled $d^{10}$ subshell.
   * **Abnormally Low Value for $\text{Zn}$:** $\text{Zn}^+ ([Ar]\,3d^{10} 4s^1) \to \text{Zn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^{10}) + e^-$. The loss of the $4s^1$ electron yields the stable closed-shell $d^{10}$ system.
3. **Third Ionisation Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_3$):**
   * Corresponds to the transition:
     

$$
\text{M}^{2+}(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_3} \text{M}^{3+}(g) + e^-
$$

   * **Very High Values for $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$:**
     * $\text{Mn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^5)$: Extraordinarily stable half-filled configuration.
     * $\text{Zn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^{10})$: Disruption of closed $d^{10}$ shell requires tremendous energy ($3826\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$). Hence, $\text{Zn}^{3+}$ does not exist.
   * **Unusually Low Value for $\text{Fe}^{2+}$:**
     

$$
\text{Fe}^{2+}([Ar]\,3d^6) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_3} \text{Fe}^{3+}([Ar]\,3d^5) + e^-
$$

     Removing the paired electron eliminates inter-electronic repulsion in the $(d_{xy})^2$ orbital and generates a stable $d^5$ half-filled manifold.

### Module 1.2: Oxidation States
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Frequency)  
**NCERT Core Premise:** Transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states because the energy difference between $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ orbitals is minimal ($\Delta E_{(n-1)d - ns} \approx 0$). Electrons from both subshells participate in chemical bonding.

#### 1. Survey of the First Row ($3d$ Series)

| Element | Valence Config. | Common Oxidation States | Maximum OS | Nature of Bonding in Max OS |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sc** | $3d^1 4s^2$ | $+3$ | $+3$ | Purely ionic / coordinate |
| **Ti** | $3d^2 4s^2$ | $+2, +3, \mathbf{+4}$ | $+4$ | Covalent in $\text{TiO}_2, \text{TiCl}_4$ |
| **V** | $3d^3 4s^2$ | $+2, +3, +4, \mathbf{+5}$ | $+5$ | Covalent in $\text{VO}_4^{3-}, \text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ |
| **Cr** | $3d^5 4s^1$ | $+2, \mathbf{+3}, +4, +5, \mathbf{+6}$ | $+6$ | Covalent in $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ |
| **Mn** | $3d^5 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}, +3, \mathbf{+4}, +6, \mathbf{+7}$ | $+7$ | Strongly covalent in $\text{MnO}_4^-$ |
| **Fe** | $3d^6 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}, \mathbf{+3}, +4, +6$ | $+6$ (unstable $\text{FeO}_4^{2-}$) | Intermediate |
| **Co** | $3d^7 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}, \mathbf{+3}$ | $+4$ | Ionic/Coordination |
| **Ni** | $3d^8 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}$ | $+4$ (in complexes like $[\text{NiF}_6]^{2-}$) | Covalent in $+4$ |
| **Cu** | $3d^{10} 4s^1$ | $+1, \mathbf{+2}$ | $+2$ (stable in aq.) | Ionic & covalent |
| **Zn** | $3d^{10} 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}$ | $+2$ | Ionic |

#### 2. Key Pedagogical Laws & Rationales
1. **Maximum Oxidation State Trend:**
   * Increases up to Manganese ($+7$), where all valence electrons ($5 \times 3d + 2 \times 4s = 7$) are available.
   * Decreases past Manganese because $d$-electrons start pairing up in the degenerate $d$-orbitals, lowering their availability for sharing/loss.
2. **Scandium & Zinc Single Oxidation States:**
   * $\text{Sc}$ shows only $+3$ because loss of all 3 valence electrons ($3d^1 4s^2$) yields noble gas core $[Ar]$.
   * $\text{Zn}$ shows only $+2$ because $3d^{10}$ is completely filled and tightly bound ($Z_{\text{eff}}$ is highest).
3. **Stabilisation by Small, Highly Electronegative Ligands ($\text{F}$ and $\text{O}$):**
   * Maximum oxidation states are reached **only** in fluorides and oxides (e.g., $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$, $\text{OsO}_4$, $\text{VF}_5$, $\text{CrF}_6$ does not form, but $\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$ forms).
   * **Fluorine vs. Oxygen:** Oxygen can stabilize higher oxidation states better than fluorine through multiple bond formation ($p\pi\text{--}d\pi$ back-bonding).
     * $\text{Mn}$ forms $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ (OS $= +7$), but the highest fluoride is only $\text{MnF}_4$ (OS $= +4$).
4. **Acidity/Basicity and Covalency Gradient (Fajans' Rule):**
   * **Low Oxidation State ($+2$):** Lower charge-to-radius ratio $\implies$ predominantly basic and ionic (e.g., $\text{MnO}, \text{CrO}$).
   * **Intermediate Oxidation State ($+3, +4$):** Amphoteric (e.g., $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3, \text{MnO}_2, \text{V}_2\text{O}_5$).
   * **High Oxidation State ($+5, +6, +7$):** High polarising power pulls ligand electron clouds $\implies$ predominantly covalent and acidic (e.g., $\text{CrO}_3, \text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$).

***

### Module 1.3: Standard Electrode Potentials ($E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$ and $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{3+}/\text{M}^{2+}}$)
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
**NCERT Core Premise:** The standard reduction potential $E^\circ$ is **not** determined by ionisation enthalpy alone; it is the net thermodynamic resultant of three competing energetic terms in the Born-Haber-Fajans cycle.

#### 1. The Born-Haber Thermodynamic Cycle for $\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}$

$$
\begin{array}{rcccl}
\text{M}(s) & \xrightarrow{\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ} & \text{M}(g) & \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ} & \text{M}^{2+}(g) \\
& \searrow_{\Delta_{\text{red}}G^\circ = -nFE^\circ} & & & \downarrow {\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ} \\
& & & & \text{M}^{2+}(aq)
\end{array}
$$

The total enthalpy of transformation ($\Delta H_{\text{total}}$) is:

$$
\Delta H_{\text{total}} = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ + \Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ
$$

Where:
* $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ > 0$ (Endothermic: Sublimation / Atomisation)
* $\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ > 0$ (Endothermic: Ionisation)
* $\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ < 0$ (Exothermic: Hydration Enthalpy)

A metal will have a **more negative** $E^\circ$ if the exothermic hydration energy exceeds or balances the endothermic sublimation and ionisation inputs.

#### 2. Anomalies in $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$ ($3d$ Series)
* **The Unique Positive Value for Copper ($E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} = +0.34\text{ V}$):**
  * The sum of $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ$ ($+339\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) and the two ionisation energies $\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ$ ($+745 + 1960 = +2705\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) is **not** compensated by the hydration enthalpy $\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ$ of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ($-2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).
  * Net $\Delta H > 0 \implies \Delta G^\circ > 0$ for reduction of $\text{H}^+(aq)$. Thus, copper cannot liberate $\text{H}_2(g)$ from dilute non-oxidising acids like $\text{HCl}$.
* **More Negative $E^\circ$ Peaks at $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$:**
  * $\text{Mn}^{2+}$: Half-filled $d^5$ configuration confers low second ionisation enthalpy relative to third, producing an unusually negative $E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{2+}/\text{Mn}} = -1.18\text{ V}$.
  * $\text{Zn}^{2+}$: Completely filled $d^{10}$ shell makes removal of $4s^2$ favourable and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ stable $\implies E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76\text{ V}$.

#### 3. Trends in $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{3+}/\text{M}^{2+}}$ Redox Couples
1. **$E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}} = +1.57\text{ V}$ (Highly Positive / Strong Oxidising Agent):**
   * $\text{Mn}^{3+} ([Ar]\,3d^4) + e^- \to \text{Mn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^5)$. The transition yields the extraordinarily stable half-filled $d^5$ configuration. Thus, $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ readily reduces itself.
2. **$E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}} = -0.41\text{ V}$ (Negative / Strong Reducing Agent):**
   * In aqueous medium, $d$-orbitals split into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ sub-levels under an octahedral crystal field.
   * $\text{Cr}^{3+} ([Ar]\,3d^3)$ has the half-filled lower-energy manifold $(t_{2g})^3$, providing high crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE). Thus, $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ readily oxidises to $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ ($E^\circ_{\text{ox}} = +0.41\text{ V}$).
3. **$E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}} = +0.77\text{ V}$ (Moderate):**
   * $\text{Fe}^{3+} ([Ar]\,3d^5)$ is stable in the gas phase, but $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ has significant hydration stability. Hence, $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is a mild oxidising agent.

### Module 1.4: Chemical Reactivity & Disproportionation
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
* **Reactivity with Non-oxidising vs Oxidising Acids:**
  * Metals with negative $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$ (Ti to Fe) react with non-oxidising dilute acids ($\text{HCl}, \text{dil. H}_2\text{SO}_4$) to liberate $\text{H}_2(g)$:
    

$$
\text{Fe}(s) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \to \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)
$$

  * Metals with positive $E^\circ$ (Cu) require oxidising acids ($\text{HNO}_3$, conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$):
    

$$
3\text{Cu} + 8\text{HNO}_3(\text{dil.}) \to 3\text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO} \uparrow + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

    

$$
\text{Cu} + 4\text{HNO}_3(\text{conc.}) \to \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO}_2 \uparrow + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Disproportionation of Copper(I) in Aqueous Solution:**
  

$$
\mathbf{2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)}
$$

  * *Thermodynamic Justification:* Although $\text{Cu}^+$ has a stable $d^{10}$ configuration, the divalent $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ion has a much higher charge density ($q/r$). The much larger negative hydration enthalpy of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ \approx -2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) more than compensates for the second ionisation enthalpy of copper ($\Delta_i H_2^\circ = +1960\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).

### Module 1.5: Magnetic Properties
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
**NCERT Core Premise:** The magnetic behaviour of transition metal complexes arises from unpaired electrons in the $(n-1)d$ orbitals.

#### 1. Classification
* **Diamagnetic Substances:** Contain **no** unpaired electrons ($n = 0$). Weakly repelled by an applied magnetic field. Examples: $\text{Sc}^{3+} (d^0)$, $\text{Ti}^{4+} (d^0)$, $\text{Zn}^{2+} (d^{10})$, $\text{Cu}^+ (d^{10})$.
* **Paramagnetic Substances:** Contain one or more unpaired electrons ($n \ge 1$). Attracted into an applied magnetic field. The magnitude of paramagnetism increases directly with the number of unpaired electrons.
* **Ferromagnetic Substances:** Unpaired electron spins align cooperatively in domains, producing permanent macroscopic magnetisation (e.g., solid $\text{Fe, Co, Ni}$).

#### 2. The Spin-Only Magnetic Moment Formula
In the $3d$ series, the orbital angular momentum ($L$) is largely quenched by the electrostatic field of surrounding ligands ("orbital quenching"). Thus, the magnetic moment ($\mu$) is determined almost entirely by electron spin:

$$
\mu_s = \sqrt{n(n + 2)}\quad \text{BM}
$$

Where:
* $n$ = Number of unpaired electrons
* $\text{BM}$ = Bohr Magneton = $\dfrac{eh}{4\pi m_e c} = 9.274 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J T}^{-1}$ (or $\text{A m}^2$)

## 2. DERIVATIONS, THERMODYNAMIC PROOFS & MECHANISMS

### Derivation 2.1: Disproportionation Feasibility of $\text{Cu}^+(aq)$ via Cell Potentials
We evaluate the standard potential for the disproportionation reaction:

$$
2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

Given standard reduction data:
1. $\text{Cu}^+(aq) + e^- \to \text{Cu}(s) \quad E^\circ_1 = +0.52\text{ V}$ (Cathode / Reduction)
2. $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + e^- \to \text{Cu}^+(aq) \quad E^\circ_2 = +0.15\text{ V}$ (Anode / Oxidation is reverse)

#### Step-by-Step Calculation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} &= E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} \\
&= E^\circ_{(\text{Cu}^+/\text{Cu})} - E^\circ_{(\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}^+)} \\
&= (+0.52\text{ V}) - (+0.15\text{ V}) \\
&= \mathbf{+0.37\text{ V}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Using Gibbs Free Energy relation:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}
$$

For $n = 1\text{ mol } e^-$ transferred per mol of reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -(1)(96485\text{ C mol}^{-1})(+0.37\text{ V}) \\
&= -35699\text{ J mol}^{-1} \\
&= \mathbf{-35.70\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta G^\circ = -35.70\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} < 0 \implies \text{Spontaneous Disproportionation in Aqueous Media.}}
$$

***

### Derivation 2.2: Thermodynamic Energy Balance of $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)$ vs $\text{Cu}^+(aq)$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Cu}^+(g) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(g) + e^- & \Delta_i H_2^\circ &= +1960\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Cu}^{2+}(g) + aq \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) & \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{2+}) &= -2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Cu}^{+}(g) + aq \to \text{Cu}^{+}(aq) & \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{+}) &\approx -580\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The net enthalpy difference favoring oxidation of gaseous $\text{Cu}^+$ to aqueous $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ over hydrating $\text{Cu}^+$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H_{\text{net}} &= \Delta_i H_2^\circ + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{2+}) - \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{+}) \\
&= (+1960) + (-2121) - (-580) \\
&= \mathbf{+419\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}} \text{ released (exothermic relative shift)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Hydration enthalpy of }\text{Cu}^{2+}\text{ completely compensates for its high second ionisation enthalpy.}}
$$

### Question 1 [CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
**(a)** Why is $E^\circ$ for $\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}$ couple much more positive than that for $\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}$ or $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$?  
**(b)** Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment of $\text{Co}^{2+}(aq)$ ($Z = 27$).

**Solution:**
* **(a)** 
  1. For $\text{Mn}$: The electronic configuration of $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ is $[Ar]\,3d^5$ (half-filled, symmetrically stable, large exchange energy), whereas $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ is $[Ar]\,3d^4$. Reduction of $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ to $\text{Mn}^{2+}$:
     

$$
\text{Mn}^{3+}(3d^4) + e^- \to \text{Mn}^{2+}(3d^5)
$$

     proceeds with great thermodynamic ease. Thus, $E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}} = +1.57\text{ V}$ is highly positive.
  2. For $\text{Cr}$: In aqueous solution, $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ has $[Ar]\,3d^3$, which corresponds to a stable half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ subshell under octahedral crystal field splitting. Hence, $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ resists reduction, making $E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}}$ negative ($-0.41\text{ V}$).
  3. For $\text{Fe}$: $\text{Fe}^{3+} (3d^5) \to \text{Fe}^{2+} (3d^6)$. $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is already half-filled; hence its reduction does not experience the same driving force as $\text{Mn}^{3+}$.
* **(b)** For Cobalt ($Z = 27$):
  * Ground state atom: $[Ar]\,3d^7 4s^2$
  * Divalent cation $\text{Co}^{2+}$: $[Ar]\,3d^7$
  * Orbital filling:
    

$$
d_{xy}^{\uparrow\downarrow} \quad d_{yz}^{\uparrow\downarrow} \quad d_{zx}^{\uparrow} \quad d_{x^2-y^2}^{\uparrow} \quad d_{z^2}^{\uparrow}
$$

    Number of unpaired electrons $n = 3$.
  * Applying the spin-only formula:
    

$$
\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = \sqrt{3(3+2)} = \sqrt{15}\text{ BM} \approx \mathbf{3.87\text{ BM}}
$$

### Question 2 [CBSE 2023, 2 Marks]
Account for the following:
1. $\text{Cu}^+$ salts are generally colourless and diamagnetic, whereas $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ salts are coloured and paramagnetic.
2. Highest oxidation state of a transition metal is exhibited in its oxides and fluorides only.

**Solution:**
1. **$\text{Cu}^+$ ($Z=29$):** Electronic configuration is $[Ar]\,3d^{10}$. All five $3d$-orbitals are completely paired ($n = 0$). No unpaired electrons are available, so it is **diamagnetic** ($\mu = 0\text{ BM}$). Since $d$-orbitals are completely filled, $d\text{--}d$ electron transitions cannot occur under visible light, rendering it **colourless**.  
   **$\text{Cu}^{2+}$:** Electronic configuration is $[Ar]\,3d^9$. It has **one unpaired electron** ($n = 1$), which makes it **paramagnetic** ($\mu = 1.73\text{ BM}$). It absorbs light from the visible region for $d\text{--}d$ transition (promoting an electron into the vacancy in the $e_g$ set), rendering it **coloured** (characteristic blue in aqueous solution).
2. **Fluorine and Oxygen** are the most electronegative elements with very small ionic radii. They possess high lattice enthalpies (with metal cations) and high bond energies, or allow extensive $p\pi\text{--}d\pi$ multiple bonding (in the case of oxygen), enabling the metal to release all valence electrons without energetic penalty.

### Question 3 [CBSE 2022, Term-II, 3 Marks]
Explain the following observations:
**(a)** $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ is a reducing agent, while $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ is an oxidising agent, though both have $d^4$ configuration.  
**(b)** The lowest oxide of transition metal is basic, whereas the highest oxide is amphoteric or acidic.

**Solution:**
* **(a)** Both $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ have $d^4$ configuration ($[Ar]\,3d^4$).
  * When $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ acts as a reducing agent, it loses an electron to become $\text{Cr}^{3+}$:
    

$$
\text{Cr}^{2+}(d^4) \to \text{Cr}^{3+}(d^3) + e^-
$$

    In aqueous solution, $d^3$ achieves the **stable half-filled $t_{2g}^3$** configuration (due to octahedral crystal field splitting of the $d$-manifold). Hence, oxidation is spontaneous ($E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}} = -0.41\text{ V}$).
  * When $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ acts as an oxidising agent, it gains an electron to become $\text{Mn}^{2+}$:
    

$$
\text{Mn}^{3+}(d^4) + e^- \to \text{Mn}^{2+}(d^5)
$$

    This yields the **stable half-filled $d^5$** configuration, which has high exchange energy. Hence, reduction is spontaneous ($E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}} = +1.57\text{ V}$).
* **(b)** In lower oxidation states (e.g., $\text{MnO}$ where $\text{Mn}$ is $+2$), the metal has a lower charge-to-radius ratio and low electronegativity. It transfers electrons easily to oxygen, forming predominantly **ionic bonds**; hence it readily dissolves/reacts as a **base**.  
  In high oxidation states (e.g., $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ where $\text{Mn}$ is $+7$), the small, highly charged cation has huge polarising power (Fajans' Rule). It pulls electron density away from the oxygen atoms, forming **covalent bonds**. In water, these oxides act as Lewis acids or form strong oxoacids ($\text{HMnO}_4$).

### Question 4 [CBSE 2021, Compartment / Sample Practice, 2 Marks]
Why is the third ionisation enthalpy of Manganese ($Z=25$) anomalously high compared to that of Iron ($Z=26$)?

**Solution:**
* **Manganese ($Z = 25$):** 
  * $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ configuration: $[Ar]\,3d^5$
  * Removal of the third electron requires disrupting the stable, half-filled $3d^5$ subshell, which has maximum exchange energy:
    

$$
\Delta_i H_3(\text{Mn}) = 3252\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

* **Iron ($Z = 26$):**
  * $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ configuration: $[Ar]\,3d^6$
  * Removal of the third electron removes the paired electron from the $3d$ orbital:
    

$$
\text{Fe}^{2+}(3d^6) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_3} \text{Fe}^{3+}(3d^5) + e^-
$$

  * This relieves electron-electron repulsion and yields the extra-stable half-filled $3d^5$ configuration:
    

$$
\Delta_i H_3(\text{Fe}) = 2957\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

* Therefore, $\Delta_i H_3(\text{Mn}) \gg \Delta_i H_3(\text{Fe})$.

### Question 5 [CBSE 2020, 3 Marks]
Give reasons for the following:
**(a)** Transition metals show catalytic properties.  
**(b)** Copper(I) ion is not stable in aqueous solutions.  
**(c)** $\text{Zn}$ has the lowest enthalpy of atomisation in the $3d$ series ($\Delta_a H^\circ = 126\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).

**Solution:**
* **(a)** Catalytic activity is due to:
  1. Availability of variable oxidation states to form unstable reaction intermediates with reactants.
  2. Vacant $d$-orbitals that can coordinate with reactant molecules, lowering the activation energy ($E_a$).
  3. Large surface area for physical adsorption of reactants.
* **(b)** $\text{Cu}^+(aq)$ undergoes disproportionation:
  

$$
2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

  Because the high hydration energy of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ = -2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) compensates for the energy required to remove the second electron ($\Delta_i H_2^\circ = +1960\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).
* **(c)** Enthalpy of atomisation depends directly on the strength of metallic bonding, which is governed by the number of unpaired $d$-electrons involved in interatomic metallic bonding. Zinc has the configuration $3d^{10} 4s^2$, with **no unpaired $d$-electrons**. Metallic bonding is weak, resulting in the lowest atomisation enthalpy.

## 4. EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

```
              ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
              │           COMMON EXAM PITFALLS & PREVENTIONS            │
              └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   [TRAP] Writing "Cu+ is unstable because of d10 configuration"
   [FIX]  Cu+ has a stable d10 config, but in WATER it is unstable due to 
          hydration enthalpy of Cu2+ driving disproportionation.
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   [TRAP] Confusing Cr3+ stability origin (Writing "half-filled d5")
   [FIX]  Cr3+ is d3, which is half-filled t2g in aqueous crystal field.
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   [TRAP] Missing units on Magnetic Moment
   [FIX]  Always suffix with "BM" (Bohr Magnetons). e.g., 3.87 BM.
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   [TRAP] Sign confusion on E°cell for disproportionation
   [FIX]  E°cell = E°(cathode) - E°(anode). Cu+/Cu is reduction (+0.52 V), 
          Cu2+/Cu+ is oxidation (+0.15 V). E°cell = +0.37 V (Spontaneous).
```

* **Trap 1: The "Half-Filled Fallacy" for $\text{Cr}^{3+}$:**
  * *Student Error:* Writing that $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ is stable because it has a "half-filled $d^5$ configuration".
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ is **$3d^3$**. In aqueous solution, the octahedral ligand field splits the $d$-orbitals into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$. A $3d^3$ ion has a **half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ level**, which provides extra crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE).
* **Trap 2: Ignoring Hydration Enthalpy when Discussing $E^\circ$:**
  * *Student Error:* Explaining $E^\circ$ trends using only ionisation enthalpy.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* Zero marks are given if $E^\circ$ is explained without stating that $E^\circ$ is the net sum of $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ$, $\sum \Delta_i H^\circ$, and $\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ$.
* **Trap 3: Forgetting Units for Magnetic Moment:**
  * *Student Error:* Writing $\mu = 3.87$.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* Writing the number without **$\text{BM}$** (Bohr Magneton) can lead to a 0.5-mark deduction.
* **Trap 4: Forgetting the Disproportionation Equation for $\text{Cu}^+$:**
  * *Student Error:* Writing $\text{Cu}^+ \to \text{Cu}^{2+} + e^-$.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* Disproportionation **must** be written as a balanced auto-redox equation:
    

$$
2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

* **Trap 5: Electron Removal Order (The $4s$ vs $3d$ Trap):**
  * *Student Error:* Writing $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ as $3d^4 4s^2$.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* During ionisation, electrons are **always removed from the outer $4s$ orbital first**, not $3d$. Therefore, $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is $[Ar]\,3d^6 4s^0$.

### 2. High Oxidation State Oxides/Fluorides Summary Chart

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Oxides: } & \text{Sc}_2\text{O}_3 (+3) \to \text{TiO}_2 (+4) \to \text{V}_2\text{O}_5 (+5) \to \text{CrO}_3 (+6) \to \mathbf{Mn_2O_7 (+7)} \\

\end{aligned}
$$

> **Key Rule:** Oxygen stabilizes the $+7$ state in $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ through **$p\pi\text{--}d\pi$ multiple bonding**, whereas fluorine can only form single bonds and cannot sterically accommodate 7 fluorines around $\text{Mn}$.

***

\boxed{\begin{aligned}
E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} &= \mathbf{+0.34\text{ V}} \implies \text{Positive due to high } \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ + \sum \Delta_i H^\circ \\

# Formation of Coloured Ions, Complex Formation, Catalytic Properties, and Interstitial Compounds & Alloy Formation

# Class 12 Chemistry Master Teaching Note

## Unit 8: The $d$- and $f$-Block Elements

### Subtopic: Formation of Coloured Ions, Complex Formation, Catalytic Properties, Interstitial Compounds & Alloy Formation

***

## 1. Pedagogical Theory & Deep Molecular Concepts

***

### Part A: Formation of Coloured Ions
**Board Frequency Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Fundamental Principle & Physics of Colour
In an isolated, gaseous transition metal atom or ion, all five $d$-orbitals ($d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}, d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}$) are degenerate (possess identical energy).

When ligands or surrounding solvent molecules (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) approach the central metal ion:
1. An electrostatic crystal field is created.
2. The degeneracy of the five $d$-orbitals is lifted (Crystal Field Splitting).
3. In an octahedral field, the orbitals split into two distinct energy levels:
   - A lower energy set of three degenerate orbitals: $t_{2g}$ ($d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}$)
   - A higher energy set of two degenerate orbitals: $e_g$ ($d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}$)

The energy separation between these levels is denoted as $\Delta_o$ (Octahedral Crystal Field Splitting Energy).

```
          --- ---  e_g (d_x^2-y^2, d_z^2)
             ^
             |  Δ_o
             v
      --- --- ---  t_2g (d_xy, d_yz, d_zx)
```

#### 2. The $d\text{--}d$ Transition Mechanism
When visible white light falls on a transition metal complex containing an **incomplete $d$-subshell** ($d^1$ to $d^9$):
- An electron in a lower energy $d$-orbital ($t_{2g}$) absorbs a photon of a specific frequency $\nu$ corresponding to the energy gap $\Delta_o$:
  

$$
\Delta_o = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

- The electron is promoted to the vacant or half-filled higher energy $d$-orbital ($e_g$). This excitation is called a **$d\text{--}d$ transition**.
- The transmitted or reflected light is depleted of the absorbed wavelength. The human eye perceives the **complementary colour** of the absorbed radiation.

#### 3. Criteria for Colourlessness
- **$d^0$ Configuration** (e.g., $\text{Sc}^{3+}, \text{Ti}^{4+}$): Absence of $d$-electrons prevents any $d\text{--}d$ transition. Hence, salts are colourless/white.
- **$d^{10}$ Configuration** (e.g., $\text{Zn}^{2+}, \text{Cd}^{2+}, \text{Hg}^{2+}, \text{Cu}^+$): Completely filled $d$-orbitals leave no vacant orbital in the higher energy set ($e_g$) to receive an excited electron. $d\text{--}d$ transitions are forbidden by the Pauli Exclusion Principle. Hence, salts are colourless/white.

#### 4. Charge-Transfer Phenomenon ($d^0$ Exception)
Certain oxometallates exhibit intense, brilliant colours despite having a strictly $d^0$ electronic configuration:
- $\text{KMnO}_4$ (Permanganate ion, $\text{MnO}_4^-$, $\text{Mn}^{7+} \implies [Ar]\,3d^0$): **Deep Purple**
- $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ (Dichromate ion, $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$, $\text{Cr}^{6+} \implies [Ar]\,3d^0$): **Intense Orange**
- $\text{K}_2\text{CrO}_4$ (Chromate ion, $\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{Cr}^{6+} \implies [Ar]\,3d^0$): **Bright Yellow**

**Reason:** Ligand-to-Metal Charge Transfer (**LMCT**).
A transient electronic transition occurs where an electron shifts from the filled $2p$ $\sigma/\pi$ molecular orbitals of the oxide ligand ($\text{O}^{2-}$) into the vacant, low-lying $3d$ orbitals of the highly electropositive metal centre ($\text{Mn}^{7+}$ or $\text{Cr}^{6+}$). Because this is a Laporte-allowed transition, the molar absorptivity is exceedingly high, yielding brilliant colouration.

***

### Part B: Complex Formation
**Board Frequency Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Definition
Complex compounds are coordination entities in which a central transition metal ion is bonded to a number of neutral molecules or anions (termed **ligands**) via coordinate covalent (dative) bonds, donating electron pairs into the vacant orbitals of the metal ion.

#### 2. Three Mandatory Structural Factors Promoting Complex Formation
According to NCERT, transition metals exhibit an unparalleled tendency to form complexes due to:
1. **Comparatively Small Cationic Size:** Minimizes internuclear distances and increases proximity to coordinating ligands.
2. **High Ionic Charge / High Charge Density:** Generates an enormous positive electrostatic potential on the ion's surface:
   

$$
\text{Charge Density} = \frac{\text{Ionic Charge}}{\text{Ionic Radius}}
$$

3. **Availability of Vacant Low-Energy $d$-Orbitals:** Readily available $(n-1)d$, $ns$, and $np$ orbitals of compatible symmetry and energy to accept lone pairs donated by ligands, undergoing hybridization ($sp^3, dsp^2, sp^3d^2, d^2sp^3$).

***

### Part C: Catalytic Properties
**Board Frequency Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Underlying Chemical Factors
Transition metals and their compounds function as industrial catalysts due to:
1. **Variable Oxidation States:** Transition elements can shuttle between oxidation states, allowing them to form unstable, reactive intermediates with reactants, opening alternate pathways with lower activation energies ($E_a$).
2. **Large Surface Area with Free Valencies:** Solid transition metals provide active surface sites for the adsorption of gaseous reactants, increasing local reactant concentration and weakening reactant bonds.

#### 2. Two Distinct Catalytic Mechanisms
1. **Intermediate Compound Formation Theory (Homogeneous Catalysis):**
   The catalyst enters the reaction cycle by altering its oxidation state to form an intermediate complex with one reactant, subsequently reacting with the second reactant to yield products and regenerate the catalyst in its original oxidation state.
2. **Adsorption Theory (Heterogeneous Catalysis):**
   - Reactant molecules diffuse to the catalyst surface.
   - Reactants are adsorbed onto active sites via chemisorption (utilizing free $d$-orbital valencies).
   - Bonds within reactants weaken; localized concentration increases.
   - Desorption of product molecules occurs, freeing active sites.

***

### Part D: Interstitial Compounds
**Board Frequency Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Definition & Formation
Interstitial compounds are formed when small non-metal atoms of low electronegativity—specifically **$\text{H}$, $\text{C}$, $\text{B}$, and $\text{N}$**—are trapped inside the interstitial voids (octahedral or tetrahedral spaces) present within the closed-packed crystal lattices of transition metals.

#### 2. Four Cardinal NCERT Physical & Chemical Characteristics
1. **Very High Melting Points:** Their melting points are considerably higher than those of the corresponding pure parent transition metals due to strong covalent metal-nonmetal bonding inside the lattice.
2. **Extreme Hardness:** Trapping non-metals impedes dislocation planes from sliding over each other. Many interstitial borides and carbides approach diamond in hardness (e.g., $\text{TiC}$, $\text{WC}$).
3. **Retention of Metallic Conductivity:** Electrical and thermal conductivity are fully retained because the parent metallic bonding framework and delocalized conduction electrons are preserved.
4. **Chemical Inertness:** They exhibit marked chemical inertness toward attack by common mineral acids, oxidizing agents, and alkalis.
5. **Non-Stoichiometric Nature:** They are non-stoichiometric compounds that do not conform to standard valency rules (e.g., $\text{TiH}_{1.7}, \text{VH}_{0.56}, \text{Fe}_3\text{C}, \text{Mn}_4\text{N}$).

***

### Part E: Alloy Formation
**Board Frequency Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Definition & Structural Condition
An alloy is an intimate solid solution of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal, prepared by mixing the components in molten states and cooling.

#### 2. The Hume-Rothery Rule
Transition metals form alloys readily because:
- The atomic radii of transition metals of any given series are very similar (within $\sim 15\%$ of each other).
- Consequently, atoms of one transition metal can replace atoms of another metal in the parent crystal lattice without introducing destructive lattice strain, yielding homogeneous substitutional solid solutions.

#### 3. Distinct Properties of Alloys
- Harder and significantly more resistant to mechanical abrasion than the parent constituent metals.
- Elevated melting points.
- Higher resistance to atmospheric corrosion and chemical oxidation (e.g., Stainless Steel, Brass, Bronze, Nichrome).
- Ferrous alloys: Transition metals like $\text{Cr, Ni, Mn, V, Mo, W}$ dissolve in iron to form specialty alloy steels.

***

## 2. Step-by-Step Chemical Reactions, Derivations & Mechanisms

***

### Mechanism 1: Catalytic Oxidation of Iodide by Peroxodisulphate (Persulphate) via $\text{Fe}^{3+}$
**Reaction Overview:**
Direct reaction between $\text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}$ and $\text{I}^-$ is kinetically very slow due to high activation energy arising from electrostatic repulsion between two negative ions (anion-anion repulsion):

$$
\text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}(aq) + 2\text{I}^-(aq) \xrightarrow{\text{Uncatalysed (Slow)}} 2\text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq) + \text{I}_2(s)
$$

**Catalyzed Two-Step Cycle by $\text{Fe}^{3+}$/$\text{Fe}^{2+}$ Couple:**
- **Step 1:** $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ oxidizes $\text{I}^-$ to iodine ($\text{I}_2$) and is itself reduced to $\text{Fe}^{2+}$:
  

$$
2\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + 2\text{I}^-(aq) \xrightarrow{\text{Fast}} 2\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{I}_2(s)
$$

- **Step 2:** $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ reduces peroxodisulphate ($\text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}$) to sulphate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$), regenerating the active catalyst $\text{Fe}^{3+}$:
  

$$
2\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}(aq) \xrightarrow{\text{Fast}} 2\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + 2\text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq)
$$

$$
\text{Net Catalytic Cycle:} \quad \text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-} + 2\text{I}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}} 2\text{SO}_4^{2-} + \text{I}_2
$$

***

### Mechanism 2: Catalytic Oxidation of $\text{SO}_2$ in the Contact Process using $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$
**Reaction Overview:**

$$
2\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \xrightleftharpoons[\text{450}^\circ\text{C, 1--2 bar}]{\text{V}_2\text{O}_5} 2\text{SO}_3(g) \quad (\Delta_r H^\circ = -196.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})
$$

**Redox Shuttling Mechanism:**
- **Step 1:** $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ ($\text{V}^{5+}$) oxidizes $\text{SO}_2$ to $\text{SO}_3$, reducing vanadium to $\text{V}^{4+}$ ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_4$):
  

$$
\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{V}_2\text{O}_5(s) \longrightarrow \text{SO}_3(g) + \text{V}_2\text{O}_4(s)
$$

- **Step 2:** Atmospheric dioxygen re-oxidizes $\text{V}_2\text{O}_4$ back to $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$:
  

$$
\text{V}_2\text{O}_4(s) + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{V}_2\text{O}_5(s)
$$

***

### Key Coordination Complex Formation Equations
1. **Copper(II) with excess ammonia:**
   

$$
\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 4\text{NH}_3(aq) \rightleftharpoons \left[\text{Cu}(\text{NH}_3)_4\right]^{2+}(aq) \quad [\text{Deep Azure Blue}]
$$

2. **Nickel(II) with dimethylglyoxime ($\text{DMG}$):**
   

$$
\text{Ni}^{2+} + 2\text{DMG}^- \longrightarrow \left[\text{Ni}(\text{dmg})_2\right](s) \quad [\text{Rosy Red Precipitate}]
$$

3. **Iron(III) with thiocyanate:**
   

$$
\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + \text{SCN}^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons [\text{Fe}(\text{SCN})]^{2+}(aq) \quad [\text{Blood Red Complex}]
$$

## 3. High-Yield Examples & 5-Year Board PYQs (2020–2025)

### Example 1: Colourless vs Coloured Ions
**[CBSE Board 2024, Set-1, 2 Marks]**
> **Question:** Account for the following:
> (a) $\text{Sc}^{3+}$ salts are white, whereas $\text{Ti}^{3+}$ salts are purple in aqueous solution.
> (b) $\text{Cu}^+$ salts are diamagnetic and colourless, whereas $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ salts are paramagnetic and coloured.

**Solution:**
- **(a)** 
  - Ground state configurations:
    

$$
\text{Sc} \,(Z=21): [Ar]\,3d^1 4s^2 \implies \text{Sc}^{3+}: [Ar]\,3d^0
$$

    

$$
\text{Ti} \,(Z=22): [Ar]\,3d^2 4s^2 \implies \text{Ti}^{3+}: [Ar]\,3d^1
$$

  - $\text{Sc}^{3+}$ has a completely empty $3d$-subshell ($3d^0$). Due to the absence of $d$-electrons, no $d\text{--}d$ transition can occur upon absorption of visible light. Hence, $\text{Sc}^{3+}$ salts transmit all visible light and appear white/colourless.
  - $\text{Ti}^{3+}$ has one electron in its $3d$-subshell ($3d^1$). In aqueous solution, it forms $\left[\text{Ti}(\text{H}_2\text{O})_6\right]^{3+}$, in which the $3d^1$ electron occupies the $t_{2g}$ level. It absorbs green-yellow radiation to undergo a $t_{2g} \to e_g$ ($d\text{--}d$) transition, transmitting the complementary purple colour.
- **(b)**
  - Configuration of $\text{Cu}$ ($Z=29$): $[Ar]\,3d^{10} 4s^1$.
  - $\text{Cu}^+: [Ar]\,3d^{10}$. It has zero unpaired electrons ($n=0$, fully filled $d$-orbitals). Thus, it is diamagnetic. Because all $d$-orbitals are fully occupied, $d\text{--}d$ transitions are forbidden, making $\text{Cu}^+$ salts colourless.
  - $\text{Cu}^{2+}: [Ar]\,3d^9$. It possesses one unpaired electron ($n=1$, partially filled $d$-orbitals). The presence of an unpaired electron makes it paramagnetic. It absorbs red-orange wavelengths and undergoes a $d\text{--}d$ transition, transmitting blue colour.

***

### Example 2: Anomalous Intense Colour of $\text{MnO}_4^-$
**[CBSE Board 2023, Set-2, 2 Marks]**
> **Question:** Why is $\text{KMnO}_4$ intensely purple in colour even though the manganese ion has an oxidation state of $+7$ with a $d^0$ configuration?

**Solution:**
1. In $\text{KMnO}_4$, manganese is in the $+7$ oxidation state:
   

$$
\text{Mn}^{7+} \implies [Ar]\,3d^0 4s^0
$$

2. Since there are no $3d$ electrons, $d\text{--}d$ transitions are strictly impossible.
3. The deep purple colour arises due to **Ligand-to-Metal Charge Transfer (LMCT)**:
   - A transient transfer of charge (electron density) occurs from the filled $2p$ orbitals of the $\text{O}^{2-}$ ligands to the vacant, low-energy $3d$ orbitals of the central $\text{Mn}^{7+}$ ion upon absorbing yellow-green light from the visible spectrum.
   - This charge transfer is spin-allowed and Laporte-allowed, producing an intense purple transmitted colour.

***

### Example 3: Catalytic Mechanism of Iron(III)
**[CBSE Board 2022, Term-II, 3 Marks]**
> **Question:** 
> (a) Explain why transition metals act as effective catalysts.
> (b) Using chemical equations, illustrate the catalytic role of $\text{Fe}(\text{III})$ in the reaction between iodide and persulphate ions.

**Solution:**
- **(a)** Transition metals are effective catalysts because:
  1. They show variable oxidation states, enabling them to form unstable, low-activation-energy intermediates.
  2. They offer a large surface area with free valencies for chemisorption of reactants.
- **(b)**
  1. The direct uncatalysed reaction is slow due to electrostatic repulsion between two anions ($\text{I}^-$ and $\text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}$).
  2. In the presence of $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, the reaction proceeds through two rapid steps involving the $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$ redox couple:
     

$$
\text{Step 1:} \quad 2\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + 2\text{I}^-(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{I}_2(s)
$$

     

$$
\text{Step 2:} \quad 2\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + 2\text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq)
$$

  3. $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is regenerated at the end of the reaction, fulfilling the definition of a catalyst.

### Example 4: Interstitial Compounds
**[CBSE Board 2020, Set-3, 3 Marks]**
> **Question:** 
> (a) What are interstitial compounds?
> (b) Write any two distinct physical/chemical characteristics of interstitial compounds.
> (c) Why do transition metals readily form alloys?

**Solution:**
- **(a)** Interstitial compounds are compounds formed when small non-metallic atoms (such as $\text{H}, \text{C}, \text{B}, \text{N}$) are trapped inside the interstitial spaces/voids of the crystal lattice of transition metals.
- **(b)** Characteristics (any two):
  1. They have very high melting points, even higher than those of the pure parent metals.
  2. They are extremely hard; some borides approach diamond in hardness.
  3. They retain metallic conductivity (electrical and thermal).
  4. They are chemically inert and non-stoichiometric (e.g., $\text{TiC}, \text{Fe}_3\text{C}, \text{TiH}_{1.7}$).
- **(c)** Transition metals readily form alloys because their atomic radii across a given series are very similar (within $\sim 15\%$). Thus, atoms of one metal can substitute for atoms of another in the crystal lattice, forming homogeneous substitutional solid solutions.

## 1. Oxides of Transition Metals & Oxoanions: Overview

### 1.1 Trend in Oxidation States & Nature of Oxides
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Transition metals react with oxygen at elevated temperatures to form metal oxides. 

1. **Maximum Oxidation State in Oxides:**
   The highest oxidation state in the oxides coincides with the total number of valence electrons $(n-1)d + ns^1 \text{ or } ns^2$ up to Group 7.
   - Scandium ($\text{Sc}$): forms $\text{Sc}_2\text{O}_3$ ($+3$)
   - Titanium ($\text{Ti}$): forms $\text{TiO}_2$ ($+4$)
   - Vanadium ($\text{V}$): forms $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ ($+5$)
   - Chromium ($\text{Cr}$): forms $\text{CrO}_3$ ($+6$)
   - Manganese ($\text{Mn}$): forms $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ ($+7$)
   - Beyond Group 7, no higher oxides of iron above $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ ($+3$) are stable under ambient conditions, though ferrates $[\text{FeO}_4]^{2-}$ ($+6$) form in alkaline media.

2. **Acidic, Basic, and Amphoteric Trends:**
   - **Low oxidation state ($+2, +3$):** Basic in nature due to lower ionic potential (charge/radius ratio $\phi$) and predominantly ionic character.
     

$$
\text{CrO} \quad (\text{Basic, } \text{Cr}^{2+})
$$

     

$$
\text{MnO} \quad (\text{Basic, } \text{Mn}^{2+})
$$

   - **Intermediate oxidation state ($+3, +4$):** Amphoteric in nature.
     

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3 \quad (\text{Amphoteric, } \text{Cr}^{3+})
$$

     

$$
\text{V}_2\text{O}_5 \quad (\text{Amphoteric, predominantly acidic, dissolves in both acids to give } \text{VO}_2^+ \text{ and alkalies to give } \text{VO}_4^{3-})
$$

     

$$
\text{MnO}_2 \quad (\text{Weakly amphoteric})
$$

   - **High oxidation state ($+5, +6, +7$):** Covalent and acidic in nature. High oxidation states polarise oxide ions intensely (Fajans' Rules), yielding covalent oxides that hydrate to form oxoacids.
     

$$
\text{CrO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CrO}_4 \quad (\text{Chromic acid - Strongly acidic})
$$

     

$$
\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{HMnO}_4 \quad (\text{Permanganic acid - Green oily liquid, violently explosive})
$$

## 2. Potassium Dichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$)

### 2.1 Industrial Preparation from Chromite Ore ($\text{FeCr}_2\text{O}_4$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Potassium dichromate is manufactured from **chromite ore** (ferrochrome or chrome iron stone, $\text{FeO}\cdot\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$ or $\text{FeCr}_2\text{O}_4$) in three sequential stages.

#### Step 1: Fusion of Chromite Ore with Sodium Carbonate in Excess Air (Oxidative Roasting)
The powdered ore is mixed with $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ (or $\text{NaOH}$) and quicklime ($\text{CaO}$, added to keep the mass porous and facilitate oxygen diffusion) and roasted in a reverberatory furnace in excess air.

$$
\boxed{4\text{FeCr}_2\text{O}_4\ (\text{s}) + 8\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\ (\text{s}) + 7\text{O}_2\ (\text{g}) \xrightarrow{\Delta \approx 1200\text{--}1300\text{ K}} 8\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4\ (\text{s}) + 2\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\ (\text{s}) + 8\text{CO}_2\ (\text{g})}
$$

- **Oxidation State Changes:**
  - $\text{Fe}^{2+} \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}$ (Oxidised)
  - $\text{Cr}^{3+} \longrightarrow \text{Cr}^{6+}$ (Oxidised)
  - $\text{O}_2^0 \longrightarrow \text{O}^{2-}$ (Reduced)
- **Separation:** The roasted mass is extracted with water. Sodium chromate ($\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4$) dissolves completely, yielding an intensely **yellow solution**, while ferric oxide ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) remains behind as an insoluble red-brown residue and is filtered out.

#### Step 2: Conversion of Sodium Chromate to Sodium Dichromate
The yellow filtrate of $\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4$ is acidified with concentrated sulphuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).

$$
\boxed{2\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4\ (\text{aq}) + 2\text{H}^+\ (\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\ (\text{aq}) + 2\text{Na}^+\ (\text{aq}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}\ (\text{l})}
$$

$$
\text{or fully balanced: } 2\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\downarrow + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

- **Isolation:** Sodium sulphate ($\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) crystallises out first upon partial evaporation and concentration of the hot solution and is removed by filtration. The remaining concentrated solution deposits bright orange-red crystals of sodium dichromate ($\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$).

#### Step 3: Conversion of Sodium Dichromate into Potassium Dichromate
Sodium dichromate is highly hygroscopic and deliquescent, making it unsuitable as a primary standard. It is treated with potassium chloride ($\text{KCl}$).

$$
\boxed{\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\ (\text{aq}) + 2\text{KCl}\ (\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\ (\text{s})\downarrow + 2\text{NaCl}\ (\text{aq})}
$$

- **Driving Principle:** $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ is moderately soluble in cold water ($4.9\text{ g}/100\text{ mL}$ at $273\text{ K}$) compared to $\text{NaCl}$ ($35.7\text{ g}/100\text{ mL}$) and $\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$. Upon cooling the hot saturated solution, brilliant orange crystals of pure anhydrous $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ separate out cleanly.

### 2.2 Chromate-Dichromate pH-Dependent Equilibrium
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In aqueous solution, chromate ion $[\text{CrO}_4]^{2-}$ (yellow) and dichromate ion $[\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7]^{2-}$ (orange) exist in dynamic equilibrium controlled strictly by the hydronium ion concentration ($\text{pH}$):

$$
\boxed{2\text{CrO}_4^{2-}\ (\text{aq, yellow}) + 2\text{H}^+\ (\text{aq}) \rightleftharpoons \text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}\ (\text{aq, orange}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}\ (\text{l})}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}\ (\text{aq, orange}) + 2\text{OH}^-\ (\text{aq}) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{CrO}_4^{2-}\ (\text{aq, yellow}) + \text{H}_2\text{O}\ (\text{l})}
$$

- **In Acidic Medium ($\text{pH} < 4$):** Equilibrium shifts to the right; $[\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7]^{2-}$ predominates (colour turns **orange**).
- **In Alkaline Medium ($\text{pH} > 7$):** Equilibrium shifts to the left; $[\text{CrO}_4]^{2-}$ predominates (colour turns **yellow**).
- **Oxidation State:** The oxidation state of Chromium remains **$+6$** in both ions. This is **not a redox reaction**, but an acid-base condensation/hydrolysis equilibrium.

***

### 2.3 Structural Features of $[\text{CrO}_4]^{2-}$ and $[\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7]^{2-}$
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

```
     Chromate Ion [CrO4]2-                Dichromate Ion [Cr2O7]2-
     
             O(-)                                  O       O
             |                                    //       \\
             Cr = O                             O=Cr - O - Cr=O
           //  \                                  |         |
          O     O(-)                             O(-)      O(-)
     (Tetrahedral, Td)                     (Two tetrahedra sharing 1 corner)
                                             Cr-O-Cr bridge angle = 126°
```

1. **Chromate Ion ($[\text{CrO}_4]^{2-}$):**
   - **Geometry:** Tetrahedral ($T_d$ symmetry).
   - **Hybridisation:** Involves $d^3s$ or $sp^3$ mixing (traditionally treated as $d^3s$ using $(n-1)d$ orbitals in transition metal oxoanions).
   - **Bond Lengths:** All four $\text{Cr}-\text{O}$ bond lengths are identical ($166\text{ pm}$) due to equivalent resonance delocalisation of two negative charges over four oxygen atoms.

2. **Dichromate Ion ($[\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7]^{2-}$):**
   - **Geometry:** Consists of two tetrahedral units sharing one apex oxygen atom ($\text{Cr}-\text{O}-\text{Cr}$ bridging oxygen).
   - **Bridge Parameters:** 
     - $\text{Cr}-\text{O}-\text{Cr}$ bond angle = $\mathbf{126^\circ}$
     - Bridging $\text{Cr}-\text{O}_{\text{bridge}}$ bond length = $\mathbf{179\text{ pm}}$ (longer and weaker single bond)
   - **Terminal Parameters:**
     - Six terminal $\text{Cr}-\text{O}_{\text{terminal}}$ bond lengths = $\mathbf{163\text{ pm}}$ (shorter due to significant $p\pi-d\pi$ multiple bond character).

***

### 2.4 Oxidising Properties of $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ in Acidic Medium
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ acts as a potent oxidising agent. Chromium is reduced from $+6$ to $+3$:

#### Master Reduction Half-Reaction:

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}\ (\text{green}) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (E^\circ = +1.33\text{ V})}
$$

- **Equivalent Weight Calculation:**
  

$$
\text{Equivalent Mass of } \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 = \frac{\text{Molar Mass } (M)}{n\text{-factor}} = \frac{M}{6} = \frac{294.185\text{ g/mol}}{6} = \mathbf{49.03\text{ g/equiv}}
$$

#### Typical Substrate Half-Reactions & Net Ionic Equations:

1. **Oxidation of Iodide ($\text{I}^-$) to Iodine ($\text{I}_2$):**
   

$$
\text{Oxidation: } 6\text{I}^- \longrightarrow 3\text{I}_2 + 6e^-
$$

   

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6\text{I}^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{I}_2 + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

2. **Oxidation of Ferrous ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) to Ferric ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$):**
   

$$
\text{Oxidation: } 6(\text{Fe}^{2+} \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + e^-)
$$

   

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6\text{Fe}^{2+} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 6\text{Fe}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

3. **Oxidation of Hydrogen Sulphide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) to Colloidal Sulphur ($\text{S}$):**
   

$$
\text{Oxidation: } 3(\text{H}_2\text{S} \longrightarrow \text{S}\downarrow + 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-)
$$

   

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 8\text{H}^+ + 3\text{H}_2\text{S} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{S}\ (\text{turbidity}) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

4. **Oxidation of Sulphite ($\text{SO}_3^{2-}$) to Sulphate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$):**
   

$$
\text{Oxidation: } 3(\text{SO}_3^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{SO}_4^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-)
$$

   

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 8\text{H}^+ + 3\text{SO}_3^{2-} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{SO}_4^{2-} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

5. **Oxidation of Stannous ($\text{Sn}^{2+}$) to Stannic ($\text{Sn}^{4+}$):**
   

$$
\text{Oxidation: } 3(\text{Sn}^{2+} \longrightarrow \text{Sn}^{4+} + 2e^-)
$$

   

$$
\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 3\text{Sn}^{2+} \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{Sn}^{4+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

### 2.5 Chromyl Chloride Test ($\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Used for detecting ionic inorganic chlorides ($\text{Cl}^-$).

1. Solid chloride salt is heated with solid $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ and conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$:
   

$$
\boxed{\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 + 4\text{NaCl} + 6\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\ (\text{conc.}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2\uparrow\ (\text{deep red vapour}) + 2\text{KHSO}_4 + 4\text{NaHSO}_4 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

2. The red fumes of chromyl chloride ($\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2$) are passed into dilute $\text{NaOH}$:
   

$$
\boxed{\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2 + 4\text{NaOH} \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4\ (\text{yellow solution}) + 2\text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

3. The yellow solution is acidified with acetic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) and treated with lead acetate $(\text{CH}_3\text{COO})_2\text{Pb}$:
   

$$
\boxed{\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4 + (\text{CH}_3\text{COO})_2\text{Pb} \longrightarrow \text{PbCrO}_4\downarrow\ (\text{canary yellow precipitate}) + 2\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}}
$$

- **Negative Tests:** Covalently bonded chlorides like $\text{HgCl}_2$, $\text{AgCl}$, $\text{PbCl}_2$, and $\text{SnCl}_4$ do not liberate $\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2$ readily because they do not yield sufficient free $\text{Cl}^-$ ions.

## 3. Potassium Permanganate ($\text{KMnO}_4$)

### 3.1 Industrial & Laboratory Preparation from Pyrolusite ($\text{MnO}_2$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### Step 1: Oxidative Alkaline Fusion of Pyrolusite to Manganate
Black pyrolusite powder ($\text{MnO}_2$) is fused with caustic alkali ($\text{KOH}$) in the presence of atmospheric oxygen or an oxidising agent ($\text{KNO}_3$ or $\text{KClO}_3$):

$$
\boxed{2\text{MnO}_2\ (\text{s}) + 4\text{KOH}\ (\text{l}) + \text{O}_2\ (\text{g}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4\ (\text{dark green mass}) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\ (\text{g})}
$$

- **Oxidation State:** Manganese oxidises from $+4$ in $\text{MnO}_2$ to $+6$ in $\text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4$.

#### Step 2: Conversion of Potassium Manganate to Potassium Permanganate
The fused green mass is leached with water. Conversion can be accomplished by two methods:

##### A. Disproportionation in Acidic/Neutral Solution (Chemical Route):
The green manganate solution is acidified or treated with carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$ or chlorine gas $\text{Cl}_2$):

$$
\boxed{3\text{MnO}_4^{2-}\ (\text{aq, green}) + 4\text{H}^+\ (\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{MnO}_4^-\ (\text{aq, purple}) + \text{MnO}_2\ (\text{s})\downarrow + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\ (\text{l})}
$$

$$
\text{With Carbon dioxide: } 3\text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4 + 4\text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{KMnO}_4 + \text{MnO}_2\downarrow + 4\text{KHCO}_3
$$

- **Disproportionation:** $\text{Mn}^{6+}$ ($3\text{MnO}_4^{2-}$) simultaneously oxidises to $\text{Mn}^{7+}$ ($2\text{MnO}_4^-$) and reduces to $\text{Mn}^{4+}$ ($\text{MnO}_2$).
- **Disadvantage:** $1/3^{\text{rd}}$ of the valuable manganese is lost as $\text{MnO}_2$ precipitate.

##### B. Electrolytic Oxidation (Industrial Route):
The alkaline potassium manganate solution is electrolysed between iron electrodes separated by a diaphragm:
- **Anode (Iron/Nickel):** Oxidation occurs:
  

$$
\boxed{\text{MnO}_4^{2-}\ (\text{green}) \longrightarrow \text{MnO}_4^-\ (\text{purple}) + e^-}
$$

- **Cathode:** Evolution of hydrogen gas:
  

$$
2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{H}_2\uparrow + 2\text{OH}^-
$$

- **Yield:** Gives a $100\%$ conversion without loss of manganese to $\text{MnO}_2$.

#### Laboratory Preparation:
Oxidation of manganese(II) salts with peroxodisulphate ($\text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}$):

$$
\boxed{2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-} + 8\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{MnO}_4^- + 10\text{SO}_4^{2-} + 16\text{H}^+}
$$

***

### 3.2 Structures & Magnetic Properties of $[\text{MnO}_4]^{2-}$ vs $[\text{MnO}_4]^-$
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

| Parameter | Manganate Ion ($[\text{MnO}_4]^{2-}$) | Permanganate Ion ($[\text{MnO}_4]^-$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Oxidation State of $\text{Mn}$** | $\mathbf{+6}$ | $\mathbf{+7}$ |
| **Outer Electronic Configuration** | $\text{Mn}^{6+}: [Ar]\,3d^1\,4s^0$ | $\text{Mn}^{7+}: [Ar]\,3d^0\,4s^0$ |
| **Number of Unpaired Electrons** | $n = 1$ | $n = 0$ |
| **Magnetic Behavior** | **Paramagnetic** ($\mu_{\text{eff}} \approx 1.73\text{ B.M.}$) | **Diamagnetic** (weak temperature-independent paramagnetism) |
| **Colour** | **Dark Green** | **Deep Purple / Magenta** |
| **Geometry** | Tetrahedral ($d^3s$ or $sp^3$) | Tetrahedral ($d^3s$ or $sp^3$) |

#### Origin of the Deep Purple Colour in $[\text{MnO}_4]^-$ (No $d\text{--}d$ Transition possible!):
In $[\text{MnO}_4]^-$, $\text{Mn}^{7+}$ has a vacant $d$-shell ($3d^0$). Therefore, **$d\text{--}d$ electronic transitions cannot occur**.
- **Mechanism:** The intense purple colour originates from **Ligand-to-Metal Charge Transfer (LMCT)**.
- An electron transiently shifts from an oxygen $2p$ molecular orbital (ligand) to a vacant $3d$ orbital of the highly positive $\text{Mn}^{7+}$ ion upon absorption of yellow-green light ($\lambda \approx 530\text{ nm}$), transmitting the complementary deep purple light.

***

### 3.3 Thermal Decomposition of $\text{KMnO}_4$
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Dry $\text{KMnO}_4$ crystals decompose upon heating at $513\text{ K}$ ($240^\circ\text{C}$):

$$
\boxed{2\text{KMnO}_4\ (\text{s}) \xrightarrow{513\text{ K}} \text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4\ (\text{s}) + \text{MnO}_2\ (\text{s}) + \text{O}_2\ (\text{g})\uparrow}
$$

Upon heating further to red heat, $\text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4$ decomposes:

$$
2\text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{K}_2\text{O} + 2\text{MnO}_2 + \text{O}_2\uparrow
$$

***

## 4. Solved Board PYQs (2020–2025)

### Problem 1 [CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
**(a)** Complete and balance the following chemical equations:
1. $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + \text{H}^+ + \text{I}^- \longrightarrow$
2. $\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{I}^- \longrightarrow$

**(b)** Why is $\text{KMnO}_4$ not acidified with $\text{HCl}$ in redox titrations?

**Solution:**
**(a)**
1. In acidic medium, dichromate oxidises iodide to elemental iodine:
   

$$
\mathbf{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6\text{I}^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{I}_2 + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

2. In neutral or faintly alkaline medium, permanganate oxidises iodide to iodate ($\text{IO}_3^-$):
   

$$
\mathbf{2\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{I}^- \longrightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2 + \text{IO}_3^- + 2\text{OH}^-}
$$

**(b)**
$\text{KMnO}_4$ is a very strong oxidising agent ($E^\circ = +1.51\text{ V}$). If hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$) is used to acidify the solution:

$$
\mathbf{2\text{MnO}_4^- + 16\text{H}^+ + 10\text{Cl}^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{Cl}_2\uparrow + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}}
$$

The $\text{KMnO}_4$ oxidises $\text{Cl}^-$ ions to hazardous $\text{Cl}_2$ gas. Consequently, a portion of the $\text{KMnO}_4$ is consumed by the acid rather than the analyte (such as $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ or oxalate), causing large positive titration errors. Dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is inert towards oxidation under titration conditions and must always be used.

***

### Problem 2 [CBSE 2023, 2 Marks]
Account for the following:
1. Permanganate titrations in presence of oxalic acid are carried out warm ($\approx 60^\circ\text{C}$).
2. Although both chromate and dichromate possess $\text{Cr}$ in $+6$ oxidation state, they show different colours.

**Solution:**
1. The reaction between $\text{MnO}_4^-$ and oxalate ion ($\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$) has a high activation energy at room temperature because it involves the cleavage of a stable $\text{C}-\text{C}$ covalent bond and coulombic repulsion between two negatively charged reacting anions ($\text{MnO}_4^-$ and $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$). Heating the reaction mixture to $50\text{--}60^\circ\text{C}$ ($323\text{--}333\text{ K}$) provides the necessary activation energy. Once the reaction starts, the generated $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ acts as an **autocatalyst**, accelerating the reaction rate.
2. The difference in colour arises from differing **Charge Transfer spectra (Ligand-to-Metal Charge Transfer - LMCT)**. In the monomeric chromate $[\text{CrO}_4]^{2-}$, the energy gap between oxygen $2p$ ligand orbitals and vacant chromium $3d$ orbitals absorbs higher energy (blue region), reflecting yellow light. In the bridged dimeric dichromate $[\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7]^{2-}$, the bridging oxygen lowers the symmetry and alters the molecular orbital energy levels, causing absorption at longer wavelengths (blue-green region) and transmitting orange light.

### Problem 4 [CBSE 2020, 2 Marks]
Explain why:
1. $[\text{MnO}_4]^-$ is intensely coloured, while $[\text{Mn(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$ is very pale pink.
2. $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ is preferred over $\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ as a primary standard in volumetric analysis.

**Solution:**
1. In $[\text{Mn(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$, $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ is a $d^5$ system with all five electrons having parallel spins ($t_{2g}^3 e_g^2$). Any electronic transition within $d$-orbitals is both **Laporte-forbidden** ($\Delta l \neq \pm 1$ for centrosymmetric octahedral field) and **spin-forbidden** ($\Delta S \neq 0$). Hence, absorption is weak, leading to an extremely faint pink colour. In contrast, in $[\text{MnO}_4]^-$, colour originates from **Ligand-to-Metal Charge Transfer (LMCT)** from $\text{O}^{2-} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{7+}$, which is Laporte-allowed ($\Delta l = \pm 1$), giving an extinction coefficient thousands of times larger and an intense purple colour.
2. $\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ is **deliquescent and hygroscopic**; it readily absorbs moisture from the atmosphere, making it impossible to weigh out accurately. On the other hand, $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ is non-hygroscopic, highly pure, thermally stable, and can be accurately weighed to prepare primary standard solutions directly.

## 1. Pedagogical Foundations & Electronic Configurations

### 1.1 Definition & Classification (⭐⭐⭐⭐)
The $f$-block elements are elements in which the differentiating electron enters the anti-penultimate $(n-2)f$ subshell. They are embedded within the transition series between groups 3 and 4 in periods 6 and 7, hence named **Inner Transition Elements**.

Inner transition elements are divided into two series:
1. **Lanthanoids ($4f$-series):** 14 elements following Lanthanum ($Z = 57$), from Cerium ($_{58}\text{Ce}$) to Lutetium ($_{71}\text{Lu}$).
2. **Actinoids ($5f$-series):** 14 elements following Actinium ($Z = 89$), from Thorium ($_{90}\text{Th}$) to Lawrencium ($_{103}\text{Lr}$).

> [!NOTE]
> Lanthanum ($_{57}\text{La}: [\text{Xe}]\,5d^1 6s^2$) and Actinium ($_{89}\text{Ac}: [\text{Rn}]\,6d^1 7s^2$) strictly belong to the $d$-block (Group 3), but are traditionally studied alongside $4f$ and $5f$ series due to close physical and chemical resemblances.

***

$$

## 2. Lanthanoid Contraction (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

### 2.1 Theoretical Origin & Shielding Mechanics
As the atomic number increases from $\text{La}$ to $\text{Lu}$, for each subsequent element:
1. The nuclear charge increases by $+1e$.
2. The extra electron enters the inner **$(n-2)f = 4f$** subshell.

Due to the diffuse, angularly extended shapes of the seven $4f$ orbitals, the electron-electron mutual repulsion is weak, and the **shielding (screening) ability of $4f$ electrons is extremely poor**. The screening efficiency follows the hierarchy:

$$
s > p > d > f
$$

Consequently, the increase in nuclear charge is not counterbalanced by the shielding provided by $4f$ electrons. The effective nuclear charge experienced by outer electrons increases continuously:

$$
Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - \sigma
$$

Since $\Delta \sigma < \Delta Z$ across the $4f$ block:

$$
\frac{d Z_{\text{eff}}}{d Z} > 0
$$

Outer shells are drawn closer to the nucleus, resulting in a steady decrease in atomic and ionic radii.

$$
\boxed{\text{Atomic Radii: } \text{La}\,(187\text{ pm}) \longrightarrow \text{Lu}\,(173\text{ pm}) \quad (\Delta r \approx 14\text{ pm})}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Ionic Radii } (\text{M}^{3+}): \text{La}^{3+}\,(106\text{ pm}) \longrightarrow \text{Lu}^{3+}\,(86\text{ pm}) \quad (\Delta r \approx 20\text{ pm})}
$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The decrease in ionic radii across $\text{M}^{3+}$ ions is remarkably uniform and regular compared to atomic radii because atomic radii are perturbed by metallic bonding and crystal packing effects (e.g., anomalously high metallic radii for $\text{Eu}$ and $\text{Yb}$ due to participation of only 2 valence electrons in bonding).

***

### 2.2 Consequences of Lanthanoid Contraction

#### 1. Similarity of Second ($4d$) and Third ($5d$) Transition Series (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)
The regular filling of the $4f$ shell prior to the $5d$ series causes the covalent and ionic radii of $5d$ elements to match almost identically with their lighter $4d$ congeners in the same vertical group.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Group 4: } & r(\text{Zr}^{4+}) = 80\text{ pm} \approx r(\text{Hf}^{4+}) = 79\text{ pm} \\
\text{Group 5: } & r(\text{Nb}^{5+}) \approx r(\text{Ta}^{5+}) \\
\text{Group 6: } & r(\text{Mo}^{6+}) \approx r(\text{W}^{6+})
\end{aligned}
$$

* **Consequence:** $\text{Zr}$ and $\text{Hf}$ occur together in nature (called **chemical twins**), possess nearly identical ionization enthalpies and chemical properties, and are extremely difficult to separate by standard chemical precipitation.

#### 2. Basic Strength of Hydroxides, $\text{M(OH)}_3$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)
According to Fajans' Rules, the covalent character of a predominantly ionic bond increases with the polarizing power of the cation:

$$
\text{Polarizing Power} \propto \frac{\text{Cationic Charge}}{\text{Cationic Radius } (r_{\text{M}^{3+}})}
$$

From $\text{La}^{3+}$ to $\text{Lu}^{3+}$:
1. $r_{\text{M}^{3+}}$ decreases from $106\text{ pm}$ to $86\text{ pm}$.
2. Polarizing power of $\text{M}^{3+}$ increases significantly.
3. The covalent character of the $\text{M}-\text{OH}$ bond increases; the ionic character decreases.
4. Cleavage of the $\text{M}-\text{OH}$ bond in aqueous solution to liberate $\text{OH}^-$ becomes increasingly difficult.

$$
\boxed{\text{La(OH)}_3 > \text{Ce(OH)}_3 > \dots > \text{Lu(OH)}_3 \quad (\text{Decreasing Order of Basic Strength})}
$$

* $\text{La(OH)}_3$ is the **most basic**.
* $\text{Lu(OH)}_3$ is the **least basic** (amphoteric/weakly basic).

#### 3. Separation of Lanthanoids (⭐⭐⭐⭐)
Because the trivalent ions have nearly identical sizes and identical charges ($+3$), their chemical properties are very similar. However, small, progressive differences in their ionic radii lead to subtle gradients in:
* **Complex formation constants** ($\beta$).
* **Hydration enthalpy** ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ \propto \frac{1}{r_{\text{ion}}}$).
* Separation is achieved using **ion-exchange chromatography** (with chelating agents like EDTA or $\alpha$-hydroxyisobutyrate) and continuous **liquid-liquid solvent extraction** (using tri-$n$-butyl phosphate, TBP).

***

## 3. Oxidation States & Redox Thermodynamics (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

### 3.1 The Dominant $+3$ Oxidation State
The characteristic and thermodynamically most stable oxidation state for all lanthanoids in solid and aqueous systems is **$+3$**.
* The sum of the first three ionization enthalpies ($IE_1 + IE_2 + IE_3$) is compensated by the high enthalpy of hydration ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H$) of the small $\text{M}^{3+}$ ion or the lattice energy of its solid ionic compounds.

***

### 3.2 Anomalous Oxidation States: $+2$ and $+4$

Variable oxidation states ($+2$ and $+4$) arise exclusively due to the extra thermodynamic stability associated with **empty ($f^0$)**, **half-filled ($f^7$)**, or **completely filled ($f^{14}$)** subshells:

```
                  ┌───────── Empty subshell: f⁰  (e.g., Ce⁴⁺)
Extra Stability ──┼───────── Half-filled:   f⁷  (e.g., Eu²⁺, Tb⁴⁺)
                  └───────── Fully-filled:   f¹⁴ (e.g., Yb²⁺)
```

#### Detailed Breakdown of Non-$+3$ States:

| Element | Oxidation State | Electronic Configuration | Thermodynamic Driver | Redox Behavior & Standard Potential |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$\text{Ce}$** | $+4$ | $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^0$ | Noble gas configuration | **Strong Oxidizing Agent**: $E^\circ(\text{Ce}^{4+}/\text{Ce}^{3+}) = +1.74\text{ V}$ |
| **$\text{Tb}$** | $+4$ | $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^7$ | Stable half-filled $4f^7$ | **Strong Oxidizing Agent**: Readily reduced to $\text{Tb}^{3+}$ |
| **$\text{Eu}$** | $+2$ | $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^7$ | Stable half-filled $4f^7$ | **Strong Reducing Agent**: $E^\circ(\text{Eu}^{3+}/\text{Eu}^{2+}) = -0.43\text{ V}$ |
| **$\text{Yb}$** | $+2$ | $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^{14}$ | Completely filled $4f^{14}$ | **Strong Reducing Agent**: Re-oxidizes to $\text{Yb}^{3+}$ |
| **$\text{Sm}$** | $+2$ | $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^6$ | Intermediate stability | **Reducing Agent** |
| **$\text{Pr}, \text{Nd}, \text{Dy}$** | $+4$ | Solid oxides only | High lattice energy | Unstable in aqueous solution |

### 3.3 Quantitative Redox Reactions

#### 1. Analytical Reduction of Cerium(IV):
Cerium(IV) acts as an analytical oxidant in cerimetric titrations:

$$
\text{Ce}^{4+}(\text{aq}) + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Ce}^{3+}(\text{aq}) \quad E^\circ = +1.74\text{ V}
$$

Although $E^\circ > 1.23\text{ V}$ suggests $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ should thermodynamically oxidize water to $\text{O}_2$:

$$
4\text{Ce}^{4+}(\text{aq}) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \longrightarrow 4\text{Ce}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + 4\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) + \text{O}_2(\text{g})
$$

The reaction is **kinetically extremely slow**, allowing ammonium cerium(IV) nitrate/sulfate to be utilized as a volumetric laboratory reagent.

#### 2. Europium(II) Aqueous Oxidation:
Europium(II) is a vigorous one-electron reducing agent:

$$
\text{Eu}^{2+}(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow \text{Eu}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + e^- \quad E^\circ_{\text{ox}} = +0.43\text{ V}
$$

In aqueous acidic solutions, it reduces protons to dihydrogen gas:

$$
2\text{Eu}^{2+}(\text{aq}) + 2\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{Eu}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + \text{H}_2(\text{g}) \uparrow
$$

## 4. Chemical Reactivity & Coordination Chemistry

### 4.1 Chemical Reactivity of Lanthanoids (⭐⭐⭐⭐)
All lanthanoids are highly electropositive metals resembling calcium in reactivity.

```
                         ┌─── With O₂ (burns) ───> M₂O₃
                         │
                         ├─── With H₂ (heat) ────> MH₂ to MH₃ (interstitial)
                         │
        Lanthanoid Metal ├─── With Halogen (X₂) ─> MX₃
             (Ln)        │
                         ├─── With S (heat) ─────> M₂S₃
                         │
                         ├─── With N₂ (heat) ────> MN
                         │
                         ├─── With C (heat) ─────> LnC₂, Ln₂C₃, Ln₃C
                         │
                         └─── With H₂O / Dil Acid > M³⁺ + H₂↑
```

#### Stoichiometric Equations:

1. **Reaction with Oxygen:**
   

$$
4\text{Ln}(\text{s}) + 3\text{O}_2(\text{g}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Ln}_2\text{O}_3(\text{s}) \quad (\text{Exception: } \text{Ce} \text{ yields } \text{CeO}_2)
$$

2. **Reaction with Water:**
   

$$
2\text{Ln}(\text{s}) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{l}) \longrightarrow 2\text{Ln(OH)}_3(\text{s}) \downarrow + 3\text{H}_2(\text{g}) \uparrow
$$

3. **Reaction with Dilute Acids:**
   

$$
2\text{Ln}(\text{s}) + 6\text{H}^+(\text{aq}) \longrightarrow 2\text{Ln}^{3+}(\text{aq}) + 3\text{H}_2(\text{g}) \uparrow
$$

4. **Reaction with Halogens:**
   

$$
2\text{Ln}(\text{s}) + 3\text{X}_2(\text{g}) \longrightarrow 2\text{LnX}_3(\text{s}) \quad (\text{X} = \text{F, Cl, Br, I})
$$

5. **Reaction with Carbon (High Temperature, $> 2500\text{ K}$):**
   

$$
\text{Ln}(\text{s}) + 2\text{C}(\text{s}) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{LnC}_2(\text{s})
$$

### 4.2 Colors, Spectral Properties, and Magnetic Moments (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

#### Origin of Color:
Color in trivalent lanthanoid ions arises from **$f \to f$ electronic transitions**.
* **Laporte Rule:** Transitions within the same subshell ($\Delta l = 0$) are Laporte-forbidden. Consequently, $f \to f$ transitions have low molar absorptivities ($\epsilon < 10\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{cm}^{-1}$), yielding **pale colors**.
* Because $4f$ electrons are shielded from the surrounding ligand field by outer $5s^2 5p^6$ electrons, the absorption bands are **sharp, line-like spectra**, unaffected by ligand geometry.
* **Complementary Pair Rule:** Lanthanoid ions with $n$ electrons in the $4f$ subshell often exhibit colors similar to ions with $(14 - n)$ electrons:

$$
\text{Color}(\text{Pr}^{3+}: 4f^2, \text{green}) \approx \text{Color}(\text{Tm}^{3+}: 4f^{12}, \text{green})
$$

$$
\text{Color}(\text{Nd}^{3+}: 4f^3, \text{lilac/pink}) \approx \text{Color}(\text{Er}^{3+}: 4f^{11}, \text{lilac/pink})
$$

* Ions with $4f^0$ ($\text{La}^{3+}, \text{Ce}^{4+}$) and $4f^{14}$ ($\text{Yb}^{2+}, \text{Lu}^{3+}$) are **colorless**.

#### Magnetic Moments:
Unlike $3d$ transition elements, where orbital angular momentum is quenched by crystal fields (allowing the spin-only formula $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\text{BM}$), the $4f$ orbitals are shielded from external crystal fields. **Spin-orbit coupling is significant**, meaning total angular momentum quantum number ($J$) must be used:

$$
\boxed{\mu_{\text{eff}} = g_J \sqrt{J(J + 1)}\ \mu_{\text{B}}}
$$

Where the Landé $g$-factor is:

$$
g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J + 1) + S(S + 1) - L(L + 1)}{2J(J + 1)}
$$

* $\text{La}^{3+} (4f^0)$ and $\text{Lu}^{3+} (4f^{14})$ are **diamagnetic** ($\mu_{\text{eff}} = 0$).
* All other $\text{Ln}^{3+}$ ions are **paramagnetic**.

### 4.3 Mischmetal (Industrial Alloy) (⭐⭐⭐)
* **Composition:**
  * Lanthanoid metals $\approx 95\%$ ($\sim 50\% \text{ Ce}$, $\sim 25\% \text{ La}$, balance other light rare earths)
  * Iron ($\text{Fe}$) $\approx 5\%$
  * Traces of $\text{S, C, Ca, Al}$.
* **Applications:** Pyrophoric alloy used in cigarette flints, tracer bullets, lighter flints, and projectile shells.

## 5. Comparative Chemistry: Lanthanoids vs. Actinoids (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

| Parameter | Lanthanoids ($4f$-series) | Actinoids ($5f$-series) | Deep Mechanistic Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Electronic Orbitals** | Progressive filling of $4f$ subshell. | Progressive filling of $5f$ subshell. | Higher principal quantum number in actinoids. |
| **Energy Gap** | Energy gap between $4f$ and $5d$ is **large**. | Energy gap between $5f, 6d,$ and $7s$ is **comparable/narrow**. | Narrow gap allows actinoids to utilize $5f, 6d,$ and $7s$ electrons concurrently in bonding. |
| **Oxidation States** | Predominantly $+3$; rarely $+2, +4$. | Shows a broader range: $+3, +4, +5, +6, +7$. | Low binding energy of $5f$ electrons allows participation in bonding up to $\text{Np}(+7)$ and $\text{Pu}(+7)$. |
| **Contraction Magnitude** | Lanthanoid Contraction: smaller per-element decrease. | **Actinoid Contraction is greater** from element to element. | $5f$ orbitals are more diffuse than $4f$, providing **poorer shielding**. |
| **Basic Character of Hydroxides** | Less basic. | **More basic**. | Actinoid ions are larger than corresponding lanthanoid ions with equal oxidation state, so they have lower polarizing power. |
| **Tendency to Form Oxocations** | **Do not form oxocations**. Exist only as simple solvated ions $\text{Ln}^{3+}$. | Readily form stable oxocations: $\text{UO}_2^{2+}, \text{PuO}_2^{2+}, \text{NpO}_2^+$. | High positive oxidation states ($+5, +6$) concentrate high charge densities, stabilizing covalent metal-oxygen $\pi$-bonding. |

### Example 1: [CBSE Board 2024, 3 Marks]
**(a) Why is $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ a strong oxidizing agent even though it has a stable noble gas configuration $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^0$?**  
**(b) Why are $\text{Zr}$ (Zirconium) and $\text{Hf}$ (Hafnium) known as chemical twins?**

**Model Answer / Marking Scheme Breakdown:**
* **(a)** Although $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ acquires an empty, stable noble gas configuration ($[\text{Xe}]\,4f^0$), the most stable and thermodynamically preferred oxidation state for all lanthanoids is **$+3$**. The standard electrode reduction potential:
  

$$
\text{Ce}^{4+}(\text{aq}) + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Ce}^{3+}(\text{aq}) \quad E^\circ = +1.74\text{ V}
$$

  is highly positive, driving $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ to act as a powerful oxidant. **[1.5 Marks]**
* **(b)** Due to **Lanthanoid Contraction**—the intervention of the fourteen $4f$ electrons between $\text{La}$ and $\text{Hf}$ that shield the outer electrons poorly—the atomic radius of $\text{Hf}$ ($159\text{ pm}$) contracts to match that of $\text{Zr}$ ($160\text{ pm}$). Because they have nearly identical ionic radii ($\text{Zr}^{4+} = 80\text{ pm}$, $\text{Hf}^{4+} = 79\text{ pm}$) and identical valence electron configurations, their physical and chemical behaviors are nearly indistinguishable. **[1.5 Marks]**

***

### Example 2: [CBSE Board 2023, 2 Marks]
**State reasons for the following:**  
**(a) Actinoid contraction is greater from element to element than lanthanoid contraction.**  
**(b) $\text{La(OH)}_3$ is more basic than $\text{Lu(OH)}_3$.**

**Model Answer / Marking Scheme Breakdown:**
* **(a)** Actinoid contraction arises from the shielding of $5f$ electrons, whereas lanthanoid contraction arises from $4f$ electrons. Because $5f$ orbitals have a more diffuse spatial distribution than $4f$ orbitals, their **shielding effect is poorer**. Thus, the effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) increases more rapidly across the actinoid series, leading to greater contraction. **[1 Mark]**
* **(b)** Across the lanthanoid series from $\text{La}^{3+}$ to $\text{Lu}^{3+}$, the ionic radius decreases from $106\text{ pm}$ to $86\text{ pm}$. According to Fajans' rule, as cation size decreases at constant charge ($+3$), its polarizing power increases, giving the $\text{Lu}-\text{OH}$ bond more covalent character than the $\text{La}-\text{OH}$ bond. As a result, $\text{La(OH)}_3$ dissociates more readily in aqueous media to release free $\text{OH}^-$ ions and is more basic. **[1 Mark]**

* $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ acts as a strong reducing agent because the $+3$ oxidation state is the dominant and thermodynamically favored state for lanthanoids. By losing an electron:
  

$$
\text{Eu}^{2+} \longrightarrow \text{Eu}^{3+} + e^- \quad (E^\circ_{\text{ox}} = +0.43\text{ V})

  $\text{Eu}^{2+}$ readily oxidizes to $\text{Eu}^{3+}$, acting as an electron donor (reducing agent). **[1 Mark]**

#### Question 12 [CBSE 2020 All India, 2 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]
**What is Lanthanoid Contraction? What is its main cause, and what is its effect on the chemistry of the second and third transition series elements?**

**Model Solution:**
* **Definition:** The steady and progressive decrease in the atomic and ionic radii of lanthanoid elements ($\text{La} \to \text{Lu}$) with increasing atomic number is known as **Lanthanoid Contraction**.
* **Cause:** With each successive element, the additional electron enters the inner $4f$ subshell. The $4f$-orbitals have diffused shapes and offer **imperfect / poor shielding** of the outer electrons from the increasing nuclear charge. Consequently, effective nuclear charge increases and pulls the valence shell inwards.
* **Consequence:** The atomic radii of elements of the $4d$ (second) and $5d$ (third) transition series belonging to the same vertical group become nearly identical (e.g., $\text{Zr} \approx \text{Hf}$, $\text{Nb} \approx \text{Ta}$).

**CBSE Marking Scheme:**
* Definition of steady decrease: $[1/2 \text{ Mark}]$
* Cause (poor shielding of $4f$ electrons): $[1/2 \text{ Mark}]$
* Consequence (identical radii of $4d/5d$ pairs like $\text{Zr/Hf}$): $[1 \text{ Mark}]$

#### Question 13 [CBSE 2024 Delhi Set-1, 3 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

As atomic number increases, the effective nuclear charge increases, binding the remaining $3d$ electrons more tightly. In the first half, reaching half-filled stability ($3d^5$ in $\text{Mn}^{2+}$) also reinforces the $+2$ state.

* (ii) Disproportionation reaction and high hydration enthalpy of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$: $[1 \text{ Mark}]$

**Compare the chemistry of Lanthanoids and Actinoids with respect to:**
**(i) Electronic configuration**
**(ii) Oxidation states**
**(iii) Chemical reactivity**

**Model Solution:**
* **(i) Electronic Configuration:**
  * **Lanthanoids:** Involve filling of $4f$ subshell; general configuration is $[\text{Xe}] 4f^{1-14} 5d^{0-1} 6s^2$.
  * **Actinoids:** Involve filling of $5f$ subshell; general configuration is $[\text{Rn}] 5f^{1-14} 6d^{0-1} 7s^2$. The $5f$ electrons do not shield as effectively as $4f$.
* **(ii) Oxidation States:**
  * **Lanthanoids:** Exhibit primarily $+3$ oxidation state. Occasionally $+2$ and $+4$ are shown only if stable configurations ($f^0, f^7, f^{14}$) are attained (e.g., $\text{Eu}^{2+}, \text{Ce}^{4+}$).
  * **Actinoids:** Exhibit a wider variety of oxidation states ($+3, +4, +5, +6, +7$) because the energy gap between $5f, 6d$, and $7s$ subshells is very narrow.
* **(iii) Chemical Reactivity:**
  * **Lanthanoids:** Moderately reactive; resemble calcium in reactivity.
  * **Actinoids:** Far more reactive, especially when finely divided. They are attacked by boiling water to form oxide and hydride, and react readily with non-metals at moderate temperatures.

**CBSE Marking Scheme:**
* (i) Accurate configuration difference: $[1 \text{ Mark}]$
* (ii) Comparison of oxidation states with energy levels explanation: $[1 \text{ Mark}]$
* (iii) Higher reactivity of actinoids vs lanthanoids: $[1 \text{ Mark}]$

#### Question 16 [CBSE 2022 Term-2, 3 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐
**Give appropriate chemical reasons for the following:**
**(i) Actinoid contraction is greater from element to element than lanthanoid contraction.**
**(ii) $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ is a good analytical oxidizing agent.**
**(iii) Transition metals form a large number of complex compounds.**

**Model Solution:**
* **(i)** $5f$ orbitals extend further in space than $4f$ orbitals and provide **poorer shielding** against the increasing nuclear charge. As a result, the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons increases more rapidly across the actinoid series, making actinoid contraction greater.
* **(ii)** The most stable oxidation state of all lanthanoids is $+3$. Although $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ has a stable noble gas configuration ($4f^0$), it has a strong thermodynamic tendency to gain an electron to reach the $+3$ state ($E^\circ(\text{Ce}^{4+}/\text{Ce}^{3+}) = +1.74\text{ V}$). Hence, it acts as a strong oxidizing agent.
* **(iii)** Transition metals form abundant coordination complexes due to:
  1. **Small cationic size** and **high ionic charge density**.
  2. Availability of **vacant $(n-1)d$ orbitals** of appropriate energy to accept lone pairs from ligands.

**CBSE Marking Scheme:**
* (i) Poorer shielding of $5f$ compared to $4f$: $[1 \text{ Mark}]$
* (ii) Tendency of $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ to revert to dominant $+3$ state ($E^\circ = +1.74\text{ V}$): $[1 \text{ Mark}]$
* (iii) High charge density + vacant $d$-orbitals: $[1 \text{ Mark}]$

#### Question 17 [CBSE Sample Paper 2024 / Board Pattern 2023-24, 4 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]
**Read the passage given below and answer the following questions:**

> Transition elements are characterized by partially filled $d$-orbitals. These elements display characteristic properties such as variable oxidation states, high melting points, formation of coloured ions, magnetic behaviour, and catalytic properties. 
> The ionization enthalpies of transition elements increase across a series, but the increase is not regular. The standard electrode potential values ($E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$) show irregularities across the $3d$ series due to the interplay of enthalpies of sublimation, ionization, and hydration.
> Potassium permanganate ($\text{KMnO}_4$) and potassium dichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$) are two widely used oxidizing agents prepared from the minerals pyrolusite and chromite ore, respectively.

**(a) Why do transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states?**  
**(b) Why is $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ reducing whereas $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ is oxidizing when both have $d^4$ configuration?**  
**(c) [Attempt either sub-part (i) or (ii)]**  
**(i) How does the yellow chromate ion ($\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$) change to orange dichromate ion ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$) with change in pH? Write the balanced equilibrium reaction.**  
*OR*  
**(ii) Write the steps involved in the preparation of potassium permanganate from pyrolusite ore ($\text{MnO}_2$).**

**Model Solution:**

* **(a)** Transition elements exhibit variable oxidation states because the energy difference between $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ orbitals is very small. As a result, both $ns$ and $(n-1)d$ electrons can participate in bond formation.

* **(b)** 
  * $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ ($d^4$) loses an electron to form $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ ($d^3$), which has a stable, half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ configuration in aqueous medium. Thus, it easily acts as a reducing agent.
  * $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ ($d^4$) gains an electron to form $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ ($d^5$), which achieves a stable half-filled $d$-subshell configuration. Thus, it acts as an oxidizing agent.

* **(c) (i)** 
  * In acidic solution, yellow chromate ($\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$) is converted to orange dichromate ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$). In basic solution, orange dichromate reverts to yellow chromate.
  * Balanced reversible equilibrium:
    

$$
2\text{CrO}_4^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

    

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 2\text{OH}^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{CrO}_4^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **OR**

* **(c) (ii)**

$$
2\text{MnO}_2 + 4\text{KOH} + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4\text{ (dark green)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

  * **Step 2: Oxidation of potassium manganate to potassium permanganate:**  
    The green potassium manganate is oxidized electrolytically (or disproportionated in acidic solution):
    

$$
\text{MnO}_4^{2-} \xrightarrow{\text{electrolytic oxidation in alkaline medium}} \text{MnO}_4^-\text{ (purple)} + e^-
$$

    *(Or via chemical disproportionation: $3\text{MnO}_4^{2-} + 4\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{MnO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$)*

#### Question 18 [CBSE 2024 All India, 5 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]
**(a) Account for the following:**
**(i) Of the $d^4$ species, $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ is strongly reducing while $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ is strongly oxidizing.**
**(ii) Cobalt(II) is stable in aqueous solution, but in the presence of strong ligands and complexing agents, it is readily oxidized to Cobalt(III).**
**(iii) The $d^1$ configuration is very unstable in ions of transition metals.**

**(b) Describe the preparation of potassium dichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$) from chromite ore ($\text{FeCr}_2\text{O}_4$). Write all balanced chemical equations.**

**Model Solution:**

* **(a) (i)** $\text{Cr}^{2+} (d^4)$ oxidizes to $\text{Cr}^{3+} (d^3)$, which has a stable half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ crystal field configuration. $\text{Mn}^{3+} (d^4)$ reduces to $\text{Mn}^{2+} (d^5)$, which achieves an extra-stable half-filled $d$-subshell configuration.
* **(a) (ii)** In the presence of strong field ligands, the crystal field splitting energy ($\Delta_\circ$) is very large. This large stabilization energy (CFSE) overcomes the third ionization enthalpy of cobalt, making $\text{Co(III)}$ with low-spin $t_{2g}^6$ (completely filled sub-level) far more stable than $\text{Co(II)}$ ($d^7$).
* **(a) (iii)** An ion with $d^1$ configuration has a strong tendency either to lose this single electron to acquire a stable noble gas configuration (e.g., $\text{Ti}^{3+} \to \text{Ti}^{4+}$) or to undergo disproportionation to more stable states (e.g., $\text{Cr}^{5+}$).

***

* **(b) Preparation of $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ from Chromite Ore:**

  * **Step 1: Fusion of chromite ore with sodium carbonate in excess of air:**  
    Powdered chromite ore is fused with $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ in a reverberatory furnace in the presence of free air to form yellow sodium chromate:
    

$$
4\text{FeCr}_2\text{O}_4 + 8\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 7\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 8\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4 + 2\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 8\text{CO}_2\uparrow
$$

  * **Step 2: Conversion of sodium chromate into sodium dichromate:**  
    The yellow solution of sodium chromate is filtered and acidified with concentrated sulphuric acid to yield orange sodium dichromate:
    

$$
2\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

    Sodium sulphate crystallizes out as $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and is removed.

  * **Step 3: Conversion of sodium dichromate to potassium dichromate:**  
    The orange sodium dichromate solution is treated with potassium chloride ($\text{KCl}$). Due to the lower solubility of $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ compared to $\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$, orange crystals of potassium dichromate separate upon cooling:
    

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 + 2\text{KCl} \longrightarrow \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 + 2\text{NaCl}
$$

#### Question 19 [CBSE 2023 All India / Foreign, 5 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]
**(a) Complete and balance the following chemical equations:**
**(i) $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 3\text{Sn}^{2+} + 14\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow$**  
**(ii) $2\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} + 16\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow$**  
**(iii) $2\text{MnO}_4^- + 10\text{I}^- + 16\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow$**  

**(b) Assign reasons for the following:**
**(i) Transition metals show paramagnetic behaviour.**  
**(ii) The enthalpies of atomization of the transition elements of $5d$ series are higher than those of corresponding elements of $3d$ and $4d$ series.**

**Model Solution:**

* **(a) Balanced Ionic Equations:**
  * **(i)** Oxidation of tin(II) by dichromate:
    

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 3\text{Sn}^{2+} + 14\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{Sn}^{4+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

  * **(ii)** Oxidation of oxalate ion by acidified permanganate:
    

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} + 16\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 10\text{CO}_2\uparrow + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

  * **(iii)** Oxidation of iodide ion by acidified permanganate:
    

$$
2\text{MnO}_4^- + 10\text{I}^- + 16\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{I}_2 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **(b) Reasons:**
  * **(i)** Transition metal atoms/ions possess one or more **unpaired electrons** in their $(n-1)d$ subshells. Each unpaired electron has a permanent magnetic dipole moment arising from its spin and orbital angular momentum, resulting in paramagnetism (attraction to magnetic fields).

This results in a higher effective nuclear charge, smaller atomic sizes, and much stronger metallic bonding involving extensive interatomic $5d\text{-}5d$ orbital overlap compared to $3d$ and $4d$ series. ## 1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

### A. Spin-Only Magnetic Moment Shortcut ($\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\text{ BM}$)
Never calculate square roots manually during the exam. The decimal expansion directly mirrors the number of unpaired electrons ($n$):
* $n = 1 \implies \sqrt{3} \approx \mathbf{1.73}\text{ BM}$
* $n = 2 \implies \sqrt{8} \approx \mathbf{2.83}\text{ BM}$
* $n = 3 \implies \sqrt{15} \approx \mathbf{3.87}\text{ BM}$
* $n = 4 \implies \sqrt{24} \approx \mathbf{4.90}\text{ BM}$
* $n = 5 \implies \sqrt{35} \approx \mathbf{5.92}\text{ BM}$

> **Examiner Tip:** If $\mu = 3.87\text{ BM}$ is given, immediately fix $n = 3$. Do not waste time writing quadratic factorizations.

***

### B. Instant $d^n$ Electronic Configuration Matrix ($3d$ Series)
For any neutral first-row transition element with atomic number $Z = 20 + x$:
* **Total valence electrons:** $(s + d) = x$.
* **Ground state rule:** $\mathrm{Ar}\ 3d^{x-2} 4s^2$ (except $\text{Cr}: 3d^5 4s^1$ and $\text{Cu}: 3d^{10} 4s^1$).
* **Ionization Rule:** Always strip electrons from **$4s$ first**, then $3d$.
  * *Example:* $\text{Fe}^{3+} (Z=26 \implies x=6)$: Neutral is $3d^6 4s^2$. Remove $2$ from $4s$, $1$ from $3d \implies 3d^5$ ($n=5$).

***

### C. Redox Equivalents & Stoichiometry Fast-Path
Memorize $n$-factors (electrons transferred per mole of reagent) to balance equations and calculate molar ratios instantly:

| Reagent | Medium | Product | $n$-factor | Half-Reaction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| $\mathbf{MnO_4^-}$ | Acidic ($\text{H}^+$) | $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ | **5** | $\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5e^- \to \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |
| $\mathbf{MnO_4^-}$ | Neutral / Faintly Alkaline | $\text{MnO}_2$ | **3** | $\text{MnO}_4^- + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 3e^- \to \text{MnO}_2 + 4\text{OH}^-$ |
| $\mathbf{MnO_4^-}$ | Strongly Alkaline | $\text{MnO}_4^{2-}$ | **1** | $\text{MnO}_4^- + e^- \to \text{MnO}_4^{2-}$ |
| $\mathbf{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}$ | Acidic ($\text{H}^+$) | $2\text{Cr}^{3+}$ | **6** | $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6e^- \to 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |

* **Acidic $\text{KMnO}_4$ shortcut:** 
  

$$
\text{Moles of Reductant} \times n_{\text{reductant}} = \text{Moles of } \text{KMnO}_4 \times 5
$$

  * $\text{Fe}^{2+} \to \text{Fe}^{3+} \implies n=1 \implies 5\text{Fe}^{2+} : 1\text{MnO}_4^-$
  * $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \to 2\text{CO}_2 \implies n=2 \implies 5\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} : 2\text{MnO}_4^-$
  * $\text{I}^- \to \text{IO}_3^-$ (*alkaline*) $\implies n=6 \implies 1\text{I}^- : 2\text{MnO}_4^-$ (since $n_{\text{permanganate}}=3$)

## 2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

### Trap 1: Explaining $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$ Values (Step-Marking Decider)
* **The Pitfall:** Writing generic statements like *"because $\text{Cu}$ is less reactive"* or *"due to high ionization energy"*.
* **Evaluation Standard:** You **must** quote the thermodynamic cycle balance:
  

$$
\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ + \Delta_{\text{i}}H_1^\circ + \Delta_{\text{i}}H_2^\circ + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ
$$

* **Why $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}}$ is positive ($+0.34\text{ V}$):** 
  High enthalpy of atomization ($\Delta_{\text{a}}H^\circ$) + high ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{i}}H^\circ$) are **not balanced** by its hydration enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ$).
* **Why $E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}}$ is highly positive ($+1.57\text{ V}$):**
  $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ has stable half-filled $d^5$ configuration; removal of an electron requires breaking extra exchange stability.
* **Why $E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}}$ is negative ($-0.41\text{ V}$):**
  $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ has a stable half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ configuration in aqueous medium.

***

### Trap 2: Disproportionation vs. Acidic/Basic Interconversions
* **Dichromate/Chromate Equilibrium:** This is **not** a redox reaction. Oxidation state of $\text{Cr}$ remains $+6$.
  

$$
2\text{CrO}_4^{2-} \text{ (Yellow)} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} \text{ (Orange)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

  

$$
\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 2\text{OH}^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{CrO}_4^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

  *Deduction:* Students frequently write oxidation/reduction half-equations here. Zero marks awarded.
* **Manganate Disproportionation in Acidic Medium:**
  

$$
3\text{MnO}_4^{2-} + 4\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{MnO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

  *(Oxidation states: $\text{Mn(VI)} \to \text{Mn(VII)} + \text{Mn(IV)}$)*.

***

### Trap 3: Oxidation of Iodide ($\text{I}^-$) by $\text{KMnO}_4$
Read the question medium carefully:
* **In acidic medium:** $\text{I}^- \to \mathbf{I_2}$
  

$$
10\text{I}^- + 2\text{MnO}_4^- + 16\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\text{I}_2 + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **In faintly alkaline / neutral medium:** $\text{I}^- \to \mathbf{IO_3^-}$ (Iodate ion)
  

$$
\text{I}^- + 2\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{MnO}_2 + \mathbf{IO_3^-} + 2\text{OH}^-
$$

  *Deduction:* Writing $\text{I}_2$ for alkaline medium loses full marks for that sub-part.

***

### Trap 4: The Chromyl Chloride Test
* Formation of red vapours of Chromyl chloride ($\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2$):
  

$$
\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 + 4\text{KCl} + 6\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \text{ (conc.)} \to 2\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2 \uparrow + 6\text{KHSO}_4 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Trap:** Chromyl chloride test is **negative** for covalent chlorides ($\text{HgCl}_2, \text{AgCl}, \text{PbCl}_2, \text{SnCl}_2$).

***

### Trap 5: Missing Units & State Symbols
* Magnetic moment must strictly carry the unit **$\text{BM}$** (Bohr Magnetons). Writing $\mu = 3.87$ without $\text{BM}$ results in a $-1/2$ mark deduction.
* When asked for electronic configuration of an ion (e.g., $\text{Cr}^{3+}$), writing $[\text{Ar}] 4s^1 3d^2$ instead of $[\text{Ar}] 3d^3$ loses 1 mark.

## 3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT In-Text Lines & High-Yield Truths)

### A. Anomalous Enthalpy of Atomization & Melting Points
1. **Transition metals have high enthalpies of atomization** due to the presence of both $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ electrons participating in strong interatomic metallic bonding.
2. **$\text{Zn}, \text{Cd}, \text{Hg}$ are soft and volatile:** They have fully filled configurations ($(n-1)d^{10} ns^2$). No unpaired $d$-electrons participate in metallic bonding. $\text{Hg}$ is liquid at room temperature.
3. **Manganese ($\text{Mn}$) and Technetium ($\text{Tc}$) show dips in melting point graphs:** Despite having $d^5$ configuration, the electrons are tightly bound in half-filled shells, resulting in weaker metallic bonding compared to their neighbors ($\text{Cr}$ and $\text{Mo}$).

***

### B. Catalytic Activity & Interstitial Compounds
* **Catalytic mechanism:** Transition metals exhibit catalytic activity because of:
  1. Ability to adopt **variable oxidation states** (e.g., $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$ in $\text{I}^- + \text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}$ oxidation).
  2. Capacity to form complexes and provide a **large surface area** with active sites.
* **Interstitial Compounds Properties:**
  * High melting points (higher than pure metals).
  * Very hard (some borides approach diamond hardness).
  * Retain metallic conductivity.
  * Chemically inert.

***

### C. Lanthanoid & Actinoid Contraction
1. **Cause:** Imperfect shielding of one electron by another in the same subshell ($4f$ and $5f$). **$5f$ shielding is poorer than $4f$** because $5f$ orbitals are more diffuse.
2. **Consequences of Lanthanoid Contraction:**
  * **Similarity of $4d$ and $5d$ atomic radii:** $\text{Zr} (160\text{ pm}) \approx \text{Hf} (159\text{ pm})$; $\text{Nb} \approx \text{Ta}$; $\text{Mo} \approx \text{W}$.
  * **Basic strength decreases across the series:** $\text{La(OH)}_3 > \text{Ce(OH)}_3 > \dots > \text{Lu(OH)}_3$. Because covalent character increases (Fajans' rules) as the size of $\text{Ln}^{3+}$ decreases.
  * Separation of lanthanoids is difficult due to identical chemical properties.

***

### D. Actinoid Chemistry vs. Lanthanoid Chemistry
* **Oxidation States:** Actinoids exhibit a greater range of oxidation states ($+3, +4, +5, +6, +7$) than lanthanoids ($+2, +3, +4$) because **$5f, 6d,$ and $7s$ levels are of comparable energies**.
* **Chemical Reactivity:** Actinoids are much more reactive than lanthanoids; finely divided actinoid metals are pyrophoric.
* **Magnetic properties:** Actinoid magnetic susceptibilities are much more complex to interpret than lanthanoid values due to $5f$ electron delocalization.

### B. High-Yield Reaction Checklist

#### 1. Preparation of Potassium Dichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$) from Chromite Ore
1. **Fusion with Soda Ash in presence of air:**
   

$$
4\text{FeCr}_2\text{O}_4 + 8\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 7\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 8\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4 \text{ (Yellow)} + 2\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 8\text{CO}_2 \uparrow
$$

2. **Acidification:**
   

$$
2\text{Na}_2\text{CrO}_4 + 2\text{H}^+ \to \text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 \text{ (Orange)} + 2\text{Na}^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

3. **Potassium substitution (due to lower solubility of $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$):**
   

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 + 2\text{KCl} \to \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 \downarrow + 2\text{NaCl}
$$

***

#### 2. Preparation of Potassium Permanganate ($\text{KMnO}_4$) from Pyrolusite ($\text{MnO}_2$)
1. **Alkaline Oxidative Fusion:**
   

$$
2\text{MnO}_2 + 4\text{KOH} + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{K}_2\text{MnO}_4 \text{ (Dark Green)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

2. **Electrolytic Oxidation (Industrial) or Acidic Disproportionation (Laboratory):**
   * *Electrolytic:*
     

$$
\text{MnO}_4^{2-} \xrightarrow{\text{Anode}} \text{MnO}_4^- \text{ (Purple)} + e^-
$$

   * *Acidic:*
     

$$
3\text{MnO}_4^{2-} + 4\text{H}^+ \to 2\text{MnO}_4^- + \text{MnO}_2 \downarrow + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$
