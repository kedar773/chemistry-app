### Module 1.1: Ionisation Enthalpies ($\Delta_i H$) of Transition Elements
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
**NCERT Core Premise:** Along a transition series ($3d$), as nuclear charge increases ($Z$), the successive electrons enter the penultimate $(n-1)d$ subshell rather than the valence $ns$ subshell.

```
       Nucleus (Z+)  <--- Shielding by (n-1)d electrons ---> Outer ns^2 electrons
```

#### 1. Fundamental Principles & Trends
* **General Variation:** Along the $3d$ series, $\Delta_i H$ increases from left to right, but the increase is **not as steep** as observed in $s$- and $p$-block elements.
* **Underlying Mechanism:** 
  

$$
\Delta Z_{\text{eff}} = \Delta Z - \Delta \sigma
$$

  As protons are added to the nucleus, electrons enter inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals. The screening effect ($\sigma$) of $d$-electrons on valence $ns$ electrons is imperfect ($\sigma_{s} > \sigma_{p} > \sigma_{d} > \sigma_{f}$). Hence, $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases gradually across the period, contracting the radius and pulling the valence $4s$ electrons inward.

#### 2. Fine Structure of Successive Ionisation Enthalpies
1. **First Ionisation Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1$):**
   * Corresponds to the removal of the first electron from the $4s$ orbital:
     

$$
\text{M}(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_1} \text{M}^+(g) + e^-
$$

   * General slight upward trend, with local maxima at $\text{Cr}$ ($3d^5 4s^1$) and $\text{Cu}$ ($3d^{10} 4s^1$) due to extra stability of half-filled and completely-filled $d$-orbitals, and an abrupt peak at $\text{Zn}$ ($3d^{10} 4s^2$) where an electron must be pulled from a stable, completely filled $4s$ orbital.
2. **Second Ionisation Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_2$):**
   * Corresponds to:
     

$$
\text{M}^+(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_2} \text{M}^{2+}(g) + e^-
$$

   * **Anomalously High Values for $\text{Cr}$ and $\text{Cu}$:**
     * $\text{Cr}^+: [Ar]\,3d^5$: Removal of the second electron disrupts the stable half-filled $d^5$ configuration with high exchange energy ($10K$).
     * $\text{Cu}^+: [Ar]\,3d^{10}$: Disruption of the symmetrical, fully filled $d^{10}$ subshell.
   * **Abnormally Low Value for $\text{Zn}$:** $\text{Zn}^+ ([Ar]\,3d^{10} 4s^1) \to \text{Zn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^{10}) + e^-$. The loss of the $4s^1$ electron yields the stable closed-shell $d^{10}$ system.
3. **Third Ionisation Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_3$):**
   * Corresponds to the transition:
     

$$
\text{M}^{2+}(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_3} \text{M}^{3+}(g) + e^-
$$

   * **Very High Values for $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$:**
     * $\text{Mn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^5)$: Extraordinarily stable half-filled configuration.
     * $\text{Zn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^{10})$: Disruption of closed $d^{10}$ shell requires tremendous energy ($3826\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$). Hence, $\text{Zn}^{3+}$ does not exist.
   * **Unusually Low Value for $\text{Fe}^{2+}$:**
     

$$
\text{Fe}^{2+}([Ar]\,3d^6) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_3} \text{Fe}^{3+}([Ar]\,3d^5) + e^-
$$

     Removing the paired electron eliminates inter-electronic repulsion in the $(d_{xy})^2$ orbital and generates a stable $d^5$ half-filled manifold.

### Module 1.2: Oxidation States
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Frequency)  
**NCERT Core Premise:** Transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states because the energy difference between $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ orbitals is minimal ($\Delta E_{(n-1)d - ns} \approx 0$). Electrons from both subshells participate in chemical bonding.

#### 1. Survey of the First Row ($3d$ Series)

| Element | Valence Config. | Common Oxidation States | Maximum OS | Nature of Bonding in Max OS |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sc** | $3d^1 4s^2$ | $+3$ | $+3$ | Purely ionic / coordinate |
| **Ti** | $3d^2 4s^2$ | $+2, +3, \mathbf{+4}$ | $+4$ | Covalent in $\text{TiO}_2, \text{TiCl}_4$ |
| **V** | $3d^3 4s^2$ | $+2, +3, +4, \mathbf{+5}$ | $+5$ | Covalent in $\text{VO}_4^{3-}, \text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ |
| **Cr** | $3d^5 4s^1$ | $+2, \mathbf{+3}, +4, +5, \mathbf{+6}$ | $+6$ | Covalent in $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ |
| **Mn** | $3d^5 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}, +3, \mathbf{+4}, +6, \mathbf{+7}$ | $+7$ | Strongly covalent in $\text{MnO}_4^-$ |
| **Fe** | $3d^6 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}, \mathbf{+3}, +4, +6$ | $+6$ (unstable $\text{FeO}_4^{2-}$) | Intermediate |
| **Co** | $3d^7 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}, \mathbf{+3}$ | $+4$ | Ionic/Coordination |
| **Ni** | $3d^8 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}$ | $+4$ (in complexes like $[\text{NiF}_6]^{2-}$) | Covalent in $+4$ |
| **Cu** | $3d^{10} 4s^1$ | $+1, \mathbf{+2}$ | $+2$ (stable in aq.) | Ionic & covalent |
| **Zn** | $3d^{10} 4s^2$ | $\mathbf{+2}$ | $+2$ | Ionic |

#### 2. Key Pedagogical Laws & Rationales
1. **Maximum Oxidation State Trend:**
   * Increases up to Manganese ($+7$), where all valence electrons ($5 \times 3d + 2 \times 4s = 7$) are available.
   * Decreases past Manganese because $d$-electrons start pairing up in the degenerate $d$-orbitals, lowering their availability for sharing/loss.
2. **Scandium & Zinc Single Oxidation States:**
   * $\text{Sc}$ shows only $+3$ because loss of all 3 valence electrons ($3d^1 4s^2$) yields noble gas core $[Ar]$.
   * $\text{Zn}$ shows only $+2$ because $3d^{10}$ is completely filled and tightly bound ($Z_{\text{eff}}$ is highest).
3. **Stabilisation by Small, Highly Electronegative Ligands ($\text{F}$ and $\text{O}$):**
   * Maximum oxidation states are reached **only** in fluorides and oxides (e.g., $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$, $\text{OsO}_4$, $\text{VF}_5$, $\text{CrF}_6$ does not form, but $\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$ forms).
   * **Fluorine vs. Oxygen:** Oxygen can stabilize higher oxidation states better than fluorine through multiple bond formation ($p\pi\text{--}d\pi$ back-bonding).
     * $\text{Mn}$ forms $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ (OS $= +7$), but the highest fluoride is only $\text{MnF}_4$ (OS $= +4$).
4. **Acidity/Basicity and Covalency Gradient (Fajans' Rule):**
   * **Low Oxidation State ($+2$):** Lower charge-to-radius ratio $\implies$ predominantly basic and ionic (e.g., $\text{MnO}, \text{CrO}$).
   * **Intermediate Oxidation State ($+3, +4$):** Amphoteric (e.g., $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3, \text{MnO}_2, \text{V}_2\text{O}_5$).
   * **High Oxidation State ($+5, +6, +7$):** High polarising power pulls ligand electron clouds $\implies$ predominantly covalent and acidic (e.g., $\text{CrO}_3, \text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$).

### Module 1.3: Standard Electrode Potentials ($E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$ and $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{3+}/\text{M}^{2+}}$)
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
**NCERT Core Premise:** The standard reduction potential $E^\circ$ is **not** determined by ionisation enthalpy alone; it is the net thermodynamic resultant of three competing energetic terms in the Born-Haber-Fajans cycle.

#### 1. The Born-Haber Thermodynamic Cycle for $\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}$

$$
\begin{array}{rcccl}
\text{M}(s) & \xrightarrow{\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ} & \text{M}(g) & \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ} & \text{M}^{2+}(g) \\
& \searrow_{\Delta_{\text{red}}G^\circ = -nFE^\circ} & & & \downarrow {\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ} \\
& & & & \text{M}^{2+}(aq)
\end{array}
$$

The total enthalpy of transformation ($\Delta H_{\text{total}}$) is:

$$
\Delta H_{\text{total}} = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ + \Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ
$$

Where:
* $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ > 0$ (Endothermic: Sublimation / Atomisation)
* $\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ > 0$ (Endothermic: Ionisation)
* $\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ < 0$ (Exothermic: Hydration Enthalpy)

A metal will have a **more negative** $E^\circ$ if the exothermic hydration energy exceeds or balances the endothermic sublimation and ionisation inputs.

#### 2. Anomalies in $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$ ($3d$ Series)
* **The Unique Positive Value for Copper ($E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} = +0.34\text{ V}$):**
  * The sum of $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ$ ($+339\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) and the two ionisation energies $\Delta_i H_1^\circ + \Delta_i H_2^\circ$ ($+745 + 1960 = +2705\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) is **not** compensated by the hydration enthalpy $\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ$ of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ($-2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).
  * Net $\Delta H > 0 \implies \Delta G^\circ > 0$ for reduction of $\text{H}^+(aq)$. Thus, copper cannot liberate $\text{H}_2(g)$ from dilute non-oxidising acids like $\text{HCl}$.
* **More Negative $E^\circ$ Peaks at $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$:**
  * $\text{Mn}^{2+}$: Half-filled $d^5$ configuration confers low second ionisation enthalpy relative to third, producing an unusually negative $E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{2+}/\text{Mn}} = -1.18\text{ V}$.
  * $\text{Zn}^{2+}$: Completely filled $d^{10}$ shell makes removal of $4s^2$ favourable and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ stable $\implies E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76\text{ V}$.

#### 3. Trends in $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{3+}/\text{M}^{2+}}$ Redox Couples
1. **$E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}} = +1.57\text{ V}$ (Highly Positive / Strong Oxidising Agent):**
   * $\text{Mn}^{3+} ([Ar]\,3d^4) + e^- \to \text{Mn}^{2+} ([Ar]\,3d^5)$. The transition yields the extraordinarily stable half-filled $d^5$ configuration. Thus, $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ readily reduces itself.
2. **$E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}} = -0.41\text{ V}$ (Negative / Strong Reducing Agent):**
   * In aqueous medium, $d$-orbitals split into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ sub-levels under an octahedral crystal field.
   * $\text{Cr}^{3+} ([Ar]\,3d^3)$ has the half-filled lower-energy manifold $(t_{2g})^3$, providing high crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE). Thus, $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ readily oxidises to $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ ($E^\circ_{\text{ox}} = +0.41\text{ V}$).
3. **$E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}} = +0.77\text{ V}$ (Moderate):**
   * $\text{Fe}^{3+} ([Ar]\,3d^5)$ is stable in the gas phase, but $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ has significant hydration stability. Hence, $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is a mild oxidising agent.

### Module 1.4: Chemical Reactivity & Disproportionation
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
* **Reactivity with Non-oxidising vs Oxidising Acids:**
  * Metals with negative $E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$ (Ti to Fe) react with non-oxidising dilute acids ($\text{HCl}, \text{dil. H}_2\text{SO}_4$) to liberate $\text{H}_2(g)$:
    

$$
\text{Fe}(s) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \to \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)
$$

  * Metals with positive $E^\circ$ (Cu) require oxidising acids ($\text{HNO}_3$, conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$):
    

$$
3\text{Cu} + 8\text{HNO}_3(\text{dil.}) \to 3\text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO} \uparrow + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

    

$$
\text{Cu} + 4\text{HNO}_3(\text{conc.}) \to \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO}_2 \uparrow + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Disproportionation of Copper(I) in Aqueous Solution:**
  

$$
\mathbf{2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)}
$$

  * *Thermodynamic Justification:* Although $\text{Cu}^+$ has a stable $d^{10}$ configuration, the divalent $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ion has a much higher charge density ($q/r$). The much larger negative hydration enthalpy of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ \approx -2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) more than compensates for the second ionisation enthalpy of copper ($\Delta_i H_2^\circ = +1960\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).

### Module 1.5: Magnetic Properties
**Board Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
**NCERT Core Premise:** The magnetic behaviour of transition metal complexes arises from unpaired electrons in the $(n-1)d$ orbitals.

#### 1. Classification
* **Diamagnetic Substances:** Contain **no** unpaired electrons ($n = 0$). Weakly repelled by an applied magnetic field. Examples: $\text{Sc}^{3+} (d^0)$, $\text{Ti}^{4+} (d^0)$, $\text{Zn}^{2+} (d^{10})$, $\text{Cu}^+ (d^{10})$.
* **Paramagnetic Substances:** Contain one or more unpaired electrons ($n \ge 1$). Attracted into an applied magnetic field. The magnitude of paramagnetism increases directly with the number of unpaired electrons.
* **Ferromagnetic Substances:** Unpaired electron spins align cooperatively in domains, producing permanent macroscopic magnetisation (e.g., solid $\text{Fe, Co, Ni}$).

#### 2. The Spin-Only Magnetic Moment Formula
In the $3d$ series, the orbital angular momentum ($L$) is largely quenched by the electrostatic field of surrounding ligands ("orbital quenching"). Thus, the magnetic moment ($\mu$) is determined almost entirely by electron spin:

$$
\mu_s = \sqrt{n(n + 2)}\quad \text{BM}
$$

Where:
* $n$ = Number of unpaired electrons
* $\text{BM}$ = Bohr Magneton = $\dfrac{eh}{4\pi m_e c} = 9.274 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J T}^{-1}$ (or $\text{A m}^2$)

### Derivation 2.1: Disproportionation Feasibility of $\text{Cu}^+(aq)$ via Cell Potentials
We evaluate the standard potential for the disproportionation reaction:

$$
2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

Given standard reduction data:
1. $\text{Cu}^+(aq) + e^- \to \text{Cu}(s) \quad E^\circ_1 = +0.52\text{ V}$ (Cathode / Reduction)
2. $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + e^- \to \text{Cu}^+(aq) \quad E^\circ_2 = +0.15\text{ V}$ (Anode / Oxidation is reverse)

#### Step-by-Step Calculation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} &= E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} \\
&= E^\circ_{(\text{Cu}^+/\text{Cu})} - E^\circ_{(\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}^+)} \\
&= (+0.52\text{ V}) - (+0.15\text{ V}) \\
&= \mathbf{+0.37\text{ V}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Using Gibbs Free Energy relation:

$$
\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}
$$

For $n = 1\text{ mol } e^-$ transferred per mol of reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^\circ &= -(1)(96485\text{ C mol}^{-1})(+0.37\text{ V}) \\
&= -35699\text{ J mol}^{-1} \\
&= \mathbf{-35.70\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta G^\circ = -35.70\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} < 0 \implies \text{Spontaneous Disproportionation in Aqueous Media.}}
$$

### Derivation 2.2: Thermodynamic Energy Balance of $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)$ vs $\text{Cu}^+(aq)$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Step 1: } & \text{Cu}^+(g) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(g) + e^- & \Delta_i H_2^\circ &= +1960\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
\text{Step 2: } & \text{Cu}^{2+}(g) + aq \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) & \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{2+}) &= -2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \\
\text{Step 3: } & \text{Cu}^{+}(g) + aq \to \text{Cu}^{+}(aq) & \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{+}) &\approx -580\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The net enthalpy difference favoring oxidation of gaseous $\text{Cu}^+$ to aqueous $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ over hydrating $\text{Cu}^+$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H_{\text{net}} &= \Delta_i H_2^\circ + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{2+}) - \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ(\text{Cu}^{+}) \\
&= (+1960) + (-2121) - (-580) \\
&= \mathbf{+419\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}} \text{ released (exothermic relative shift)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Hydration enthalpy of }\text{Cu}^{2+}\text{ completely compensates for its high second ionisation enthalpy.}}
$$

### Question 1 [CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
**(a)** Why is $E^\circ$ for $\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}$ couple much more positive than that for $\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}$ or $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$?  
**(b)** Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment of $\text{Co}^{2+}(aq)$ ($Z = 27$).

**Solution:**
* **(a)** 
  1. For $\text{Mn}$: The electronic configuration of $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ is $[Ar]\,3d^5$ (half-filled, symmetrically stable, large exchange energy), whereas $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ is $[Ar]\,3d^4$. Reduction of $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ to $\text{Mn}^{2+}$:
     

$$
\text{Mn}^{3+}(3d^4) + e^- \to \text{Mn}^{2+}(3d^5)
$$

     proceeds with great thermodynamic ease. Thus, $E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}} = +1.57\text{ V}$ is highly positive.
  2. For $\text{Cr}$: In aqueous solution, $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ has $[Ar]\,3d^3$, which corresponds to a stable half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ subshell under octahedral crystal field splitting. Hence, $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ resists reduction, making $E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}}$ negative ($-0.41\text{ V}$).
  3. For $\text{Fe}$: $\text{Fe}^{3+} (3d^5) \to \text{Fe}^{2+} (3d^6)$. $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ is already half-filled; hence its reduction does not experience the same driving force as $\text{Mn}^{3+}$.
* **(b)** For Cobalt ($Z = 27$):
  * Ground state atom: $[Ar]\,3d^7 4s^2$
  * Divalent cation $\text{Co}^{2+}$: $[Ar]\,3d^7$
  * Orbital filling:
    

$$
d_{xy}^{\uparrow\downarrow} \quad d_{yz}^{\uparrow\downarrow} \quad d_{zx}^{\uparrow} \quad d_{x^2-y^2}^{\uparrow} \quad d_{z^2}^{\uparrow}
$$

    Number of unpaired electrons $n = 3$.
  * Applying the spin-only formula:
    

$$
\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = \sqrt{3(3+2)} = \sqrt{15}\text{ BM} \approx \mathbf{3.87\text{ BM}}
$$

### Question 2 [CBSE 2023, 2 Marks]
Account for the following:
1. $\text{Cu}^+$ salts are generally colourless and diamagnetic, whereas $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ salts are coloured and paramagnetic.
2. Highest oxidation state of a transition metal is exhibited in its oxides and fluorides only.

**Solution:**
1. **$\text{Cu}^+$ ($Z=29$):** Electronic configuration is $[Ar]\,3d^{10}$. All five $3d$-orbitals are completely paired ($n = 0$). No unpaired electrons are available, so it is **diamagnetic** ($\mu = 0\text{ BM}$). Since $d$-orbitals are completely filled, $d\text{--}d$ electron transitions cannot occur under visible light, rendering it **colourless**.  
   **$\text{Cu}^{2+}$:** Electronic configuration is $[Ar]\,3d^9$. It has **one unpaired electron** ($n = 1$), which makes it **paramagnetic** ($\mu = 1.73\text{ BM}$). It absorbs light from the visible region for $d\text{--}d$ transition (promoting an electron into the vacancy in the $e_g$ set), rendering it **coloured** (characteristic blue in aqueous solution).
2. **Fluorine and Oxygen** are the most electronegative elements with very small ionic radii. They possess high lattice enthalpies (with metal cations) and high bond energies, or allow extensive $p\pi\text{--}d\pi$ multiple bonding (in the case of oxygen), enabling the metal to release all valence electrons without energetic penalty.

### Question 3 [CBSE 2022, Term-II, 3 Marks]
Explain the following observations:
**(a)** $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ is a reducing agent, while $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ is an oxidising agent, though both have $d^4$ configuration.  
**(b)** The lowest oxide of transition metal is basic, whereas the highest oxide is amphoteric or acidic.

**Solution:**
* **(a)** Both $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ have $d^4$ configuration ($[Ar]\,3d^4$).
  * When $\text{Cr}^{2+}$ acts as a reducing agent, it loses an electron to become $\text{Cr}^{3+}$:
    

$$
\text{Cr}^{2+}(d^4) \to \text{Cr}^{3+}(d^3) + e^-
$$

    In aqueous solution, $d^3$ achieves the **stable half-filled $t_{2g}^3$** configuration (due to octahedral crystal field splitting of the $d$-manifold). Hence, oxidation is spontaneous ($E^\circ_{\text{Cr}^{3+}/\text{Cr}^{2+}} = -0.41\text{ V}$).
  * When $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ acts as an oxidising agent, it gains an electron to become $\text{Mn}^{2+}$:
    

$$
\text{Mn}^{3+}(d^4) + e^- \to \text{Mn}^{2+}(d^5)
$$

    This yields the **stable half-filled $d^5$** configuration, which has high exchange energy. Hence, reduction is spontaneous ($E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{3+}/\text{Mn}^{2+}} = +1.57\text{ V}$).
* **(b)** In lower oxidation states (e.g., $\text{MnO}$ where $\text{Mn}$ is $+2$), the metal has a lower charge-to-radius ratio and low electronegativity. It transfers electrons easily to oxygen, forming predominantly **ionic bonds**; hence it readily dissolves/reacts as a **base**.  
  In high oxidation states (e.g., $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ where $\text{Mn}$ is $+7$), the small, highly charged cation has huge polarising power (Fajans' Rule). It pulls electron density away from the oxygen atoms, forming **covalent bonds**. In water, these oxides act as Lewis acids or form strong oxoacids ($\text{HMnO}_4$).

### Question 4 [CBSE 2021, Compartment / Sample Practice, 2 Marks]
Why is the third ionisation enthalpy of Manganese ($Z=25$) anomalously high compared to that of Iron ($Z=26$)?

**Solution:**
* **Manganese ($Z = 25$):** 
  * $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ configuration: $[Ar]\,3d^5$
  * Removal of the third electron requires disrupting the stable, half-filled $3d^5$ subshell, which has maximum exchange energy:
    

$$
\Delta_i H_3(\text{Mn}) = 3252\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

* **Iron ($Z = 26$):**
  * $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ configuration: $[Ar]\,3d^6$
  * Removal of the third electron removes the paired electron from the $3d$ orbital:
    

$$
\text{Fe}^{2+}(3d^6) \xrightarrow{\Delta_i H_3} \text{Fe}^{3+}(3d^5) + e^-
$$

  * This relieves electron-electron repulsion and yields the extra-stable half-filled $3d^5$ configuration:
    

$$
\Delta_i H_3(\text{Fe}) = 2957\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

* Therefore, $\Delta_i H_3(\text{Mn}) \gg \Delta_i H_3(\text{Fe})$.

### Question 5 [CBSE 2020, 3 Marks]
Give reasons for the following:
**(a)** Transition metals show catalytic properties.  
**(b)** Copper(I) ion is not stable in aqueous solutions.  
**(c)** $\text{Zn}$ has the lowest enthalpy of atomisation in the $3d$ series ($\Delta_a H^\circ = 126\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).

**Solution:**
* **(a)** Catalytic activity is due to:
  1. Availability of variable oxidation states to form unstable reaction intermediates with reactants.
  2. Vacant $d$-orbitals that can coordinate with reactant molecules, lowering the activation energy ($E_a$).
  3. Large surface area for physical adsorption of reactants.
* **(b)** $\text{Cu}^+(aq)$ undergoes disproportionation:
  

$$
2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

  Because the high hydration energy of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ($\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ = -2121\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) compensates for the energy required to remove the second electron ($\Delta_i H_2^\circ = +1960\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$).
* **(c)** Enthalpy of atomisation depends directly on the strength of metallic bonding, which is governed by the number of unpaired $d$-electrons involved in interatomic metallic bonding. Zinc has the configuration $3d^{10} 4s^2$, with **no unpaired $d$-electrons**. Metallic bonding is weak, resulting in the lowest atomisation enthalpy.

## 4. EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

```
              ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
              │           COMMON EXAM PITFALLS & PREVENTIONS            │
              └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   [TRAP] Writing "Cu+ is unstable because of d10 configuration"
   [FIX]  Cu+ has a stable d10 config, but in WATER it is unstable due to 
          hydration enthalpy of Cu2+ driving disproportionation.
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   [TRAP] Confusing Cr3+ stability origin (Writing "half-filled d5")
   [FIX]  Cr3+ is d3, which is half-filled t2g in aqueous crystal field.
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   [TRAP] Missing units on Magnetic Moment
   [FIX]  Always suffix with "BM" (Bohr Magnetons). e.g., 3.87 BM.
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   [TRAP] Sign confusion on E°cell for disproportionation
   [FIX]  E°cell = E°(cathode) - E°(anode). Cu+/Cu is reduction (+0.52 V), 
          Cu2+/Cu+ is oxidation (+0.15 V). E°cell = +0.37 V (Spontaneous).
```

* **Trap 1: The "Half-Filled Fallacy" for $\text{Cr}^{3+}$:**
  * *Student Error:* Writing that $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ is stable because it has a "half-filled $d^5$ configuration".
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ is **$3d^3$**. In aqueous solution, the octahedral ligand field splits the $d$-orbitals into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$. A $3d^3$ ion has a **half-filled $t_{2g}^3$ level**, which provides extra crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE).
* **Trap 2: Ignoring Hydration Enthalpy when Discussing $E^\circ$:**
  * *Student Error:* Explaining $E^\circ$ trends using only ionisation enthalpy.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* Zero marks are given if $E^\circ$ is explained without stating that $E^\circ$ is the net sum of $\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ$, $\sum \Delta_i H^\circ$, and $\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ$.
* **Trap 3: Forgetting Units for Magnetic Moment:**
  * *Student Error:* Writing $\mu = 3.87$.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* Writing the number without **$\text{BM}$** (Bohr Magneton) can lead to a 0.5-mark deduction.
* **Trap 4: Forgetting the Disproportionation Equation for $\text{Cu}^+$:**
  * *Student Error:* Writing $\text{Cu}^+ \to \text{Cu}^{2+} + e^-$.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* Disproportionation **must** be written as a balanced auto-redox equation:
    

$$
2\text{Cu}^+(aq) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)
$$

* **Trap 5: Electron Removal Order (The $4s$ vs $3d$ Trap):**
  * *Student Error:* Writing $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ as $3d^4 4s^2$.
  * *Marking Scheme Correction:* During ionisation, electrons are **always removed from the outer $4s$ orbital first**, not $3d$. Therefore, $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is $[Ar]\,3d^6 4s^0$.

## 5. SPEED HACKS & GOLDEN FORMULAS

### 1. Rapid Spin-Only Value Lookup Shortcut
To avoid computing square roots ($\sqrt{n(n+2)}$) during time-constrained exams:

$$
\mathbf{\mu \approx n \boldsymbol{.} [n+7]\text{ to } n \boldsymbol{.} [n+8]\text{ BM}}
$$

* $n = 1 \implies \sqrt{3} = \mathbf{1.73\text{ BM}}$
* $n = 2 \implies \sqrt{8} = \mathbf{2.83\text{ BM}}$
* $n = 3 \implies \sqrt{15} = \mathbf{3.87\text{ BM}}$
* $n = 4 \implies \sqrt{24} = \mathbf{4.90\text{ BM}}$
* $n = 5 \implies \sqrt{35} = \mathbf{5.92\text{ BM}}$

> **Mnemonic:** The calculated magnetic moment in Bohr Magnetons always starts with the integer value equal to the number of unpaired electrons $n$ (i.e. $n.\dots\text{ BM}$). If $n=3$, $\mu$ must be $3.\text{87 BM}$.

### 2. High Oxidation State Oxides/Fluorides Summary Chart

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Oxides: } & \text{Sc}_2\text{O}_3 (+3) \to \text{TiO}_2 (+4) \to \text{V}_2\text{O}_5 (+5) \to \text{CrO}_3 (+6) \to \mathbf{Mn_2O_7 (+7)} \\
\text{Fluorides: } & \text{ScF}_3 (+3) \to \text{TiF}_4 (+4) \to \text{VF}_5 (+5) \to \text{CrF}_6\text{ (unstable)} \to \mathbf{MnF_4 (+4)}
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Key Rule:** Oxygen stabilizes the $+7$ state in $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ through **$p\pi\text{--}d\pi$ multiple bonding**, whereas fluorine can only form single bonds and cannot sterically accommodate 7 fluorines around $\text{Mn}$.
