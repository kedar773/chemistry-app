## 1. Pedagogical Theory & Foundational Concepts

Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) and haloarenes (aryl halides) are hydrocarbons in which one or more hydrogen atoms have been replaced by halogen atoms ($X = \text{F, Cl, Br, I}$). 

* **Haloalkanes**: Contain halogen atom(s) attached to the $sp^3$-hybridized carbon atom of an alkyl group ($R-X$). General formula for monohaloalkanes: $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}X$.
* **Haloarenes**: Contain halogen atom(s) attached directly to $sp^2$-hybridized carbon atom(s) of an aromatic ring ($\text{Ar}-X$).

***

### 1.1 Classification of Halogen Derivatives ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Halogen derivatives are classified according to two fundamental criteria:

```
                          Halogen Derivatives
                                   │
         ┌─────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                   ▼
Based on Number of Halogen Atoms                Based on Carbon Hybridization
(Mono, Di, Tri, Tetra/Polyhalo)                             │
                                   ┌─────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┐
                                   ▼                                                   ▼
                       Compounds containing $sp^3$ C-X                     Compounds containing $sp^2$ C-X
                       ├── Alkyl halides (1°, 2°, 3°)                      ├── Vinylic halides
                       ├── Allylic halides                                 └── Aryl halides
                       └── Benzylic halides (1°, 2°, 3°)
```

#### A. Based on the Number of Halogen Atoms
1. **Monohaloalkanes / Monohaloarenes**: Contain one halogen atom.
   

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{Cl} \quad (\text{Chloroethane}), \quad \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} \quad (\text{Chlorobenzene})
$$

2. **Dihaloalkanes**: Contain two halogen atoms.
   * **Geminal dihalides (Alkylidene halides)**: Both halogen atoms are bonded to the **same** carbon atom.
     

$$
\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}X_2 \quad (\text{e.g., } \text{CH}_3\text{CHBr}_2 : \text{ethylidene bromide / 1,1-dibromoethane})
$$

   * **Vicinal dihalides (Alkylene dihalides)**: Halogen atoms are bonded to two **adjacent** carbon atoms.
     

$$
\text{CH}_2X-\text{CH}_2X \quad (\text{e.g., } \text{CH}_2\text{Cl}-\text{CH}_2\text{Cl} : \text{ethylene dichloride / 1,2-dichloroethane})
$$

3. **Polyhaloalkanes**: Trihaloalkanes ($\text{CHX}_3$, e.g., chloroform $\text{CHCl}_3$), tetrahaloalkanes ($\text{CX}_4$, e.g., carbon tetrachloride $\text{CCl}_4$).

#### B. Compounds Containing $sp^3\text{ C}-X$ Bond
1. **Alkyl halides or Haloalkanes ($R-X$)**:
   The halogen atom is bonded to an alkyl group. Depending on the nature of the carbon atom bearing the halogen, they are sub-classified as:
   * **Primary ($1^\circ$)**: Halogen bonded to a primary carbon ($R-\text{CH}_2-X$).
   * **Secondary ($2^\circ$)**: Halogen bonded to a secondary carbon ($R_2\text{CH}-X$).
   * **Tertiary ($3^\circ$)**: Halogen bonded to a tertiary carbon ($R_3\text{C}-X$).
2. **Allylic Halides**:
   Halogen is bonded to an $sp^3$-hybridized carbon atom adjacent to an aliphatic carbon-carbon double bond (i.e., at the *allylic position*):
   

$$
\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\text{CH}_2-X
$$

3. **Benzylic Halides**:
   Halogen is bonded to an $sp^3$-hybridized carbon atom attached directly to an aromatic ring:
   

$$
\text{Ar}-\text{CH}_2-X \quad (1^\circ), \quad \text{Ar}-\text{CH}(R)-X \quad (2^\circ), \quad \text{Ar}-\text{C}(R)(R')-X \quad (3^\circ)
$$

#### C. Compounds Containing $sp^2\text{ C}-X$ Bond
1. **Vinylic Halides**:
   Halogen is bonded directly to one of the $sp^2$-hybridized carbon atoms of an aliphatic double bond:
   

$$
\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-X
$$

2. **Aryl Halides**:
   Halogen is bonded directly to an $sp^2$-hybridized carbon atom of an aromatic ring:
   

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5-X
$$

### 1.2 IUPAC and Common Nomenclature ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In IUPAC nomenclature, halogen derivatives are named as halo-substituted hydrocarbons. Halogens are **always considered as substituent prefixes** (fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-), never as principal suffixes.

#### Priority Rules & Locant Assignment
1. **Longest Continuous Carbon Chain**: Must contain the principal functional group or multiple bonds (if present), regardless of whether it gives a shorter chain than a pure alkane skeleton.
2. **Lowest Locant Rule**:
   * If only halogen substituents and alkyl chains are present, number from the end that gives lower numbers at the first point of difference.
   * If locants are identical from both ends, assign the lower number based on **alphabetical order**:
     

$$
\overset{1}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3-\overset{2}{\text{CH}}(\text{Cl})-\overset{3}{\text{CH}}(\text{Br})-\overset{4}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3 \implies \text{2-Bromo-3-chlorobutane (not 3-bromo-2-chlorobutane)}
$$

   * Double/triple bonds have **higher nomenclature priority** over halogen substituents:
     

$$
\overset{4}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3-\overset{3}{\text{CH}}(\text{Cl})-\overset{2}{\text{CH}}=\overset{1}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 \implies \text{3-Chlorobut-1-ene}
$$

3. **Aryl Halides**: Ortho ($o$-), Meta ($m$-), and Para ($p$-) are used in trivial/common systems; numerical locants ($1,2$-; $1,3$-; $1,4$-) are required in IUPAC nomenclature.

### 1.3 Nature of the $\text{C}-X$ Bond ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The carbon-halogen bond is formed by the overlap of an $sp^3$ (or $sp^2$) hybrid orbital of carbon with a singly occupied $p$-orbital of the halogen atom.

#### 1. Bond Polarity and Partial Charges
Halogen atoms are significantly more electronegative than carbon (Pauling scale: $\text{F}=4.0$, $\text{Cl}=3.0$, $\text{Br}=2.8$, $\text{I}=2.5$, $\text{C}=2.5$). 
Consequently, the electron cloud of the $\text{C}-X$ $\sigma$-bond is shifted toward the halogen atom, inducing a permanent dipole:

$$
\overset{\delta+}{\text{C}} \quad \boldsymbol{-\!\!\!-\!\!\!-} \quad \overset{\delta-}{X}
$$

This carbon center is **electrophilic** ($\delta+$), making haloalkanes susceptible to attack by electron-rich **nucleophiles** ($\text{Nu}^-$).

#### 2. Periodic Trends in $\text{C}-X$ Bond Parameters (NCERT Benchmark Data)

As one moves down Group 17 from Fluorine to Iodine:
* Halogen atomic radius increases: $\text{F} < \text{Cl} < \text{Br} < \text{I}$
* Overlap efficiency between carbon ($2s/2p$) and halogen valence orbitals ($2p, 3p, 4p, 5p$) decreases.
* Bond length increases, and bond dissociation enthalpy decreases.

$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Compound & Bond Length / \text{pm} & Bond Enthalpy / \text{kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} & Dipole Moment / \text{D} \\
\hline
\text{CH}_3-\text{F} & 139 & 452 & 1.847 \\
\text{CH}_3-\text{Cl} & 178 & 351 & 1.860 \\
\text{CH}_3-\text{Br} & 193 & 293 & 1.830 \\
\text{CH}_3-\text{I} & 214 & 234 & 1.636 \\
\hline
\end{array}
$$

#### The $\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ vs $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$ Dipole Moment Anomaly (Critical Board Concept)
Dipole moment ($\mu$) is defined as:

$$
\mu = q \times d
$$

where $q$ is the magnitude of the partial charges and $d$ is the internuclear bond distance.

* Electronegativity trend: $\text{F} > \text{Cl} \implies q(\text{C}-\text{F}) > q(\text{C}-\text{Cl})$
* Bond length trend: $d(\text{C}-\text{Cl}) = 178\text{ pm} \gg d(\text{C}-\text{F}) = 139\text{ pm}$
* In $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$, the substantially larger bond length ($d$) overcompensates for the slightly smaller charge separation ($q$). 

$$
\boxed{\mu(\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}) \ [1.860\text{ D}] > \mu(\text{CH}_3\text{F}) \ [1.847\text{ D}] > \mu(\text{CH}_3\text{Br}) \ [1.830\text{ D}] > \mu(\text{CH}_3\text{I}) \ [1.636\text{ D}]}
$$

### 1.4 Comparative Analysis: $\text{C}-X$ in Haloalkanes vs Haloarenes ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Aryl halides and vinyl halides are **exceptionally unreactive** toward nucleophilic substitution reactions compared to alkyl halides under ordinary conditions. Four fundamental factors explain this phenomenon:

```
                Factors for Low Reactivity of Aryl Halides
                                   │
      ┌────────────────┬───────────┴───────────┬────────────────┐
      ▼                ▼                       ▼                ▼
Resonance Effect  Hybridization          Instability of    Electrostatic
(+R Effect)       State of Carbon        Phenyl Cation     Repulsion
(Partial double   (sp² vs sp³)           (Unstable via     (π-electron cloud
 bond character)   (shorter, stronger)    self-ionization)  repels nucleophile)
```

#### 1. Resonance Effect ($+R$ Effect)
In haloarenes, the lone pair of electrons on the halogen atom is conjugated with the $\pi$-electrons of the aromatic ring. The $+R$ delocalization creates partial double bond character in the $\text{C}-X$ bond:

```
    :X:                      :X:+                    :X:+                    :X:+
   /   \                    /   \\                  /   \                   //   \
  |  ○  |   <----->        |     |   <----->       |     |   <----->       |     |
   \   /                    \   //                  \\  /                   \   /
                             (-)                     (-)                     (-)
```

$$
\text{Canonical Forms I, II, III, IV show a partial double bond between carbon and halogen.}
$$

Because of this partial double bond character, the carbon-chlorine bond in chlorobenzene ($169\text{ pm}$) is shorter and significantly stronger than in chloroalkanes ($178\text{ pm}$), making cleaving via nucleophilic substitution challenging at room temperature.

#### 2. Difference in Hybridization of Carbon in the $\text{C}-X$ Bond
* In alkyl halides: $\text{C}$ is $sp^3$-hybridized ($25\%$ $s$-character).
* In aryl/vinyl halides: $\text{C}$ is $sp^2$-hybridized ($33.3\%$ $s$-character).

$$
\% \text{ } s\text{-character} \propto \text{Electronegativity of carbon} \propto \text{Orbital contraction}
$$

The $sp^2$ carbon holds the shared pair of electrons more tightly toward itself, shortening the bond length ($169\text{ pm}$ vs $178\text{ pm}$) and increasing the bond dissociation energy.

#### 3. Instability of the Phenyl Cation
If an aryl halide undergoes unimolecular heterolysis ($S_N1$), it forms a **phenyl cation** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5^+$):

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5-\text{Cl} \overset{\text{slow}}{\centernot\longrightarrow} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5^+ + \text{Cl}^-
$$

In the phenyl cation, the vacant $sp^2$ orbital is oriented perpendicular to the aromatic $\pi$-system. It cannot be stabilized by resonance delocalization of the ring's $\pi$-electrons. Therefore, $S_N1$ mechanism is ruled out.

#### 4. Electronic Repulsion
The approaching nucleophile (an electron-rich species with lone pairs or negative charge) experiences electrostatic repulsion from the $\pi$-electron cloud of the benzene ring.

## 2. Step-by-Step Mechanisms and Quantitative Derivations

### 2.1 Quantitative Vector Dipole Derivations for Polyhaloalkanes and Isomers

The net molecular dipole moment ($\vec{\mu}_{\text{net}}$) is the vector sum of individual bond moments ($\vec{\mu}_i$):

$$
\vec{\mu}_{\text{net}} = \sum_{i} \vec{\mu}_i
$$

#### Case 1: Carbon Tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) vs Chloroform ($\text{CHCl}_3$)
* In $\text{CCl}_4$, carbon adopts an ideal tetrahedral geometry ($T_d$ symmetry, bond angles $109.5^\circ$).
  

$$
\vec{\mu}_{\text{net}} = \vec{\mu}_{(\text{C}-\text{Cl}_1)} + \vec{\mu}_{(\text{C}-\text{Cl}_2)} + \vec{\mu}_{(\text{C}-\text{Cl}_3)} + \vec{\mu}_{(\text{C}-\text{Cl}_4)}
$$

  The vector resultant of three $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bonds is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the fourth $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond:
  

$$
\vec{\mu}_{1,2,3} = -\vec{\mu}_4 \implies \boxed{\vec{\mu}_{\text{net}}(\text{CCl}_4) = 0.00\text{ D}}
$$

* In $\text{CHCl}_3$, the three bond dipoles of $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ pull in one general direction, while the $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bond dipole also points toward carbon ($\text{C}$ is marginally more electronegative than $\text{H}$):
  

$$
\vec{\mu}_{\text{net}}(\text{CHCl}_3) \neq 0 \implies \boxed{\mu = 1.04\text{ D}}
$$

```
       Cl                              H
       |                               |
       C                               C
     / | \                           / | \
   Cl  Cl Cl                       Cl  Cl Cl
  Net Dipole = 0 D              Net Dipole = 1.04 D
 (Bond vectors cancel)         (Bond vectors reinforce)
```

#### Case 2: Dichlorobenzene Isomers
The bond angle between two $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ dipoles ($\mu_0 \approx 1.57\text{ D}$) determines the resultant dipole:

$$
\mu_{\text{isomer}} = \sqrt{\mu_0^2 + \mu_0^2 + 2\mu_0^2\cos\theta} = \mu_0\sqrt{2(1 + \cos\theta)} = 2\mu_0\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
$$

1. **$o$-Dichlorobenzene ($\theta = 60^\circ$)**:
   

$$
\mu_o = 2\mu_0\cos(30^\circ) = 2\mu_0 \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = \sqrt{3}\mu_0 \approx 1.73 \times 1.57 \approx 2.54\text{ D}
$$

2. **$m$-Dichlorobenzene ($\theta = 120^\circ$)**:
   

$$
\mu_m = 2\mu_0\cos(60^\circ) = 2\mu_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \mu_0 \approx 1.57\text{ D}
$$

3. **$p$-Dichlorobenzene ($\theta = 180^\circ$)**:
   

$$
\mu_p = 2\mu_0\cos(90^\circ) = 0\text{ D}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mu_{\text{ortho}} > \mu_{\text{meta}} > \mu_{\text{para}} = 0\text{ D}}
$$

### 2.2 Mechanism of Nucleophilic Activation in Haloarenes (Electron-Withdrawing Groups)

While chlorobenzene is inert under mild conditions, the introduction of strong electron-withdrawing groups ($-M / -I$, e.g., $-\text{NO}_2$) at **ortho** and **para** positions dramatically activates the $\text{C}-X$ bond toward nucleophilic aromatic substitution ($S_N\text{Ar}$ - Addition-Elimination Mechanism).

#### General Reaction Equation

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_4(\text{NO}_2)\text{Cl} + \text{NaOH} \xrightarrow[\text{(ii) } \text{H}^+]{\text{(i) } T\text{ K}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_4(\text{NO}_2)\text{OH} + \text{NaCl}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{rcc}
\text{No } -\text{NO}_2 \text{ group}: & \text{NaOH}, \ 623\text{ K}, \ 300\text{ atm} & (\text{Dow's Process}) \\
1 \times -\text{NO}_2 \ (para): & \text{NaOH}, \ 443\text{ K}, \ \text{H}^+ \\
2 \times -\text{NO}_2 \ (ortho, para): & \text{NaOH}, \ 368\text{ K}, \ \text{H}^+ \\
3 \times -\text{NO}_2 \ (2,4,6\text{-trinitro}): & \text{Warm } \text{H}_2\text{O} \ (\sim 323\text{ K}) & (\text{Picric Acid Formation})
\end{array}
$$

#### Step-by-Step Bimolecular Addition-Elimination ($S_N\text{Ar}$) Mechanism:
* **Step 1 (Slow, Rate-Determining Step - Nucleophilic Attack)**:
  The nucleophile ($\text{OH}^-$) attacks the $sp^2$ ipso-carbon bearing the chlorine atom to form a resonance-stabilized carbanion intermediate called the **Meisenheimer Complex**:

```
           Cl                               Cl   OH
           |                                \   /
         /   \                               \ /
        |  ○  |   +   OH-   --------->     - | |       (Meisenheimer Complex)
         \   /                (slow)         \ /
           |                                  |
          NO2                                NO2
```

The negative charge delocalizes over the aromatic ring and is directly stabilized by the $-\text{NO}_2$ group when it is ortho or para to the halogen:

```
      Cl   OH                    Cl   OH                   Cl   OH
      \   /                      \   /                     \   /
     - | |        <----->       |     | -   <----->       |     |
       \ /                       \   /                     \   /
        |                          |                         |
        N+=O                       N+=O                     -N-O(-)
       / \                        / \                       ||
      O-  O-                     O-  O-                     O
```

* **Step 2 (Fast - Elimination of Halide Ion)**:
  The aromatic system rearomatizes with the expulsion of the leaving group ($\text{Cl}^-$):

$$
\left[\text{Meisenheimer Complex}\right]^- \xrightarrow[\text{fast}]{} \text{C}_6\text{H}_4(\text{NO}_2)\text{OH} + \text{Cl}^-
$$

#### Why does $-\text{NO}_2$ at Meta position show NO significant activation?
When the $-\text{NO}_2$ group is at the **meta** position, the negative charge in the resonance structures never falls on the carbon atom bearing the $-\text{NO}_2$ substituent. 
Hence, the nitro group cannot stabilize the carbanion intermediate via its powerful $-M$ (mesomeric) effect.

## 3. High-Yield Solved Examples & 5-Year Board PYQs (2020–2025)

### Example 1 [CBSE 2024, 2 Marks]
**Problem:** 
Write the IUPAC names of the following compounds:
1. $(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}=\text{CH}-\text{CH}_2\text{Br}$
2. $\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}(\text{Cl})-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}=\text{CH}_2$

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
* **Part (1):**
  * Find the longest continuous chain containing the functional/multiple bond: 4 carbons (but-).
  * Numbering starts from the end that gives the lowest locant to the double bond over the halogen:
    

$$
\overset{4}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3-\overset{3}{\text{C}}(\text{CH}_3)=\overset{2}{\text{C}}\text{H}-\overset{1}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2\text{Br}
$$

  * Substituents: 1-Bromo, 3-Methyl.
  * Alphabetical assembly: **1-Bromo-3-methylbut-2-ene**.

* **Part (2):**
  * The chain has 5 carbons with a double bond at terminal position:
    

$$
\overset{5}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3-\overset{4}{\text{C}}\text{H}(\text{Cl})-\overset{3}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2-\overset{2}{\text{C}}\text{H}=\overset{1}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2
$$

  * Double bond receives priority locant ($1$) over chloro ($4$).
  * Name: **4-Chloropent-1-ene**.

### Example 2 [CBSE 2023, 3 Marks]
**Problem:**
Account for the following:
1. The dipole moment of chlorobenzene is lower than that of cyclohexyl chloride.
2. Alkyl halides, though polar, are immiscible with water.
3. $p$-Dichlorobenzene has a higher melting point and lower solubility than its $o$- and $m$-isomers.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Chlorobenzene vs Cyclohexyl chloride:**
   * In chlorobenzene, the carbon attached to chlorine is $sp^2$-hybridized ($33.3\%$ $s$-character), whereas in cyclohexyl chloride, it is $sp^3$-hybridized ($25\%$ $s$-character). 
   * The $sp^2$ carbon is more electronegative and draws bonding electrons less towards $\text{Cl}$, reducing polarity.
   * Furthermore, the $+R$ resonance effect introduces partial double-bond character in chlorobenzene, shortening the $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ distance ($d$) and delocalizing halogen lone pairs into the ring. Both factors reduce dipole moment ($\mu = q \times d$).
2. **Immiscibility with water:**
   * For an alkyl halide to dissolve in water, energy is required to break existing hydrogen bonds between water molecules ($\text{H}_2\text{O}\cdots\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and dipole-dipole attractions between $R-X$ molecules.
   * The new attractive interactions set up between the alkyl halide and water molecules ($R-X \cdots \text{H}_2\text{O}$) are weak dipole-dipole/dipole-induced dipole forces that release **less energy** than what is required to break the strong hydrogen bonds in water.
3. **Melting Point of $p$-Dichlorobenzene:**
   * $p$-Dichlorobenzene is highly symmetrical compared to $o$- and $m$-isomers.
   * Due to its symmetry, it packs closely and uniformly in the crystal lattice.
   * Higher intermolecular lattice forces require more thermal energy to break, leading to a significantly higher melting point ($\sim 323\text{ K}$) compared to $o$- ($248\text{ K}$) and $m$- ($251\text{ K}$) isomers, and lower solubility in nonpolar/polar solvents at room temperature.

### Example 3 [CBSE 2022 Term-1, 1 Mark]
**Problem:**
Which of the following compounds represents an allylic halide?
* (A) 2-Chlorobutane
* (B) 1-Bromobut-2-ene
* (C) 3-Bromobut-1-ene
* (D) 4-Bromobut-1-ene

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
* By NCERT definition, an allylic halide features a halogen atom attached to an $sp^3$ carbon adjacent to a $\text{C}=\text{C}$ double bond.
* Examine Option (C):
  

$$
\overset{1}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2=\overset{2}{\text{C}}\text{H}-\overset{3}{\text{C}}\text{H}(\text{Br})-\overset{4}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3
$$

  The bromine is bonded to $\text{C}-3$ ($sp^3$), which is directly adjacent to the $\text{C}-1=\text{C}-2$ double bond.
* Note on Option (B): $\text{Br}-\overset{1}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2-\overset{2}{\text{C}}\text{H}=\overset{3}{\text{C}}\text{H}-\overset{4}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3$ is also allylic. In the 2022 board exam paper, (C) was the unique key because it was presented as 3-bromobut-1-ene. Both (B) and (C) satisfy the allylic criteria.
* **Answer:** **(C)** (or (B) if framed as primary allylic).

### Example 4 [CBSE 2021, 2 Marks]
**Problem:**
Give reasons:
1. Vinyl chloride is hydrolytically less reactive than ethyl chloride.
2. Out of chloromethane, fluoromethane, and iodomethane, which has the highest boiling point and why?

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. In vinyl chloride ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\text{Cl}$), the unshared pair of electrons on chlorine is conjugated with the double bond:
   

$$
\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\overset{\cdot\cdot}{\underset{\cdot\cdot}{\text{Cl}}}: \longleftrightarrow \overset{-}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2-\text{CH}=\overset{+}{\text{Cl}}:
$$

   This imparts partial double bond character to the $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond, making it shorter and stronger than the pure single $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ $\sigma$-bond in ethyl chloride ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}$). Hence, nucleophilic cleavage by $\text{OH}^-$ is difficult.
2. **Boiling Point Order:**
   

$$
\boxed{\text{CH}_3\text{I} > \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} > \text{CH}_3\text{F}}
$$

   * For a given alkyl group, molecular mass increases with size of the halogen: $\text{F} < \text{Cl} < \text{Br} < \text{I}$.
   * Magnitude of polarizability and van der Waals (London dispersion) forces increases directly with surface area and total electrons. 
   * Hence, iodomethane ($\text{CH}_3\text{I}$) exhibits the strongest intermolecular attraction and has the highest boiling point.

### Example 5 [CBSE 2020, 3 Marks]
**Problem:**
1. Draw the structure of 1-bromo-4-sec-butyl-2-methylbenzene.
2. Arrange the following compounds in increasing order of their boiling points:
   * 1-Chlorobutane
   * 2-Chlorobutane
   * 1-Chloro-2-methylpropane
   * 2-Chloro-2-methylpropane

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
* **Part (1): Structural Representation**
  * Parent ring: Benzene.
  * Locant 1: $-\text{Br}$
  * Locant 2: $-\text{CH}_3$
  * Locant 4: $-\text{CH}(\text{CH}_3)\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_3$ ($sec$-butyl group)

```
             Br  (1)
            /
        --// \-- CH3 (2)
       /  ||  \
       \  ||  /
        --\\ /-- CH(CH3)CH2CH3 (4)
```

* **Part (2): Boiling Point Comparison**
  * All four compounds are structural isomers with molecular formula $\text{C}_4\text{H}_9\text{Cl}$ (molar mass = $92.5\text{ g/mol}$).
  * Rule: **As branching increases, the molecule approaches a spherical shape, surface area decreases, van der Waals forces decrease, and the boiling point decreases.**
  * Chain analysis:
    * 2-Chloro-2-methylpropane: Highly branched spherical structure ($3^\circ$).
    * 2-Chlorobutane: Branched at substituent ($2^\circ$).
    * 1-Chloro-2-methylpropane: Branched chain, primary halide ($1^\circ$).
    * 1-Chlorobutane: Unbranched straight chain ($1^\circ$) $\implies$ maximum contact surface area.

$$
\boxed{\text{2-Chloro-2-methylpropane} < \text{2-Chlorobutane} < \text{1-Chloro-2-methylpropane} < \text{1-Chlorobutane}}
$$

## 4. Examiner Traps & Common Mistakes

```
                                EXAMINER TRAPS
  ┌───────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────┐
  ▼                               ▼                              ▼
Nomenclature Traps              Dipole Moment Trap             Physical Properties
• Double bond takes numbering   • CH3Cl > CH3F                 • M.P. depends on symmetry
  priority over halogen           (d overcompensates q)          (p-isomer highest)
• Alphabetical order determines • CCl4 has dipole = 0          • B.P. depends on surface
  tie-breakers (bromo > chloro)   (vector cancellation)          area (straight > branched)
```

### Trap 1: Locant Priority between Double Bonds and Halogens
* **Student Error:** Writing `4-bromopent-2-ene` as `2-bromopent-3-ene`.
* **Correction:** Alkenes/alkynes have priority over halogens. Numbering **must** give the lowest possible numbers to multiple bonds, regardless of the halogen position.

### Trap 2: The Dipole Moment Reversal ($\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ vs $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$)
* **Student Error:** Predicting $\mu(\text{CH}_3\text{F}) > \mu(\text{CH}_3\text{Cl})$ assuming electronegativity difference dictates dipole moment entirely.
* **Correction:** Dipole moment is $\mu = q \times d$. The larger $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond distance ($178\text{ pm}$ vs $139\text{ pm}$) outweighs the slightly greater electronegativity of fluorine. Always write:
  

$$
\mu(\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}) \ [1.860\text{ D}] > \mu(\text{CH}_3\text{F}) \ [1.847\text{ D}]
$$

### Trap 3: Melting Point vs Boiling Point Trends of Dihalobenzenes
* **Student Error:** Writing that $p$-dichlorobenzene has the highest boiling point.
* **Correction:** 
  * Boiling points of ortho, meta, and para isomers are **nearly identical** because molecular weights and dipole interactions are comparable ($o$: $453\text{ K}$, $m$: $446\text{ K}$, $p$: $448\text{ K}$).
  * Melting points vary drastically: **para ($323\text{ K}$) $\gg$ ortho ($248\text{ K}$) $\approx$ meta ($251\text{ K}$)** due to crystal lattice symmetry. Do not confuse boiling point with melting point in board reasoning questions.

### Trap 4: Classifying Benzylic vs Aryl Halides
* **Student Error:** Categorizing $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}$ (benzyl chloride) as an aryl halide.
* **Correction:** 
  * Aryl halide: Halogen bonded directly to the ring ($sp^2$ carbon).
  * Benzylic halide: Halogen bonded to a carbon outside the ring ($sp^3$ carbon). Benzyl chloride undergoes rapid $S_N1/S_N2$ substitution, whereas chlorobenzene does not.

# SECTION 2: Methods of Preparation of Haloalkanes

### Method 1.1: From Alcohols ($\text{R—OH}$)  
⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(Highest Board Frequency)*

#### Reaction Pathways Overview
Alcohols react with hydrogen halides, phosphorus halides, or thionyl chloride to replace the hydroxyl group ($-\text{OH}$) with a halide group ($-\text{X}$).

***

#### 1.1.1 Reaction with Hydrogen Halides ($\text{HX}$) — The Lucas Reagent Pathway

$$
R\text{—}\text{OH} + \text{HX} \xrightarrow{\text{Anhydrous }\text{ZnCl}_2} R\text{—}\text{X} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Reactivity of Alcohols:**
  

$$
\text{Tertiary } (3^\circ) > \text{Secondary } (2^\circ) > \text{Primary } (1^\circ)
$$

  *Rationale:* The reaction proceeds via a carbocation intermediate ($R^+$). The stability order of carbocations ($3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ$) governs the kinetics.

* **Reactivity of Hydrogen Halides ($\text{HX}$):**
  

$$
\text{HI} > \text{HBr} > \text{HCl} \gg \text{HF}
$$

  *Rationale:* Governed by bond dissociation enthalpy:
  

$$
\Delta_{\text{bond}}H(\text{H—I}) = 299\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} < \Delta_{\text{bond}}H(\text{H—Br}) = 366\text{ kJ mol}^{-1} < \Delta_{\text{bond}}H(\text{H—Cl}) = 431\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

* **Role of Anhydrous $\text{ZnCl}_2$:**
  Anhydrous $\text{ZnCl}_2$ is a Lewis acid. It coordinates with the oxygen atom of the alcohol, weakening the $\text{C—O}$ bond and converting the poor leaving group ($-\text{OH}$) into a good leaving complex ($-\text{O}^+(\text{H})\text{—ZnCl}_2^-$):

$$
\text{R—}\overset{\bullet\bullet}{\underset{\bullet\bullet}{\text{O}}}\text{—H} + \text{ZnCl}_2 \rightleftharpoons R\text{—}\overset{+}{\underset{\text{H}}{\text{O}}}\text{—}\overline{\text{Zn}}\text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Slow}} R^+ + [\text{ZnCl}_2(\text{OH})]^{-}
$$

$$
R^+ + \text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Fast}} R\text{—}\text{Cl}
$$

* **Diagnostic Application: Lucas Test:**
  Lucas Reagent consists of an equimolar mixture of **Conc. $\text{HCl}$ and Anhydrous $\text{ZnCl}_2$**.
  * **$3^\circ$ Alcohols:** Immediate turbidity/cloudiness (formation of insoluble alkyl chloride layers) at room temperature ($298\text{ K}$).
  * **$2^\circ$ Alcohols:** Turbidity appears within $5\text{ to }10\text{ minutes}$.
  * **$1^\circ$ Alcohols:** No turbidity at room temperature; appears only upon sustained heating.

* **Specific Preparation for Alkyl Bromides & Iodides:**
  * **For Bromoalkanes:** Reflux with $48\%\text{ HBr}$ in the presence of catalytic $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$:
    

$$
R\text{—}\text{OH} + \text{NaBr} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} R\text{—}\text{Br} + \text{NaHSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

  * **For Iodoalkanes:** Reflux with $95\%\text{ H}_3\text{PO}_4$ (phosphoric acid) and $\text{KI}$:
    

$$
R\text{—}\text{OH} + \text{KI} + \text{H}_3\text{PO}_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} R\text{—}\text{I} + \text{KH}_2\text{PO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

***

#### 1.1.2 Reaction with Phosphorus Halides ($\text{PCl}_5$, $\text{PCl}_3$, $\text{PBr}_3$, $\text{PI}_3$)

* **With Phosphorus Pentachloride ($\text{PCl}_5$):**
  

$$
R\text{—}\text{OH} + \text{PCl}_5 \longrightarrow R\text{—}\text{Cl} + \text{POCl}_3\text{ (Phosphoryl chloride)} + \text{HCl}\uparrow
$$

  *By-products:* Phosphoryl chloride ($\text{POCl}_3$, liquid, b.p. $378.5\text{ K}$) and gaseous $\text{HCl}\uparrow$.

* **With Phosphorus Trichloride ($\text{PCl}_3$):**
  

$$
3R\text{—}\text{OH} + \text{PCl}_3 \longrightarrow 3R\text{—}\text{Cl} + \text{H}_3\text{PO}_3\text{ (Phosphorous acid)}
$$

  *Stoichiometry:* Requires a $3:1$ molar ratio of alcohol to $\text{PCl}_3$.

* **With Tribromide ($\text{PBr}_3$) and Triiodide ($\text{PI}_3$):**
  $\text{PBr}_3$ and $\text{PI}_3$ are unstable; they are generated *in situ* using red phosphorus and elemental bromine/iodine:
  

$$
2\text{P} + 3\text{X}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{PX}_3 \quad (\text{where }\text{X}_2 = \text{Br}_2\text{ or }\text{I}_2)
$$

  

$$
3R\text{—}\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{P }/\text{ Br}_2\text{ or }\text{I}_2} 3R\text{—}\text{X} + \text{H}_3\text{PO}_3
$$

***

#### 1.1.3 Reaction with Thionyl Chloride ($\text{SOCl}_2$) — The Darzens Procedure

$$
R\text{—}\text{OH} + \text{SOCl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Pyridine / Reflux}} R\text{—}\text{Cl} + \text{SO}_2\uparrow + \text{HCl}\uparrow
$$

* **Why this is the industrially and academically preferred method:**
  Both by-products, sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) and hydrogen chloride ($\text{HCl}$), are volatile gases and escape into the atmosphere, leaving behind virtually pure alkyl chloride without requiring tedious fractional distillation.
* **Stereochemical Nuance (Advanced NCERT Connect):**
  * In the **absence of pyridine** ($S_N i$ mechanism — Internal Nucleophilic Substitution): **Retention of configuration**.
  * In the **presence of pyridine** ($S_N 2$ mechanism): Pyridine neutralizes $\text{HCl}$ to form pyridinium chloride ($\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{NH}^+\text{Cl}^-$), liberating free chloride ion ($\text{Cl}^-$) for backside attack: **Inversion of configuration**.

### Method 1.2: From Hydrocarbons

***

#### 1.2.1 Free Radical Halogenation of Alkanes
⭐⭐⭐ *(Moderate Board Priority, High JEE Main Priority)*

Alkanes react with chlorine or bromine in the presence of ultraviolet light ($\text{UV}$) or high temperature ($520\text{--}670\text{ K}$) via a free radical chain mechanism.

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_3 \xrightarrow[\text{UV light / } h\nu]{\text{Cl}_2} \underset{\text{1-Chlorobutane (28%)}}{\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}} + \underset{\text{2-Chlorobutane (72%)}}{\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH(Cl)CH}_3} + \text{Polychloro derivatives}
$$

* **Major Limitation:**
  Yields complex, hard-to-separate isomeric mixtures of mono-, di-, and poly-haloalkanes. Hence, this reaction gives poor synthetic yields of specific haloalkanes.
* **Relative Selectivity of Halogen Radicals at $298\text{ K}$:**
  * **Chlorination:**
    

$$
3^\circ\text{ H} : 2^\circ\text{ H} : 1^\circ\text{ H} = 5.0 : 3.8 : 1.0
$$

    *(Chlorine is unselective and highly reactive)*
  * **Bromination:**
    

$$
3^\circ\text{ H} : 2^\circ\text{ H} : 1^\circ\text{ H} = 1600 : 82 : 1.0
$$

    *(Bromine is highly regioselective and attacks the most stable radical site)*

***

#### 1.2.2 From Alkenes
⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(Highest Board Frequency)*

##### (A) Addition of Hydrogen Halides ($\text{HX}$)

1. **Markovnikov's Addition (Electrophilic Addition Mechanism):**
   When an unsymmetrical alkene is treated with a hydrogen halide, the halide ($\text{X}^-$) adds to the carbon possessing fewer hydrogen atoms.

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{—CH}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H—Br} \longrightarrow \underset{\text{2-Bromopropane (Major)}}{\text{CH}_3\text{—CH(Br)—CH}_3} + \underset{\text{1-Bromopropane (Minor)}}{\text{CH}_3\text{—CH}_2\text{—CH}_2\text{Br}}
$$

**Step-by-Step Electrophilic Mechanism:**
* **Step 1: Generation of Electrophile and Formation of Carbocation (Rate Determining Step):**

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{—CH}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}^+ \longrightarrow \begin{cases} \text{CH}_3\text{—}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H—CH}_3 & (2^\circ\text{ carbocation, More stable via } 6\alpha\text{-H hyperconjugation}) \\ \text{CH}_3\text{—CH}_2\text{—}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 & (1^\circ\text{ carbocation, Less stable via } 2\alpha\text{-H hyperconjugation}) \end{cases}
$$

* **Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack on the Planar Carbocation:**

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{—}\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H—CH}_3 + \text{Br}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Fast}} \text{CH}_3\text{—CH(Br)—CH}_3
$$

***

2. **Anti-Markovnikov's Addition (Kharasch Effect / Peroxide Effect):**
   Exclusively observed when **$\text{HBr}$** is added in the presence of organic peroxides (e.g., benzoyl peroxide, $(\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CO})_2\text{O}_2$).

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{—CH}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{HBr} \xrightarrow{(\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CO})_2\text{O}_2} \underset{\text{1-Bromopropane (Exclusively Major)}}{\text{CH}_3\text{—CH}_2\text{—CH}_2\text{Br}}
$$

**Complete Radical Mechanism:**
* **Step 1: Initiation:**

$$
(\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CO})_2\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta \text{ or } h\nu} 2\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^\bullet \xrightarrow{-\text{CO}_2} 2\text{C}_6\text{H}_5^\bullet
$$

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5^\bullet + \text{H—Br} \longrightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_6 + \text{Br}^\bullet
$$

* **Step 2: Propagation:**

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{—CH}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{Br}^\bullet \longrightarrow \underset{\text{More stable } 2^\circ\text{ free radical}}{\text{CH}_3\text{—}\dot{\text{C}}\text{H—CH}_2\text{—Br}}
$$

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{—}\dot{\text{C}}\text{H—CH}_2\text{—Br} + \text{H—Br} \longrightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{—CH}_2\text{—CH}_2\text{—Br} + \text{Br}^\bullet
$$

* **Thermodynamic Basis for Peroxide Specificity:**
  * The peroxide effect is observed **only with $\text{HBr}$**.
  * **With $\text{HCl}$:** The $\text{H—Cl}$ bond energy ($431\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$) is too strong to be cleaved homolytically by the peroxy radical ($\Delta H^\circ > 0$).
  * **With $\text{HI}$:** The $\text{H—I}$ bond is weak ($299\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), but iodine free radicals ($\text{I}^\bullet$) rapidly dimerize to form molecular $\text{I}_2$ instead of adding to the olefinic double bond:
  

$$
2\text{I}^\bullet \longrightarrow \text{I}_2 \quad (\Delta H^\circ = -151\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})
$$

***

##### (B) Addition of Halogens ($\text{X}_2$) — The Unsaturation Test

$$
\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 + \text{Br}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{CCl}_4} \underset{\text{1,2-Dibromoethane (Vicinal dibromide, Colorless)}}{\text{Br—CH}_2\text{—CH}_2\text{—Br}}
$$

* **Analytical Diagnostic:** The reddish-brown color of bromine in $\text{CCl}_4$ is discharged. This serves as an essential laboratory test for the presence of carbon-carbon multiple bonds ($= \text{or} \equiv$).
* **Mechanism:** Stereospecific **anti-addition** via a non-classical cyclic bromonium ion intermediate.

### Method 1.3: Halogen Exchange Reactions
⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(Extremely High Frequency)*

These reactions are used because direct fluorination is violently exothermic (explosive) and direct iodination is reversible.

```
                    ┌───────────────────────────────┐
                    │   HALOGEN EXCHANGE REACTIONS  │
                    └───────────────┬───────────────┘
            ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
            ▼                                               ▼
┌───────────────────────┐                       ┌───────────────────────┐
│ FINKELSTEIN REACTION  │                       │    SWARTS REACTION    │
├───────────────────────┤                       ├───────────────────────┤
│ Target: Alkyl Iodides │                       │ Target: AlkylFluorides│
│ Reagent: NaI/Acetone  │                       │ Reagents: AgF, Hg₂F₂, │

└───────────────────────┘                       └───────────────────────┘
```

#### 1.3.1 Finkelstein Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Iodides)

$$
R\text{—X} + \text{NaI} \xrightarrow{\text{Dry Acetone}} R\text{—I} + \text{NaX}\downarrow \quad (\text{X} = \text{Cl}, \text{Br})
$$

* **Chemical Principle & Le Chatelier’s Law:**
  Dry acetone acts as a polar aprotic solvent. According to **Fajans' Rules**, sodium iodide ($\text{NaI}$) has covalent character due to the large, polarizable iodide ion and remains soluble in acetone. Conversely, sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) and sodium bromide ($\text{NaBr}$) are predominantly ionic lattice compounds and are completely insoluble in acetone. They precipitate out:
  

$$
\text{NaX}_{(s)} \downarrow
$$

  The continuous precipitation of $\text{NaX}$ removes product ions from solution, driving the equilibrium forward in accordance with **Le Chatelier's Principle**.

***

#### 1.3.2 Swarts Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Fluorides)

$$
R\text{—X} + \text{AgF} \xrightarrow{\Delta} R\text{—F} + \text{AgX}\downarrow \quad (\text{X} = \text{Cl}, \text{Br})
$$

* **Alternative Fluorinating Heavy Metal Fluorides:**
  

$$
\text{Hg}_2\text{F}_2\text{, }\text{CoF}_3\text{, or }\text{SbF}_3
$$

* **Example:**
  

$$
2\text{CH}_3\text{—Cl} + \text{Hg}_2\text{F}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{CH}_3\text{—F} + \text{Hg}_2\text{Cl}_2\downarrow
$$

***

### Method 1.4: Borodine-Hunsdiecker Reaction
⭐⭐⭐ *(Advanced/Competitive Edge)*

Preparation of alkyl bromides by decarboxylation of silver salts of carboxylic acids:

$$
R\text{—COOAg} + \text{Br}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{CCl}_4\text{, Reflux } (350\text{ K})} R\text{—Br} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow + \text{AgBr}\downarrow
$$

* **Characteristic:** Step-down reaction (the product haloalkane contains one carbon atom fewer than the parent carboxylate salt).
* **Yield Order:** $1^\circ\text{ RCOOAg} > 2^\circ\text{ RCOOAg} > 3^\circ\text{ RCOOAg}$.

# SECTION 3: Methods of Preparation of Haloarenes

Aromatic halides cannot typically be prepared by the direct reaction of phenols with $\text{HX}$, $\text{PCl}_5$, or $\text{SOCl}_2$ because the partial double-bond character of the $\text{C—O}$ bond resists cleavage.

```
   │     │  <->   │     │   <->   │     │   <->   │     │
   └─────┘        └──┬──┘         └──┬──┘         └──┬──┘
                     ¯               ¯               ¯
```

### Method 2.1: Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution ($\text{S}_\text{E}\text{Ar}$)
⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(Top Board Frequency)*

Direct halogenation of aromatic rings occurs using halogens in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst in the dark.

```
                          CH₃                               CH₃
                           │                                 │
                         ┌───┐                             ┌───┐
                         │   │   +  Cl₂  ─────────────►    │   │──Cl  +
                         └───┘           Fe/Dark, 298 K    └───┘
                        Toluene                          o-Chlorotoluene
                                                           (Minor)
                                                            CH₃
                                                             │
                                                           ┌───┐
                                                           │   │
                                                           └───┘
                                                             │
                                                             Cl
                                                       p-Chlorotoluene
                                                           (Major)
```

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Anhydrous }\text{FeCl}_3\text{ / Dark, 298 K}} \underset{o\text{-Chlorotoluene (Minor)}}{\text{CH}_3\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}} + \underset{p\text{-Chlorotoluene (Major)}}{\text{CH}_3\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}} + \text{HCl}
$$

* **Reagents:** Halogen carriers like $\text{Fe}$ filings, anhydrous $\text{FeCl}_3$, or anhydrous $\text{AlCl}_3$.
* **Role of the Lewis Acid Catalyst:**
  Generates the strong halonium electrophile ($\text{Cl}^+$):
  

$$
\text{Cl—Cl} + \text{FeCl}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{Cl}^+ + [\text{FeCl}_4]^-
$$

* **Isomeric Separation:**
  The ortho and para isomers exhibit a significant difference in melting points due to the symmetry of the para isomer. Consequently, they can be separated by fractional crystallization:
  * **$p$-isomer:** Packs symmetrically into the crystal lattice, leading to a higher melting point and lower solubility in nonpolar solvents.
  * **$o$-isomer:** Lower melting point and higher steric congestion.

* **Contrast: Nuclear vs Side-Chain Halogenation:**
  * Under **$\text{Fe / Dark}$** conditions: Electrophilic substitution yields ring-chlorinated toluenes.
  * Under **$\text{UV Light / } h\nu \text{ / Boiling}$** conditions: Free radical substitution occurs selectively on the alkyl side-chain:
  

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu\text{, }383\text{ K}} \underset{\text{Benzyl chloride}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}} \xrightarrow{h\nu\text{, }\text{Cl}_2} \underset{\text{Benzal dichloride}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CHCl}_2} \xrightarrow{h\nu\text{, }\text{Cl}_2} \underset{\text{Benzotrichloride}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CCl}_3}
$$

***

### Method 2.2: Direct Iodination and Fluorination of Arenes
⭐⭐⭐⭐

* **Direct Iodination:**
  

$$
\text{Ar—H} + \text{I}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{Ar—I} + \text{HI}
$$

  *Issue:* The reaction is reversible because hydroiodic acid ($\text{HI}$) is a powerful reducing agent that reduces aryl iodide back to the parent arene.
  *Remedy:* The reaction must be carried out in the presence of strong oxidizing agents such as concentrated nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) or iodic acid ($\text{HIO}_3$) to oxidize the $\text{HI}$ as it forms:
  

$$
5\text{HI} + \text{HIO}_3 \longrightarrow 3\text{I}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Direct Fluorination:**
  Direct reaction with $F_2$ is not feasible due to high exothermicity ($\Delta H \ll 0$), which ruptures the aromatic carbon skeleton.

***

### Method 2.3: Sandmeyer and Gattermann Reactions (From Diazonium Salts)
⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(Guaranteed CBSE Board Question)*

A primary aromatic amine dissolved or suspended in cold aqueous mineral acid is treated with sodium nitrite to form a benzene diazonium halide.

```
       Primary Aromatic Amine               Benzene Diazonium Chloride
              NH₂                                  N₂⁺ Cl⁻
            ┌─────┐                                ┌─────┐
            │     │  + NaNO₂ + 2 HCl ──────────►   │     │  + NaCl + 2 H₂O
            └─────┘                  273-278 K     └─────┘
            Aniline
```

#### Step 1: Diazotization Reaction

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{273\text{--}278\text{ K } (0\text{--}5^\circ\text{C})} \underset{\text{Benzene Diazonium Chloride}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^-} + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

*Temperature constraint:* Temperature must be kept below $278\text{ K}$ ($5^\circ\text{C}$); above this, diazonium salts decompose into phenol with evolution of $\text{N}_2$ gas:

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{>278\text{ K}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{N}_2\uparrow + \text{HCl}
$$

***

#### Step 2: Conversion to Haloarene

##### (A) Sandmeyer Reaction
Mixing fresh diazonium salt solution with cuprous chloride ($\text{Cu}_2\text{Cl}_2$) or cuprous bromide ($\text{Cu}_2\text{Br}_2$) dissolved in the corresponding halogen acid leads to the displacement of the diazonium group by $-\text{Cl}$ or $-\text{Br}$.

$$
\text{Ar—N}_2^+\text{X}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cu}_2\text{Cl}_2\text{ / }\text{HCl}} \text{Ar—Cl} + \text{N}_2\uparrow
$$

$$
\text{Ar—N}_2^+\text{X}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cu}_2\text{Br}_2\text{ / }\text{HBr}} \text{Ar—Br} + \text{N}_2\uparrow
$$

##### (B) Gattermann Reaction
A modification of the Sandmeyer reaction using copper powder in the presence of halogen acid ($\text{HCl}$ or $\text{HBr}$).

$$
\text{Ar—N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cu powder / }\text{HCl}} \text{Ar—Cl} + \text{CuCl} + \text{N}_2\uparrow
$$

$$
\text{Ar—N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cu powder / }\text{HBr}} \text{Ar—Br} + \text{CuCl} + \text{N}_2\uparrow
$$

*Yield Comparison:* The **Sandmeyer reaction yields are superior** to those obtained via the Gattermann pathway.

##### (C) Synthesis of Aryl Iodides (No Copper Catalyst Required)
Iodobenzene is prepared by shaking the diazonium salt directly with an aqueous solution of potassium iodide ($\text{KI}$):

$$
\text{Ar—N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{KI} \xrightarrow{\text{Warm / Shake}} \text{Ar—I} + \text{KCl} + \text{N}_2\uparrow
$$

***

### Method 2.4: Balz-Schiemann Reaction (Synthesis of Fluoroarenes)
⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(High-Value Reaction)*

Direct fluorination cannot be used, and cuprous fluoride cannot be employed in Sandmeyer reactions. Instead, the diazonium salt is converted to a fluoroborate intermediate.

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{HBF}_4\text{ (Fluoroboric acid)} \xrightarrow{273\text{--}278\text{ K}} \underset{\text{Benzene diazonium fluoroborate (Precipitate)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{BF}_4^-}\downarrow + \text{HCl}
$$

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{BF}_4^- \xrightarrow{\text{Dry / Heat }\Delta} \underset{\text{Fluorobenzene}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{F}} + \text{BF}_3\uparrow + \text{N}_2\uparrow
$$

# SECTION 4: Examiner Traps & Common Errors

1. **The $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ Trap with $\text{KI}$:**
   * **Question:** Why is sulfuric acid not used during the reaction of alcohols with $\text{KI}$?
   * **Examiner Insight:** Students often write that $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ decomposes the alcohol.
   * **Correct NCERT Mechanism:** $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is an oxidizing agent. It oxidizes $\text{HI}$ produced during the reaction into free iodine ($\text{I}_2$), preventing it from reacting with the alcohol:
   

$$
2\text{KI} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \longrightarrow 2\text{KHSO}_4 + 2\text{HI}
$$

   

$$
2\text{HI} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \longrightarrow \text{I}_2 + \text{SO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

   *Remedy:* Non-oxidizing phosphoric acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$) is used instead.

2. **Peroxide Effect Specificity Trap:**
   * Students often incorrectly apply the peroxide effect to $\text{HCl}$ or $\text{HI}$.
   * **Rule:** The Kharasch/Peroxide effect applies **only to $\text{HBr}$**. Both additions of $\text{HCl}$ and $\text{HI}$ in the presence of peroxides yield **Markovnikov products**.

3. **Phenol Cleavage Trap:**
   * **Trap:** Attempting to synthesize chlorobenzene by reacting phenol ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) with $\text{SOCl}_2$ or $\text{PCl}_5$.
   * **Reason for Zero Yield:** In phenol, the lone pair of electrons on oxygen is conjugated with the $\pi$-electron cloud of the aromatic ring, conferring a **partial double bond character** ($136\text{ pm}$ vs $143\text{ pm}$ for aliphatic $\text{C—O}$). Furthermore, the $sp^2$-hybridized carbon has high electronegativity, making the $\text{C—O}$ bond difficult to cleave.

4. **Temperature Slippage in Diazotization:**
   * Writing temperatures above $5^\circ\text{C}$ ($278\text{ K}$) causes decomposition to phenol. Always write $273\text{--}278\text{ K}$ or $0\text{--}5^\circ\text{C}$.

#### [CBSE 2023, Set-2, 3 Marks]
**Q2. Account for the following observations:**  
**(a) The dipole moment of chlorobenzene is lower than that of cyclohexyl chloride.**  
**(b) Alkyl halides, though polar, are immiscible with water.**  
**(c) Grignard reagents must be prepared under strictly anhydrous conditions.**

* **Solution:**  
  (a) In chlorobenzene, the $sp^2$-hybridized carbon has higher $s$-character (33.3%) and is more electronegative than the $sp^3$-hybridized carbon (25% $s$-character) in cyclohexyl chloride. This reduces the polarity of the $\text{C—Cl}$ bond. Furthermore, resonance causes delocalization of the chlorine lone pair, giving the bond partial double bond character and shortening the dipole length ($d$):
  

$$
\mu = \delta \times d
$$

  (b) Intermolecular hydrogen bonding exists between water molecules. Alkyl halides cannot form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, and the new dipole-dipole attractions formed between alkyl halides and water are insufficient to break the existing $\text{H}$-bonds and overcome dipole-dipole attractions in pure halides.  
  (c) Grignard reagents ($R\text{—MgX}$) are strong bases and react with any source of active protons (such as water, alcohols, or amines) to form alkanes:
  

$$
R\text{MgX} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow R\text{—H} + \text{Mg(OH)X}
$$

#### [CBSE 2021, Compartment, 1 Mark]
**Q4. Out of $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH—Cl}$ and $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{—Cl}$, which one is more reactive towards nucleophilic substitution and why?**

* **Solution:**  
  $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{—Cl}$ is more reactive. In chloroethene ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH—Cl}$), the unshared electron pairs on chlorine enter into resonance with the double bond:

\text{CH}_2=\text{CH—}\overset{\bullet\bullet}{\underset{\bullet\bullet}{\text{Cl}}} \longleftrightarrow \overset{-}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2\text{—CH}=\overset{+}{\text{Cl}}

# Physical Properties: Boiling Points, Density, and Solubility

# Class 12 Chemistry | Unit: Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

## Topic: Physical Properties — Boiling Points, Density, and Solubility

***

# 1. Pedagogical Theory & Molecular Concepts

Haloalkanes and haloarenes are polar organic halogen derivatives. Their physical properties depend primarily on two molecular characteristics:
1. **The Polarity of the Carbon–Halogen Bond ($\mathrm{C^{\delta+} - X^{\delta-}}$)**
2. **The Size and Polarizability of the Halogen Atom ($\mathrm{F < Cl < Br < I}$)**

***

### Concept 1: Dipole Moment & Bond Polarity
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Due to electronegativity differences ($\chi_{\text{Halogen}} > \chi_{\text{Carbon}}$), the $\mathrm{C-X}$ bond is polarized:

$$
\mathrm{C}^{\delta+} - \mathrm{X}^{\delta-}
$$

The dipole moment $\mu$ is defined as:

$$
\mu = q \times d
$$

Where:
- $q$ is the magnitude of separated partial charge (in Coulombs, $\mathrm{C}$).
- $d$ is the bond length / internuclear distance (in meters, $\mathrm{m}$).
- Expressed in Debye units: $1\text{ D} = 3.33564 \times 10^{-30}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}$.

#### The Dipole Moment Anomaly (NCERT Exception)
Electronegativity decreases down Group 17:

$$
\mathrm{F} \,(4.0) > \mathrm{Cl} \,(3.0) > \mathrm{Br} \,(2.8) > \mathrm{I} \,(2.5)
$$

Bond length increases down Group 17:

$$
d_{\mathrm{C-F}} \,(139\text{ pm}) < d_{\mathrm{C-Cl}} \,(178\text{ pm}) < d_{\mathrm{C-Br}} \,(193\text{ pm}) < d_{\mathrm{C-I}} \,(214\text{ pm})
$$

Although Fluorine is the most electronegative element, the dipole moment of chloromethane ($\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$) is higher than that of fluoromethane ($\mathrm{CH_3F}$):

$$
\mu (\mathrm{CH_3Cl}) = 1.860\text{ D} \quad > \quad \mu (\mathrm{CH_3F}) = 1.847\text{ D} \quad > \quad \mu (\mathrm{CH_3Br}) = 1.830\text{ D} \quad > \quad \mu (\mathrm{CH_3I}) = 1.636\text{ D}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mu\text{ order: } \mathrm{CH_3Cl} > \mathrm{CH_3F} > \mathrm{CH_3Br} > \mathrm{CH_3I}}
$$

> **NCERT Keyword Explanation:** Even though the charge separation $q$ in $\mathrm{C-F}$ is exceptionally large, the $\mathrm{C-F}$ bond distance $d$ is very small. In $\mathrm{C-Cl}$, the significantly larger bond length ($178\text{ pm}$ vs $139\text{ pm}$) outweighs the slightly lower charge separation, making the product $q \times d$ larger for $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$.

***

### Concept 2: Boiling Points of Haloalkanes
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board Frequency)

The boiling point ($T_b$) is the temperature at which the vapor pressure of a liquid equals atmospheric pressure. At a molecular level, it measures the energy required to overcome intermolecular forces in the liquid phase.

#### Factor A: Magnitude of Intermolecular Forces
Molecules of haloalkanes are held together by:
1. **Dipole-dipole interactions** (arising from permanent $\mathrm{C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}$ polarity).
2. **London dispersion forces / van der Waals forces** (arising from instantaneous induced dipoles).

Because haloalkanes possess permanent dipoles and greater polarizability than parent hydrocarbons, their boiling points are significantly higher than those of alkanes of comparable molecular mass.

$$
\text{Boiling Point: } \mathrm{R-X} \gg \mathrm{R-H} \quad (\text{for comparable carbon skeletons})
$$

#### Factor B: Effect of Halogen Identity (For the same alkyl group $\mathrm{R}$)
As we move from $\mathrm{F}$ to $\mathrm{I}$, atomic size and the number of electrons increase. This increases the polarizability of the electron cloud, strengthening London dispersion forces:

$$
\text{Boiling Point Order: } \boxed{\mathrm{R-I} > \mathrm{R-Br} > \mathrm{R-Cl} > \mathrm{R-F}}
$$

*Data verification (NCERT, for $\mathrm{R = CH_3}$):*
- $\mathrm{CH_3I}$: $315.4\text{ K}$
- $\mathrm{CH_3Br}$: $276.6\text{ K}$
- $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$: $248.8\text{ K}$
- $\mathrm{CH_3F}$: $194.7\text{ K}$

#### Factor C: Effect of Homologous Chain Length (For the same halogen $\mathrm{X}$)
As the length of the carbon chain increases, the surface area and polarizability of the hydrocarbon chain increase, raising London dispersion forces:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{CH_3X} < \mathrm{CH_3CH_2X} < \mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2X} < \mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_3X}}
$$

#### Factor D: Effect of Branching in Isomeric Haloalkanes
For isomeric haloalkanes (same molecular formula and mass):

$$
\boxed{\text{Boiling Point Order: } \text{Primary } (1^\circ) > \text{Secondary } (2^\circ) > \text{Tertiary } (3^\circ)}
$$

$$
\text{Branching} \uparrow \implies \text{Molecular geometry approaches a sphere} \implies \text{Surface area} \downarrow \implies \text{van der Waals forces} \downarrow \implies T_b \downarrow
$$

*NCERT Representative Case ($\mathrm{C_4H_9Br}$, Molar Mass = $137\text{ g/mol}$):*
1. **$1$-Bromobutane** ($\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Br}$, linear): $T_b = 375\text{ K}$
2. **$2$-Bromobutane** ($\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH(Br)CH_3}$, branched): $T_b = 364\text{ K}$
3. **$2$-Bromo-$2$-methylpropane** ($(\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{CBr}$, highly branched spherical): $T_b = 346\text{ K}$

***

### Concept 3: Boiling Points and Melting Points of Dihalobenzenes
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

For isomeric dihalobenzenes ($o\text{-, } m\text{-, } p\text{-}$dichlorobenzene):

```
     Cl                  Cl                  Cl
     |                   |                   |
   /¯¯¯\ Cl            /¯¯¯\               /¯¯¯\
   \___/               \___/ Cl            \___/
                                             |
                                             Cl
 ortho (1,2)         meta (1,3)          para (1,4)
```

#### 1. Boiling Point Trends
The boiling points of the three isomers are nearly identical because their molecular masses, surface areas, and gross dipoles produce very similar magnitudes of intermolecular attraction in the liquid state:

$$
T_b(\text{para}) \approx T_b(\text{ortho}) > T_b(\text{meta})
$$

- $o$-Dichlorobenzene: $453\text{ K}$
- $m$-Dichlorobenzene: $446\text{ K}$
- $p$-Dichlorobenzene: $448\text{ K}$

#### 2. Melting Point Trends (The Critical NCERT Concept)
Unlike boiling point, **melting point depends strictly on crystal lattice packing efficiency**:

$$
\boxed{\text{Melting Point: } \text{para-isomer} \gg \text{ortho-isomer} > \text{meta-isomer}}
$$

- $p$-Dichlorobenzene: $323\text{ K}$ (Solid at room temperature)
- $o$-Dichlorobenzene: $256\text{ K}$ (Liquid at room temperature)
- $m$-Dichlorobenzene: $249\text{ K}$ (Liquid at room temperature)

> **NCERT Key Phrase for Board Exams:**
> *"The para-isomer has high symmetry compared to ortho and meta-isomers. Due to its symmetrical structure, its molecules pack closely and symmetrically in the crystal lattice. Consequently, stronger intermolecular forces exist in the solid state, requiring a considerably greater amount of thermal energy to break the lattice."*

***

### Concept 4: Density Trends
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐

The density ($\rho = \frac{m}{V}$) of haloalkanes is governed by the heavy atomic mass of the halogen compared to hydrogen/carbon, along with intermolecular packing volume.

1. **Comparison with Water ($\rho_{\mathrm{H_2O}} \approx 1.00\text{ g/cm}^3$):**
   - Chloroalkanes (monochloro): **Lighter than water** ($\rho < 1.0\text{ g/cm}^3$, e.g., $1$-chlorobutane $\rho = 0.89\text{ g/cm}^3$).
   - Monobromoalkanes, monoiodoalkanes, and polychloroalkanes: **Denser than water** ($\rho > 1.0\text{ g/cm}^3$).
2. **Periodic Trend (Same alkyl group):**
   

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{R-I} > \mathrm{R-Br} > \mathrm{R-Cl}}
$$

   *Example ($\mathrm{R = } n\text{-}\mathrm{C_3H_7}$ at $293\text{ K}$):*
   - $n\text{-}\mathrm{C_3H_7Cl}$: $0.890\text{ g/cm}^3$
   - $n\text{-}\mathrm{C_3H_7Br}$: $1.335\text{ g/cm}^3$
   - $n\text{-}\mathrm{C_3H_7I}$: $1.747\text{ g/cm}^3$
3. **Number of Halogen Atoms:**
   

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2} \,(1.326\text{ g/cm}^3) < \mathrm{CHCl_3} \,(1.489\text{ g/cm}^3) < \mathrm{CCl_4} \,(1.595\text{ g/cm}^3)}
$$

Density increases with:
- Increasing atomic mass of halogen.
- Increasing number of halogen atoms.
- Increasing length of carbon chain (less pronounced than halogen substitution).

### Concept 5: Solubility in Water vs. Organic Solvents
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Haloalkanes are **extremely slightly soluble (almost immiscible) in water**, despite having a polar $\mathrm{C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}$ bond.

#### Thermodynamic Dissolution Cycle:
For a solute to dissolve in water, three steps occur:
1. **Solute–Solute separation:** Energy must be supplied to break dipole-dipole attractions between $\mathrm{R-X}$ molecules ($\Delta H_1 > 0$).
2. **Solvent–Solvent cavity formation:** Energy must be supplied to break strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds ($\mathrm{O-H\cdots O}$) between water molecules ($\Delta H_2 \gg 0$).
3. **Solute–Solvent interaction:** Energy is released when new weak dipole–dipole or dipole–induced dipole attractions form between $\mathrm{R-X}$ and $\mathrm{H_2O}$ ($\Delta H_3 < 0$).

$$
\Delta H_{\text{solution}} = \Delta H_1 + \Delta H_2 + \Delta H_3
$$

Because the new attractions between haloalkanes and water are weak:

$$
|\Delta H_3| \ll (\Delta H_1 + \Delta H_2) \implies \Delta H_{\text{solution}} > 0 \quad (\text{Unfavorable Endothermic Process})
$$

Furthermore, the dissolution requires forming an ordered cage of water molecules around the hydrophobic alkyl chain, leading to an unfavorable entropy change:

$$
\Delta S < 0 \implies \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S > 0 \quad (\text{Non-spontaneous})
$$

> **NCERT Key Formulation:**
> *"Less energy is released when new attractions are set up between the haloalkane and the water molecules because these are not as strong as the original hydrogen bonds in water. As a result, the solubility of haloalkanes in water is very low."*

#### Solubility in Non-Polar / Organic Solvents:
Haloalkanes dissolve readily in typical organic solvents (benzene, ether, chloroform, alcohol) because the new intermolecular forces between haloalkanes and solvent molecules have comparable strength to the broken solute-solute and solvent-solvent interactions:

$$
\Delta H_{\text{solution(organic)}} \approx 0, \quad \Delta S_{\text{solution}} > 0 \implies \Delta G < 0
$$

# 2. Mathematical Formalisms & Physical Quantities

### A. Clausius-Clapeyron Vapor Pressure Formalism
The dependence of vapor pressure $P$ on temperature $T$, and therefore the boiling point $T_b$ (when $P = P_{\text{ext}} = 1\text{ atm} = 1.01325\text{ bar}$), is given by:

$$
\ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) = -\frac{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}{R} \left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)
$$

Where:
- $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$ is the enthalpy of vaporization ($\mathrm{J\cdot mol^{-1}}$).
- $R = 8.314\text{ J}\cdot\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{K^{-1}}$.
- $T$ is temperature in Kelvin ($\mathrm{K}$).

Because branched haloalkanes have lower intermolecular surface contact:

$$
\Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{linear}) > \Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{branched}) \implies T_b(\text{linear}) > T_b(\text{branched})
$$

### B. Lennard-Jones Potential (London Dispersion Basis)
The attractive London dispersion potential $V_{\text{att}}$ between two neutral polarizable haloalkane molecules separated by distance $r$ is:

$$
V_{\text{disp}}(r) = -\frac{3}{4} \left[ \frac{I_1 I_2}{I_1 + I_2} \right] \frac{\alpha_1 \alpha_2}{r^6}
$$

Where:
- $\alpha$ is electronic polarizability ($\mathrm{m^3}$ or $\mathrm{C\cdot m^2\cdot V^{-1}}$).
- $I$ is the first ionization energy ($\mathrm{J}$).
- $r$ is the center-to-center distance ($\mathrm{m}$).

Since iodine has a much larger, more diffuse valence shell ($5p$) than fluorine ($2p$), $\alpha_{\mathrm{I}} \gg \alpha_{\mathrm{F}}$, making $V_{\text{disp}}$ significantly more negative (more stabilizing) for iodoalkanes.

### Question 1 [CBSE 2024, 2 Marks]
**Prompt:** Account for the following:
1. The boiling point of ethanol ($\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}$) is much higher than that of ethyl chloride ($\mathrm{C_2H_5Cl}$), even though ethyl chloride has a higher molecular mass.
2. $p$-Dichlorobenzene has a higher melting point than its $o$- and $m$-isomers.

#### Model Solution:
1. **Ethanol vs. Ethyl Chloride:**
   Ethanol molecules associate through strong **intermolecular hydrogen bonding** ($\mathrm{O-H\cdots O}$) due to the presence of an electronegative oxygen atom bonded to hydrogen. Ethyl chloride molecules are held only by weaker **dipole-dipole and van der Waals interactions**. More thermal energy is required to cleave intermolecular hydrogen bonds in ethanol, resulting in a higher boiling point ($T_b\text{ ethanol} = 351\text{ K}$; $T_b\text{ ethyl chloride} = 285\text{ K}$). *(1 Mark)*

2. **Melting Point of $p$-Dichlorobenzene:**
   The $p$-dichlorobenzene isomer is symmetrical. Due to this **symmetry**, its molecules pack more closely and uniformly into the crystal lattice than the unsymmetrical $o$- and $m$-isomers. Because it forms a more stable and tightly packed lattice, significantly higher thermal energy is required to melt it. *(1 Mark)*

### Question 2 [CBSE 2023, 3 Marks]
**Prompt:**
(a) Arrange the following compounds in increasing order of their boiling points:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Br}, \quad (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{CBr}, \quad (\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CHCH_2Br}
$$

(b) Haloalkanes, though polar, are practically insoluble in water. Explain why.

#### Model Solution:
**(a) Arrangement:**
All three compounds are structural isomers of bromobutane ($\mathrm{C_4H_9Br}$):
- $(\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{CBr}$ ($2$-Bromo-$2$-methylpropane): $3^\circ$ alkyl halide, maximum branching, most spherical shape, lowest surface area.

For haloalkanes to dissolve in water, energy is needed to break the preexisting dipole-dipole attractions between haloalkane molecules and the strong hydrogen bonds ($\mathrm{O-H\cdots O}$) between water molecules. 

The new intermolecular attractions established between haloalkane and water molecules (dipole-dipole) are **substantially weaker** than the hydrogen bonds broken in water. Therefore, the energy released during hydration is insufficient to compensate for the energy required to break hydrogen bonds, making dissolution energetically unfavorable. *(1.5 Marks)*

# 5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

### 1. The "S-P-L" Boiling Point Hierarchy
Use this mental checklist when comparing boiling points:
1. **S**ize/Mass: Higher molecular mass $\implies$ larger electron cloud $\implies$ higher $T_b$ ($\mathrm{R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F}$).
2. **P**ath Length: Longer carbon chain $\implies$ greater surface area $\implies$ higher $T_b$ ($n\text{-butyl} > n\text{-propyl} > \text{ethyl} > \text{methyl}$).
3. **L**ack of Branching: For isomers, more linear $\implies$ higher $T_b$ ($1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ$).

### 2. The Symmetry Mnemonic for Dihalobenzenes

$$
\text{Melting Point: } \mathbf{P}ara \gg \mathbf{O}rtho > \mathbf{M}eta \quad \longrightarrow \quad "POM"
$$

- **P**ara fits into the crystal lattice like bricks in a wall (**Symmetrical**).
- **O**rtho and **M**eta are irregular shapes that do not pack tightly.

### 3. Density Threshold Checklist

$$
\rho < 1.0\text{ g/cm}^3 \quad (\text{Floats on Water}): \text{All pure alkanes, 1-Monochloroalkanes}
$$

$$
\rho > 1.0\text{ g/cm}^3 \quad (\text{Sinks in Water}): \text{All Monobromoalkanes, Monoiodoalkanes, Polychloroalkanes } (\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2, CHCl_3, CCl_4})
$$

### 4. Golden Formula for Dipole Direction in Dihalobenzenes
For dihalobenzenes, net dipole depends on bond angle $\theta$ via the vector formula:

$$
\mu_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{\mu_1^2 + \mu_2^2 + 2\mu_1\mu_2\cos\theta}
$$

- Ortho ($\theta = 60^\circ$): $\mu_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{\mu^2 + \mu^2 + 2\mu^2\cos 60^\circ} = \sqrt{3}\mu \approx 1.73\mu$
- Meta ($\theta = 120^\circ$): $\mu_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{\mu^2 + \mu^2 + 2\mu^2\cos 120^\circ} = \mu$
- Para ($\theta = 180^\circ$): $\mu_{\text{net}} = \mu - \mu = 0\text{ D}$ (Individual bond dipoles cancel out)

$$
\boxed{\text{Dipole Moment Order: } \text{ortho} > \text{meta} > \text{para} = 0}
$$

## 1. Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions ($S_N$)

Haloalkanes contain an $sp^3$-hybridized carbon bonded to an electronegative halogen atom ($X$). The polar carbon–halogen bond ($\mathrm{C^{\delta+}—X^{\delta-}}$) makes the carbon electrophilic, rendering it susceptible to attack by electron-rich species known as **nucleophiles** ($\mathrm{Nu^-}$ or $:\!\mathrm{Nu}$).

$$
\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{Nu}^- \longrightarrow \mathrm{R{-}Nu} + \mathrm{X}^-
$$

***

### A. $S_N2$ Reaction Mechanism (Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Fundamental Principles & Kinetics
The $S_N2$ mechanism proceeds via a **concerted, single-step** bimolecular pathway without any reaction intermediates. The bond-forming event between the incoming nucleophile and the substrate carbon occurs simultaneously with the cleavage of the carbon–leaving group bond.

$$
\text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{R{-}X}]\,[\mathrm{Nu}^-]
$$

* **Order of Reaction:** $1 + 1 = 2$ (Second order kinetics)
* **Molecularity:** 2 (Bimolecular)
* **Rate constant unit:** $\mathrm{mol^{-1} \cdot L \cdot s^{-1}}$

#### 2. Stereochemistry & Walden Inversion
* The nucleophile approaches the electrophilic carbon from the **backside** ($180^\circ$ relative to the leaving group) to minimize electrostatic repulsion with the electron cloud of the leaving group.
* The reaction proceeds through a high-energy **pentacoordinated transition state** wherein the central carbon approaches planar $sp^2$ hybridization with a perpendicular unhybridized $p$-orbital simultaneously overlapping with the incoming nucleophile and outgoing leaving group.
* As the leaving group departs with its electron pair, the remaining three bonds attached to the central carbon flip to the opposite side, much like an umbrella blowing inside out in a strong wind.
* This complete stereochemical reversal is defined as **Walden Inversion** (100% inversion of configuration at an asymmetric carbon).

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Configuration:}\quad (R)\text{-enantiomer} &\xrightarrow{S_N2} (S)\text{-enantiomer} \quad (\text{if priority sequence is conserved}) \\
(S)\text{-enantiomer} &\xrightarrow{S_N2} (R)\text{-enantiomer}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### 3. Energy Profile & Transition State
```
        [ Transition State: Nu···C···X ]‡  (sp2-like planar carbon, pentacoordinate)
                    /\
                   /  \
                  /    \
  Reactants      /      \
  R-X + Nu⁻    /        \
  -----------             \
                           \   Products
                            \  R-Nu + X⁻
                             -----------
      Reaction Coordinate ------>
```

* **Transition State Structure:** 

$$
\left[ \, \mathrm{Nu}^{\delta-} \cdots \mathrm{C}(\mathrm{R}_1\mathrm{R}_2\mathrm{R}_3) \cdots \mathrm{X}^{\delta-} \, \right]^\ddagger
$$

Carbon has 3 fully formed $\sigma$-bonds in an equatorial plane ($120^\circ$ apart) and 2 partial axial bonds (bond order $\approx 0.5$ each).

#### 4. Factors Affecting $S_N2$ Rate
1. **Steric Hindrance (Dominant Factor):**
   Bulkier alkyl groups shield the backside of the carbon atom, sharply raising the transition state activation energy:
   

$$
\boxed{\text{Methyl halide} > 1^\circ\ (1\text{-haloalkane}) > 2^\circ\ (2\text{-haloalkane}) \gg 3^\circ\ (3\text{-haloalkane})}
$$

   *Relative rates (approximate, with $\mathrm{CH_3Br}$ as reference):*
   

$$
\mathrm{CH_3Br}\ (30) > \mathrm{CH_3CH_2Br}\ (1) > (\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CHBr}\ (0.02) \gg (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{CBr}\ (\sim 0)
$$

2. **Leaving Group Ability:**
   Weaker bases stabilize negative charge more effectively and act as superior leaving groups. The conjugate bases of strong hydrohalic acids follow:
   

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{I}^- > \mathrm{Br}^- > \mathrm{Cl}^- \gg \mathrm{F}^-}
$$

3. **Nucleophile Strength:**
   Strong, negatively charged nucleophiles accelerate $S_N2$ rates:
   

$$
\mathrm{HS}^- > \mathrm{CN}^- > \mathrm{OH}^- > \mathrm{OR}^- > \mathrm{I}^- > \mathrm{Br}^- > \mathrm{NH_3} > \mathrm{H_2O}
$$

4. **Solvent Effect:**
   **Polar Aprotic Solvents** (e.g., Acetone, DMSO, DMF, Acetonitrile) solvate cations via dipole coordination but leave nucleophilic anions "naked" (unsolvated) and highly reactive, dramatically increasing the $S_N2$ rate.

***

### B. $S_N1$ Reaction Mechanism (Substitution Nucleophilic Unimolecular)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

#### 1. Fundamental Principles & Kinetics
The $S_N1$ mechanism is a **two-step, stepwise** reaction mediated by a planar carbocation intermediate.

$$
\text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{R{-}X}]
$$

* **Order of Reaction:** 1 (First order kinetics; independent of nucleophile concentration and strength).
* **Molecularity:** 1 (Unimolecular rate-determining step).
* **Rate constant unit:** $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$

#### 2. Detailed Stepwise Mechanism
* **Step 1: Heterolytic Cleavage (Slow / Rate-Determining Step):**
  The polar $\mathrm{C{-}X}$ bond cleaves slowly assisted by protic solvent dipole stabilization to form a planar carbocation intermediate and a halide ion.
  

$$
\mathrm{R_3C{-}X} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{polar protic}]{\text{Slow (r.d.s)}} \left[\mathrm{R_3C}\right]^+ + \mathrm{X}^-
$$

  * Carbocation geometry: $sp^2$-hybridized, planar, $120^\circ$ bond angles, empty $2p_z$ orbital.

* **Step 2: Attack of Nucleophile (Fast Step):**
  Because the carbocation is flat and planar, the nucleophile can attack the vacant $p$-orbital with equal probability from either the top face (frontside) or the bottom face (backside).
  

$$
\left[\mathrm{R_3C}\right]^+ + \mathrm{Nu}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Fast}} \mathrm{R_3C{-}Nu}
$$

#### 3. Stereochemistry: Racemization with Partial Inversion
* **Ideal Case:** Equal $50 : 50$ attack on both faces produces equal quantities of $(+)$ and $(-)$ enantiomers, yielding an optically inactive **racemic mixture** ($[\alpha] = 0^\circ$).
* **Real/Experimental Observation (NCERT Nuance):**
  The departing leaving group often shields the frontside as an **intimate ion pair** $[\mathrm{R}^+ \parallel \mathrm{X}^-]$. Consequently, backside attack slightly exceeds frontside attack:
  

$$
\boxed{\text{Net Stereochemical Result} = \text{Inversion} > \text{Retention} \implies \text{Partial Racemization with Net Inversion}}
$$

#### 4. Factors Affecting $S_N1$ Rate
1. **Carbocation Stability (Dominant Factor):**
   

$$
\boxed{\text{Benzylic} \approx \text{Allylic} > 3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \mathrm{CH_3{-}X}}
$$

   * Stabilized by inductive effect ($+I$), hyperconjugation ($\alpha\text{-hydrogen}$ count), and resonance delocalization:
   

$$
\mathrm{[C_6H_5CH_2]}^+ \longleftrightarrow \text{Resonance hybrid with 4 canonical forms}
$$

   

$$
\mathrm{[CH_2{=}CH{-}CH_2]}^+ \longleftrightarrow \mathrm{[CH_2{-}CH{=}CH_2]}^+
$$

2. **Solvent Effect:**
   **Polar Protic Solvents** ($\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$, $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$) possess high dielectric constants ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{H_2O}} \approx 80$) and hydrogen-bond donors. They solvate the leaving halide ($\mathrm{X}^-$) via hydrogen bonding and the carbocation through dipole interaction, significantly lowering the activation barrier for the rate-determining ionization step.

***

### C. Comprehensive Comparative Matrix: $S_N1$ vs. $S_N2$

| Parameter | $S_N1$ | $S_N2$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Kinetics & Rate Law** | $\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{RX}]$ (1st order) | $\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{RX}][\mathrm{Nu}^-]$ (2nd order) |
| **Number of Steps** | Two steps via discrete intermediate | One step (concerted, no intermediate) |
| **Intermediate** | Carbocation ($sp^2$, planar) | None (Pentacoordinate Transition State) |
| **Substrate Reactivity** | $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \mathrm{CH_3X}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3X} > 1^\circ > 2^\circ \gg 3^\circ$ |
| **Stereochemistry** | Racemization (with partial inversion) | 100% Walden Inversion |
| **Favored Nucleophile** | Weak, neutral ($\mathrm{H_2O, ROH}$) | Strong, anionic ($\mathrm{OH^-, CN^-, I^-}$) |
| **Favored Solvent** | Polar Protic ($\mathrm{H_2O, EtOH, AcOH}$) | Polar Aprotic ($\text{Acetone, DMSO, DMF}$) |
| **Rearrangements** | Possible (via hydride/alkyl shifts) | Not possible (no carbocation formed) |

### D. Ambident Nucleophiles: Dual-Site Attack
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

An **ambident nucleophile** is a nucleophile that possesses two nucleophilic donor atoms with unshared electron pairs, through either of which it can attack the electrophilic carbon.

#### 1. Cyanide vs. Isocyanide: Reaction with $\mathrm{KCN}$ vs. $\mathrm{AgCN}$
* **Ambident ion:** $[:\!\mathrm{C} \equiv \mathrm{N}:]^-$ has negative charge on carbon and lone pair on nitrogen.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{Reaction\ 1:}\quad &\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{KCN} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{aq.\ EtOH}} \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}CN}}\ (\text{Alkyl cyanide / Alkane nitrile}) + \mathrm{KX} \\
\mathbf{Reaction\ 2:}\quad &\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{AgCN} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{aq.\ EtOH}} \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}NC}}\ (\text{Alkyl isocyanide / Carbylamine}) + \mathrm{AgX}\downarrow
\end{aligned}
$$

* **NCERT Rationalization:**
  * $\mathrm{KCN}$ is predominantly **ionic** in nature and dissociates completely to provide free cyanide ions ($[:\!\mathrm{C} \equiv \mathrm{N}:]^-$). Because the $\mathrm{C{-}C}$ bond enthalpy ($\approx 348\ \mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}}$) is significantly greater and more thermodynamically stable than the $\mathrm{C{-}N}$ bond enthalpy ($\approx 305\ \mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}}$), nucleophilic attack occurs predominantly through the carbon atom, yielding an **alkyl cyanide**.
  * $\mathrm{AgCN}$ is predominantly **covalent**. The silver-carbon bond is not ionized; hence, the lone pair on the nitrogen atom is the only site freely available for nucleophilic attack. This leads to the formation of a coordination-like bond through nitrogen, producing an **alkyl isocyanide** ($\mathrm{R{-}N{\equiv}C}$).

***

#### 2. Nitrite vs. Nitroalkane: Reaction with $\mathrm{KNO_2}$ vs. $\mathrm{AgNO_2}$
* **Ambident ion:** The nitrite ion contains donor sites at oxygen and nitrogen:

$$
[\,^-\mathrm{O{-}N{=}O} \longleftrightarrow \mathrm{O{=}N{-}O^-}\,]
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{Reaction\ 1:}\quad &\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{KNO_2} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}O{-}N{=}O}}\ (\text{Alkyl nitrite}) + \mathrm{KX} \\
\mathbf{Reaction\ 2:}\quad &\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{AgNO_2} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}NO_2}}\ (\text{Nitroalkane}) + \mathrm{AgX}\downarrow
\end{aligned}
$$

* **NCERT Rationalization:**
  * $\mathrm{KNO_2}$ is an **ionic** compound. In the free nitrite ion $[\mathrm{O{-}N{=}O}]^-$, the negative charge resides predominantly on the more electronegative oxygen atom. Attack occurs through the oxygen donor atom, forming an **alkyl nitrite** ($\mathrm{R{-}ONO}$).
  * $\mathrm{AgNO_2}$ possesses substantial **covalent** character ($\mathrm{Ag{-}O{-}N{=}O}$). The silver-oxygen bond is intact, leaving the unshared electron pair on the nitrogen atom available to mount the nucleophilic attack. Because nitrogen is less electronegative than oxygen, its electron pair is more readily donated, forming a carbon–nitrogen bond that yields a **nitroalkane** ($\mathrm{R{-}NO_2}$).

# SECTION 2: ELIMINATION REACTIONS & REGIOSELECTIVITY

## 2. Dehydrohalogenation ($\beta$-Elimination / $E2$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

When a haloalkane containing at least one $\beta$-hydrogen atom is heated with a concentrated solution of a strong base (such as alcoholic potassium hydroxide, $\mathrm{alc.\ KOH}$), it undergoes concerted elimination of a halogen atom from the $\alpha$-carbon and a proton from the $\beta$-carbon, generating an **alkene**.

$$
\mathrm{R{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}X} + \mathrm{KOH}_{\text{(alc)}} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{R{-}CH{=}CH_2} + \mathrm{KX} + \mathrm{H_2O}
$$

***

### A. General Mechanism of $E2$ (Elimination Bimolecular)
* **Concerted single-step:** The base abstracts the $\beta$-proton simultaneously with the departure of the $\alpha$-halide leaving group.
* **Stereoelectronic Requirement:** The $\beta\text{--}\mathrm{H}$ and $\alpha\text{--}\mathrm{X}$ must be in an **anti-periplanar** conformation ($180^\circ$ dihedral angle) to allow overlap of breaking $\sigma$-orbitals into the new $\pi$-bond.

$$
\left[\, \mathrm{B}^{\delta-} \cdots \mathrm{H} \cdots \mathrm{C}_{\beta} \mathbin{-\!\!-} \mathrm{C}_{\alpha} \cdots \mathrm{X}^{\delta-} \,\right]^\ddagger \longrightarrow \mathrm{B{-}H} + \mathrm{C{=}C} + \mathrm{X}^-
$$

$$
\text{Rate} = k\,[\text{Alkyl halide}]\,[\text{Base}]
$$

***

### B. Regioselectivity: Zaitsev (Saytzeff) vs. Hofmann Rule

#### 1. Zaitsev's (Saytzeff's) Rule
> *"In dehydrohalogenation reactions, the preferred alkene product is that which has the greater number of alkyl groups attached to the doubly bonded carbon atoms (i.e., the more substituted, thermodynamically stable alkene)."*

* **Origin of Stability:** The more substituted alkene is stabilized by **hyperconjugation** (greater number of stabilizing $\alpha\text{-C--H}$ hyperconjugative structures) and smaller bond angle strain.

**Canonical Example: 2-Bromobutane Dehydrohalogenation**

$$
\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH(Br){-}CH_3} \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\mathrm{alc.\ KOH}} \mathbf{\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH{=}CH{-}CH_3}}\ (81\%,\ \text{Major}) + \mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH{=}CH_2}\ (19\%,\ \text{Minor})
$$

* But-2-ene has $6\ \alpha\text{-hydrogens}$ (more substituted $\to$ Zaitsev product).
* But-1-ene has $2\ \alpha\text{-hydrogens}$ (less substituted $\to$ Hofmann product).

#### 2. Factors Inducing Hofmann Product (Less Substituted Alkene as Major)
1. **Sterically Hindered / Bulky Base:**
   When a bulky base like potassium *tert*-butoxide ($\mathrm{K^+ ^-OC(CH_3)_3}$ or $\mathrm{KO}t\mathrm{Bu}$) is employed, steric congestion prevents abstraction of the internal, more hindered $\beta$-proton. The base abstracts the sterically accessible terminal proton:
   

$$
\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH(Br){-}CH_3} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{KO}t\mathrm{Bu},\ \Delta} \mathbf{\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH{=}CH_2}}\ (\text{Major}) + \mathrm{CH_3{-}CH{=}CH{-}CH_3}\ (\text{Minor})
$$

2. **Poor Leaving Group (Fluoride):**
   Alkyl fluorides ($\mathrm{R{-}F}$) eliminate via a carbanionic transition state ($E1cB$-like) where proton acidity dictates selectivity, yielding terminal alkenes.

***

### C. Competition: Substitution vs. Elimination ($S_N$ vs. $E$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Whether a haloalkane undergoes nucleophilic substitution or elimination depends on substrate structure, base strength/size, and temperature:

```
Substrate Type        Nucleophile / Base Conditions               Dominant Pathway
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Primary (1°)          Weak / Good nucleophile (e.g., I⁻, CN⁻)      SN2 (Major)
Primary (1°)          Bulky base (e.g., KOtBu, Δ)                  E2 (Major)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Secondary (2°)        Weak base / Good nucleophile (I⁻, CN⁻)       SN2 (Major)
Secondary (2°)        Strong base in protic/heat (alc. KOH, Δ)     E2 (Major)
Secondary (2°)        Weak nucleophile / protic (H₂O, MeOH)        SN1 / E1 mixture
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Tertiary (3°)         Strong base (aq. or alc. OH⁻, OR⁻, Δ)        E2 (Alkene exclusively)
Tertiary (3°)         Weak nucleophile (H₂O, EtOH, neutral)        SN1 (Solvolysis)
```

$$
\boxed{\text{Higher Temperature } (\Delta) \implies \text{Favors Elimination ($E$) over Substitution ($S_N$)}}
$$

*Thermodynamic Rationale:* Elimination is an endothermic fragmentation ($\Delta n = +1$ product molecules; $\Delta S^\circ > 0$). Because $\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$, raising $T$ increases the magnitude of $-T\Delta S^\circ$, making elimination thermodynamically more favorable.

# SECTION 3: ORGANOMETALLIC COMPOUNDS OF HALOALKANES

## 3. Preparation & Reactions of Grignard Reagent ($\mathrm{R{-}Mg{-}X}$)
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Compounds containing a direct carbon–metal bond are known as **organometallic compounds**. In 1900, Victor Grignard synthesized alkylmagnesium halides ($\mathrm{R{-}Mg{-}X}$).

### A. Synthesis

$$
\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{Mg} \xrightarrow{\text{Dry, pure ether}} \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}Mg{-}X}}
$$

* **Solvent Requirement:** Anhydrous **diethyl ether** or **tetrahydrofuran (THF)** is mandatory.
* **Coordination stabilization:** The magnesium atom in $\mathrm{R{-}Mg{-}X}$ is electron-deficient. Ether molecules donate lone pairs from oxygen atoms into empty orbitals of magnesium to form a stable dietherate complex:

$$
\begin{array}{c}
\mathrm{R_2'O} \\
\downarrow \\
\mathrm{R{-}Mg{-}X} \\
\uparrow \\
\mathrm{R_2'O}
\end{array}
$$

***

### B. Nature of the Carbon–Magnesium Bond

$$
\mathrm{R}^{\delta-} \mathbin{---} \mathrm{Mg}^{\delta+} \mathbin{---} \mathrm{X}^-
$$

* The $\mathrm{C{-}Mg}$ bond is **polar covalent** ($\sim 35\%$ ionic character) because carbon (electronegativity 2.5) is far more electronegative than magnesium (electronegativity 1.2).
* The carbon atom acts as a powerful **carbanion** equivalent ($\mathrm{R:^-}$), making Grignard reagents exceptionally strong bases as well as formidable nucleophiles.
* The $\mathrm{Mg{-}X}$ bond is essentially **ionic**.

***

### C. Reaction with Active Hydrogen Compounds (Zerewitinoff Reaction)
Grignard reagents react violently and quantitatively with any compound possessing an **active hydrogen** (proton attached to electronegative atoms: $\mathrm{O, N, S}$, or terminal alkyne carbon $\mathrm{C_{sp}}$) to produce **alkanes**.

$$
\mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}MgX}} + \mathrm{H{-}OH} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}H}}\ (\text{Alkane}) + \mathrm{Mg(OH)X}\downarrow
$$

$$
\mathrm{CH_3{-}MgBr} + \mathrm{C_2H_5OH} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\mathrm{CH_4}}\uparrow + \mathrm{Mg(OC_2H_5)Br}
$$

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2{-}MgBr} + \mathrm{NH_3} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\mathrm{CH_3CH_3}} + \mathrm{Mg(NH_2)Br}
$$

$$
\mathrm{R{-}MgX} + \mathrm{D_2O} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}D}} + \mathrm{Mg(OD)X}
$$

> [!CAUTION]
> **Why must Grignard reactions be maintained under strictly anhydrous conditions?**
> Grignard reagents are strong bases. Even trace moisture hydrolyzes them instantly into corresponding alkanes, quenching the reagent before it can undergo substitution or addition:
> 

$$
\mathrm{R{-}MgX} + \mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R{-}H} + \mathrm{Mg(OH)X}
$$

## 4. Wurtz Reaction
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Haloalkanes react with metallic sodium in dry ether to form symmetrical alkanes with **double** the number of carbon atoms.

$$
\mathrm{R{-}X} + 2\mathrm{Na} + \mathrm{X{-}R} \xrightarrow{\text{Dry Ether}} \mathbf{\mathrm{R{-}R}} + 2\mathrm{NaX}
$$

$$
\mathrm{2\,CH_3{-}CH_2{-}Br} + 2\mathrm{Na} \xrightarrow{\text{Dry Ether}} \mathbf{\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}}\ (\text{n-Butane}) + 2\mathrm{NaBr}
$$

### Mechanistic Pathways
* **Ionic Route:**
  

$$
\mathrm{R{-}X} + 2\mathrm{Na} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R^-Na^+} + \mathrm{NaX}
$$

  

$$
\mathrm{R^-Na^+} + \mathrm{R{-}X} \xrightarrow{S_N2} \mathrm{R{-}R} + \mathrm{NaX}
$$

* **Free Radical Route:**
  

$$
\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{Na} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R}^\bullet + \mathrm{NaX}
$$

  

$$
2\,\mathrm{R}^\bullet \longrightarrow \mathrm{R{-}R}
$$

### Synthetic Limitations
1. **Unsymmetrical Alkanes Give Low Yields:**
   Reacting a mixture of two different alkyl halides ($\mathrm{R{-}X} + \mathrm{R'{-}X}$) yields three different alkanes ($\mathrm{R{-}R}$, $\mathrm{R'{-}R'}$, and $\mathrm{R{-}R'}$). Because their boiling points are close, separating them by fractional distillation is difficult:
   

$$
\mathrm{CH_3Br} + \mathrm{C_2H_5Br} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Na/dry\ ether}} \mathrm{C_2H_6} + \mathrm{C_4H_{10}} + \mathrm{C_3H_8}\ (\text{Separation bottleneck})
$$

2. **Cannot Synthesize Methane:** The smallest possible coupling product is ethane ($\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_3}$).
3. **Side-Reaction with $3^\circ$ Halides:**
   Tertiary alkyl halides undergo $E2$ elimination instead of coupling due to steric congestion and the high basicity of sodium/organosodium intermediates, producing alkenes.

### Question 1 [CBSE Board 2024, 3 Marks]
**(a)** Out of $S_N1$ and $S_N2$, which reaction mechanism is accompanied by stereochemical inversion of configuration? Explain the mechanistic reason.  
**(b)** Which one of the following compounds will react faster in $S_N1$ reaction with $\mathrm{OH}^-$? Give reasons.

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5{-}CH_2{-}Cl} \quad \text{or} \quad \mathrm{C_6H_5{-}CH(Cl){-}CH_3}
$$

**Solution:**
**(a)**  
* The **$S_N2$** mechanism proceeds with complete **inversion of configuration** (Walden Inversion).  
* **Reason:** The nucleophile attacks from the backside of the carbon atom directly opposite to the leaving group ($180^\circ$ angle) to minimize steric and electrostatic repulsion. As the leaving group departs from the front face, the three non-reacting ligands are forced into an inverted umbrella arrangement.

**(b)**  
* $\mathbf{\mathrm{C_6H_5{-}CH(Cl){-}CH_3}}$ (1-chloro-1-phenylethane) reacts faster via $S_N1$.  
* **Reason:** The rate-determining step of $S_N1$ produces a carbocation intermediate. Ionization of $\mathrm{C_6H_5{-}CH(Cl){-}CH_3}$ yields the secondary benzylic carbocation $[\mathrm{C_6H_5{-}\overset{+}{C}H{-}CH_3}]$, which is stabilized both by extended resonance over the aromatic ring and by hyperconjugation from the three $\alpha$-hydrogens of the adjacent methyl group ($+I$ and hyperconjugation).  
In contrast, $\mathrm{C_6H_5{-}CH_2{-}Cl}$ produces a primary benzylic carbocation $[\mathrm{C_6H_5{-}CH_2^+}]$, which lacks this hyperconjugative stabilization.

### Question 2 [CBSE Board 2023, 2 Marks]
Give reasons for the following observations:  
**(i)** Haloalkanes react with $\mathrm{KCN}$ to form alkyl cyanides as main product, while with $\mathrm{AgCN}$ they form isocyanides.  
**(ii)** The dipole moment of chlorobenzene is lower than that of cyclohexyl chloride.

**Solution:**
**(i)**  
* $\mathrm{KCN}$ is predominantly **ionic** and produces free ambident cyanide ions ($[:\!\mathrm{C{\equiv}N}:]^-$). Since the $\mathrm{C{-}C}$ bond is thermodynamically stronger and more stable than the $\mathrm{C{-}N}$ bond, nucleophilic attack occurs through carbon, forming **alkyl cyanides** ($\mathrm{R{-}CN}$).  
* $\mathrm{AgCN}$ is predominantly **covalent** ($\mathrm{Ag{-}C{\equiv}N}$), leaving only the lone pair on nitrogen free to mount the nucleophilic attack, producing **alkyl isocyanides** ($\mathrm{R{-}NC}$).

**(ii)**  
* In chlorobenzene, the carbon attached to chlorine is $sp^2$-hybridized (with higher $s$-character, $33.3\%$), making it more electronegative and less willing to release electron density to chlorine. In cyclohexyl chloride, the ring carbon is $sp^3$-hybridized ($25\%\ s$-character, less electronegative).  
* Resonance delocalization in chlorobenzene imparts partial double bond character to the $\mathrm{C{-}Cl}$ bond, shortening bond length ($d$). Since dipole moment $\mu = q \times d$, this shorter bond length and opposing resonance dipole reduce the dipole moment of chlorobenzene ($\mu \approx 1.69\ \mathrm{D}$) relative to cyclohexyl chloride ($\mu \approx 1.86\ \mathrm{D}$).

Predict the order of reactivity of the following compounds towards $S_N2$ displacement and justify your answer:

$$
\mathrm{2\text{-}Bromo\text{-}2\text{-}methylbutane},\quad \mathrm{1\text{-}Bromopentane},\quad \mathrm{2\text{-}Bromopentane}
$$

**Solution:**
* **Structures:**
  * 1-Bromopentane: $\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_3CH_2Br}$ ($1^\circ$ alkyl halide)

# SECTION 5: EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

> [!WARNING]
> ### 1. Solvent Confusion: Aqueous $\mathrm{KOH}$ vs. Alcoholic $\mathrm{KOH}$
> * **$\mathrm{KOH}_{\text{(aq)}}$:** Ionizes to provide hydrated hydroxide ions ($\mathrm{OH^-}$), which act as **nucleophiles**, driving **substitution** ($S_N$):
>   

$$
\mathrm{R{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}X} + \mathrm{KOH}_{\text{(aq)}} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}OH}
$$

> * **$\mathrm{KOH}_{\text{(alc)}}$:** Dissolved in alcohol to form ethoxide ions ($\mathrm{C_2H_5O^-}$), which act as strong, sterically hindered **Brønsted bases**, driving **elimination** ($E2$):
>   

$$
\mathrm{R{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}X} + \mathrm{KOH}_{\text{(alc)}} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{R{-}CH{=}CH_2}
$$

> * *Common error:* Writing an alkene product for aqueous $\mathrm{KOH}$, or an alcohol for alcoholic $\mathrm{KOH}$.

***

> [!CAUTION]
> ### 2. The $\mathrm{AgCN} / \mathrm{KCN}$ Inversion Trap
> * Writing an alkyl cyanide ($\mathrm{R{-}CN}$) for $\mathrm{AgCN}$ costs full marks.
> * *Correction:* Always pair **$\mathrm{Ag}$ salts with the less electronegative donor atom** ($\mathrm{AgCN} \to \mathrm{R{-}NC}$; $\mathrm{AgNO_2} \to \mathrm{R{-}NO_2}$).
> * Always pair **$\mathrm{K}$ salts with the atom forming the stronger bond or holding negative charge** ($\mathrm{KCN} \to \mathrm{R{-}CN}$; $\mathrm{KNO_2} \to \mathrm{R{-}ONO}$).

***

> [!IMPORTANT]
> ### 3. Optical Inactivity Terminology: Racemic Mixture vs. Meso Compound
> * *Error:* Stating that an $S_N1$ product is optically inactive due to *internal compensation*.
> * *Correction:* A racemic mixture from an $S_N1$ reaction is optically inactive due to **external compensation** (equimolar mixture of two separate enantiomeric species whose optical rotations cancel). *Internal compensation* applies only to meso compounds possessing an internal plane of symmetry.

***

> [!WARNING]
> ### 4. Grignard Reagent Exposure to Air / Moisture
> * In conversion problems, writing $\mathrm{R{-}MgX}$ reacting with a reagent that contains water or alcohol without noting that the Grignard reagent will be destroyed is a frequent mistake. Any protic solvent converts $\mathrm{RMgX}$ directly into an alkane:
>   

$$
\mathrm{CH_3MgBr} + \mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH} \longrightarrow \mathrm{CH_4} + \mathrm{Mg(OCH_2CH_3)Br}
$$

## 1. Foundational Theory, Physical Laws & NCERT Definitions

### 1.1 Optical Activity and Polarimetry
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
**NCERT Definition:** Certain organic compounds, when placed in the path of plane polarized light (PPL), rotate the plane of polarization through a characteristic angle. This property is termed **optical activity**, and such compounds are designated as **optically active**.

#### The Physical Genesis of Plane Polarized Light:
1. **Ordinary Light:** An electromagnetic wave with electric field vectors ($\vec{E}$) vibrating randomly in all planes perpendicular to the propagation axis.
2. **Polarizer (Nicol Prism / Polaroid):** Consists of calcite ($\text{CaCO}_3$) cleaved along specific crystallographic axes. By birefringence and total internal reflection, it transmits light vibrating in only a single plane: **Plane Polarized Light (PPL)**.
3. **Polarimeter Tube:** Contains the solution of the chiral organic analyte at known concentration ($c$) and path length ($l$).

#### Direction of Rotation:
* **Dextrorotatory ($d$ or $(+)$):** Rotation of the plane of PPL to the **right** (clockwise).
* **Laevorotatory ($l$ or $(-)$):** Rotation of the plane of PPL to the **left** (anticlockwise).
* *Crucial NCERT Distinction:* The $(+)$ and $(-)$ signs designate the experimentally observed optical rotation and **have no direct correlation** with the absolute configuration $(R/S)$ or Fischer projection relative configuration $(D/L)$.

***

### 1.2 Quantitative Physical Law: Specific Rotation $[\alpha]_\lambda^T$
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐  
The angle of rotation $\alpha$ observed in a polarimeter depends on the analyte's molecular structure, the number of interacting molecules (concentration and path length), wavelength of light, and temperature.

$$
\alpha \propto l \cdot c
$$

$$
\boxed{[\alpha]_{\lambda}^{T} = \frac{\alpha}{l \cdot c}}
$$

Where:
* $\alpha$ = Observed angle of rotation in degrees ($^\circ$)
* $l$ = Path length of polarimeter tube in decimeters ($\text{dm}$) ($1\text{ dm} = 10\text{ cm}$)
* $c$ = Concentration of the enantiomer solution in grams per milliliter ($\text{g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$ or $\text{g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$)
* $T$ = Temperature (standard is typically $298\text{ K}$ or $20^\circ\text{C}$ / $25^\circ\text{C}$)
* $\lambda$ = Wavelength of incident monochromatic beam (conventionally the Sodium D-line, $\lambda = 589\text{ nm}$ or $5893\text{ Å}$)
* **SI / Conventional Unit of $[\alpha]$:** $\text{deg}\cdot\text{dm}^{-1}\cdot\text{g}^{-1}\cdot\text{cm}^{3}$ (frequently reported in degrees, $^\circ$).

***

### 1.3 Molecular Asymmetry, Chirality, and Stereocentres
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  
* **Asymmetric Carbon (Chiral Carbon / Stereocentre):** An $sp^3$-hybridized carbon bonded to **four completely different atoms or groups**:

$$
\text{C}^*(\text{R}_1)(\text{R}_2)(\text{R}_3)(\text{R}_4) \quad \text{where } \text{R}_1 \neq \text{R}_2 \neq \text{R}_3 \neq \text{R}_4
$$

* **Chirality (Handedness):** The geometric property of any object/molecule of being **non-superimposable on its mirror image**.
* **Achiral:** Objects/molecules that are superimposable on their mirror images. Achiral molecules possess at least one symmetry element:
  * **Plane of Symmetry ($\sigma$):** An imaginary plane bisecting the molecule into two identical halves that reflect each other.
  * **Center of Inversion ($i$):** An inversion point through which reflection inverts all coordinates $(x,y,z) \rightarrow (-x,-y,-z)$ into identical atoms.
* **Criterion of Optical Activity:** **Molecular asymmetry (absence of any improper axis of rotation $S_n$, specifically plane of symmetry $\sigma$ and centre of symmetry $i$)**. A stereocentre is neither strictly necessary nor sufficient alone (e.g., meso-compounds have stereocentres but are achiral; chiral allenes/binaphthyls lack chiral carbons but are chiral).

***

### 1.4 Enantiomers, Diastereomers, and Meso Compounds
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

| Parameter | Enantiomers | Diastereomers | Meso Compounds |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mirror Image Relationship** | Non-superimposable mirror images | Stereoisomers that are **not** mirror images | Superimposable on mirror image despite chiral centres |
| **Chirality** | Chiral | Can be chiral or achiral | Achiral |
| **Optical Activity** | Optically active ($+ / -$) | Optically active (if chiral) | Optically inactive ($[\alpha] = 0^\circ$) |
| **Reason for Inactivity** | N/A | N/A | **Internal Compensation** (plane of symmetry $\sigma$) |
| **Physical Properties** (b.p., m.p., density, refractive index) | **Identical** (in an achiral environment) | **Different** | Different from stereoisomers |
| **Separation Method** | Cannot be separated by fractional distillation; requires **chiral resolving agents** | Easily separated by fractional crystallization / distillation | Standard separation methods |
| **Sign & Magnitude of Rotation** | Equal magnitude, **opposite sign**: $[\alpha]_A = -[\alpha]_B$ | Different magnitude, may have same/opposite sign | Zero rotation |

### 1.5 Inversion, Retention, and Racemisation
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

Let a spatial arrangement at a chiral center be defined with reference to three non-reacting ligands ($a, b, c$) and a leaving group ($X$) replaced by nucleophile ($Y$).

#### 1. Inversion of Configuration (Walden Inversion)
* Occurs when the nucleophile attacks from the **backside** (at an angle of $180^\circ$ relative to the carbon-leaving group bond).
* The three unperturbed ligands flex through a planar transition state like an umbrella turning inside out in a gale.
* The absolute spatial disposition of the groups around the stereocentre is **inverted**.

#### 2. Retention of Configuration
* Occurs when the spatial integrity of bonds around the asymmetric carbon is preserved throughout the reaction.
* Typically observed when:
  * The reaction does **not** involve cleavage of any bond directly attached to the asymmetric stereocentre.
  * Or during an $\text{S}_\text{N}\text{i}$ mechanism (e.g., chlorination by $\text{SOCl}_2$ in non-polar ether without pyridine).

#### 3. Racemisation
* The conversion of an optically active enantiomer (pure $(+)$ or $(-)$) into an equimolar ($1:1$) mixture of both $(+)$ and $(-)$ enantiomers.
* A $50:50$ mixture is called a **Racemic Modification** or **Racemic Mixture** ($dl$ or $(\pm)$).
* **Net Optical Rotation of Racemic Mixture:**

$$
[\alpha]_{\text{racemic}} = 0.5 \cdot [\alpha]_{(+)} + 0.5 \cdot [\alpha]_{(-)} = 0^\circ
$$

* **Mechanism of Inactivity:** **External Compensation** (the clockwise rotation produced by molecules of one enantiomer is canceled out by the equal and opposite counterclockwise rotation produced by an equal number of molecules of the other enantiomer).

## 2. Mechanisms, Stereochemical Proofs & Reaction Coordinate Pathways

### 2.1 $\text{S}_\text{N}2$ Stereochemical Pathway: Complete Stereospecific Inversion
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

#### Reaction Conditions:
* **Substrate:** Primary ($1^\circ$) or unhindered secondary ($2^\circ$) chiral haloalkane (e.g., $(-) \text{-} 2\text{-bromooctane}$).
* **Nucleophile:** Strong, non-bulky, unhindered nucleophile (e.g., $\text{OH}^-$, $\text{CN}^-$, $\text{I}^-$).
* **Solvent:** Polar Aprotic Solvent (DMF, DMSO, Acetone) to prevent nucleophile solvation shells.
* **Kinetics:** Bimolecular, second-order rate law:

$$
\text{Rate} = k_2 [\text{R-X}][\text{Nu}^-]
$$

#### Detailed Step-by-Step Mechanism:
1. **Concerted Backside Attack:** The nucleophile ($\text{Nu}^-$) donates its non-bonding electron pair into the empty $\sigma^*$ (antibonding) orbital of the $\text{C}-\text{X}$ bond from the direction directly opposite to the departing halogen atom ($180^\circ$ trajectory).
2. **Penta-coordinate Activated Complex (Transition State):**
   * Central carbon undergoes dynamic rehybridization from $sp^3$ towards a planar $sp^2$-like geometry.
   * Three spectator groups occupy equatorial positions in a plane with bond angles approaching $120^\circ$.
   * Both the incoming nucleophile and the departing leaving group occupy axial positions via partial bonds formed by a non-hybridized $p$-orbital.

$$
\text{Nu}^- + \begin{pmatrix} \text{R}_1 \\ | \\ \text{C} - \text{X} \\ / \quad \backslash \\ \text{R}_2 \quad \text{R}_3 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\text{Slow}} \left[ \delta^-\text{Nu} \cdots \begin{matrix} \text{R}_1 \\ | \\ \text{C} \\ / \quad \backslash \\ \text{R}_2 \quad \text{R}_3 \end{matrix} \cdots \text{X}\delta^- \right]^{\ddagger} \xrightarrow{\text{Fast}} \begin{pmatrix} \text{R}_1 \\ | \\ \text{Nu} - \text{C} \\ / \quad \backslash \\ \text{R}_3 \quad \text{R}_2 \end{pmatrix} + \text{X}^-
$$

#### Classical Stereochemical Proof:
Hydrolysis of $(-)\text{-}2\text{-bromooctane}$ with aqueous $\text{NaOH}$ leads exclusively to $(+)\text{-octan-2-ol}$:

$$
(-)\text{-}(R)\text{-}2\text{-bromooctane} + \text{OH}^- \xrightarrow{\text{S}_\text{N}2} (+)\text{-}(S)\text{-octan-2-ol} + \text{Br}^-
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Stereochemical Outcome: } 100\% \text{ Inversion of Configuration (Stereospecific)}}
$$

***

### 2.2 $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ Stereochemical Pathway: Carbocation Intermediate and Racemisation
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐  

#### Reaction Conditions:
* **Substrate:** Tertiary ($3^\circ$) or resonance-stabilized benzylic/allylic chiral haloalkane.
* **Nucleophile:** Weak/neutral nucleophile ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{ROH}$).
* **Solvent:** Polar Protic Solvent ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) to solvate ions by dipole-dipole interaction and hydrogen bonding.
* **Kinetics:** Unimolecular, first-order rate law:

$$
\text{Rate} = k_1 [\text{R-X}]
$$

#### Detailed Step-by-Step Mechanism:

**Step 1: Heterolytic Bond Cleavage (Rate-Determining Step, Slow)**
The polar protic solvent assists the departure of $\text{X}^-$. The tetrahedral chiral carbon ($sp^3$) flattens into an achiral carbocation intermediate ($sp^2$ planar):

$$
\text{R}_1\text{R}_2\text{R}_3\text{C}-\text{X} \xrightarrow[\text{slow}]{k_1} \left[ \text{R}_1\text{R}_2\text{R}_3\text{C} \right]^+ + \text{X}^-
$$

$$
\text{Geometry of Intermediate: Trigonal Planar } (sp^2), \text{ Bond Angles } \approx 120^\circ, \text{ Empty } 2p_z \text{ Orbital}
$$

**Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack on Planar Carbocation (Fast)**
The nucleophile can attack the unhybridized, planar $2p_z$ orbital with equal geometric probability from either face:
* **Frontside Attack (Retention):** Nucleophile approaches from the same side the leaving group departed $\rightarrow$ product preserves configuration.
* **Backside Attack (Inversion):** Nucleophile approaches from the opposite face $\rightarrow$ inverted configuration.

```
                  Face A Attack (Backside: 50%)
                 -----------------------------> Inverted Enantiomer (50%)
                |
     R1         |
     |         / 
     C(+) <--- Empty 2pz
   /   \       \
  R2    R3      |
                 -----------------------------> Retained Enantiomer (50%)
                  Face B Attack (Frontside: 50%)
```

#### Classical Stereochemical Example:
Hydrolysis of optically active $(-)\text{-}2\text{-bromobutane}$ under $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ conditions:

$$
(-)\text{-}2\text{-bromobutane} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{S}_\text{N}1} (\pm)\text{-butan-2-ol} + \text{HBr}
$$

#### Physical Reality (NCERT Note on Intimate Ion Pairs):
In practical polar systems, complete ($100\%$) racemisation is rarely achieved. The leaving group $\text{X}^-$ departs and temporarily shields the front face as part of an **intimate ion pair** $[\text{R}^+ \parallel \text{X}^-]$. Consequently:

$$
\% \text{ Backside Attack (Inversion)} > \% \text{ Frontside Attack (Retention)}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Net Practical Outcome: Racemisation with Partial Inversion}}
$$

***

### 2.3 Reactions Without Cleavage of Chiral Bonds: Retention of Configuration
**Priority:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐  
When an optically active substrate reacts without breaking any of the four direct covalent bonds to the asymmetric stereocentre, the spatial arrangement of the remaining ligands is unchanged.

#### Quantitative Model:
Conversion of $(-)\text{-}2\text{-methylbutan-1-ol}$ to $(+)\text{-}1\text{-chloro-2-methylbutane}$ using concentrated $\text{HCl}/\text{heat}$:

$$
\begin{matrix}
\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2-\overset{\text{C}^*}{\text{C}}(\text{CH}_3)\text{H}-\text{CH}_2-\text{OH} \\
(-)\text{-}2\text{-methylbutan-1-ol}
\end{matrix}
\xrightarrow{\text{HCl},\ \Delta}
\begin{matrix}
\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2-\overset{\text{C}^*}{\text{C}}(\text{CH}_3)\text{H}-\text{CH}_2-\text{Cl} \\
(+)\text{-}1\text{-chloro-2-methylbutane}
\end{matrix} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

* **Mechanism Note:** The cleavage takes place at the $\text{C}-\text{O}$ bond of the achiral $-\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$ group, leaving the $\text{C}^*$ stereocentre unperturbed.
* **Why the sign of rotation changes ($(-)$ to $(+)$):** The sign and magnitude of rotation depend on the polarizability and mass distribution of the whole molecule. A change in optical sign does **not** signify an inversion of configuration at the stereocentre.

$$
\boxed{\text{Stereochemical Outcome: Absolute Retention of Configuration}}
$$

## 4. Examiner Traps, Common Mistakes & Remedial Checklist

### 1. The Sign Confusion Trap: $(+) / (-)$ vs. $D / L$ vs. $R / S$
* **Error:** Students assume that a dextrorotatory $(+)$ compound must have $(R)$ or $(D)$ configuration, and inversion of configuration always converts $(+)$ to $(-)$.
* **Correction:** 
  * $(+) / (-)$ is an **experimentally measured physical property** via a polarimeter.
  * $D / L$ and $R / S$ are **notational conventions** based on IUPAC/Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules.
  * An inversion of configuration does **not** guarantee a flip from $(+)$ to $(-)$. It guarantees an inversion of spatial coordinates at the stereocentre.

### 2. External vs. Internal Compensation
* **Error:** Stating that a racemic mixture is inactive due to "internal compensation".
* **Correction:**
  * **Racemic mixture $(\pm)$** $\rightarrow$ Optically inactive due to **EXTERNAL COMPENSATION** (intermolecular: between two distinct enantiomeric molecules).
  * **Meso compound** $\rightarrow$ Optically inactive due to **INTERNAL COMPENSATION** (intramolecular: between two halves of the identical molecule).

### 3. Asymmetric Carbon vs. Chiral Molecule
* **Error:** Writing "a molecule is optically active because it contains an asymmetric carbon."
* **Correction:** The presence of a stereocentre does **not** guarantee optical activity. *Meso*-tartaric acid contains two asymmetric carbon atoms, yet it is completely achiral and optically inactive. The strictly necessary and sufficient condition is **molecular asymmetry / non-superimposability on its mirror image**.

### 4. Polarimeter Units Slip in Calculation Questions
* **Error:** Directly substituting path length in centimeters ($l = 20\text{ cm}$) or concentration in $\text{g}/100\text{ mL}$ directly as $c$.
* **Correction:** In $[\alpha] = \frac{\alpha}{l \cdot c}$:
  * $l$ must always be converted to **decimeters**: $20\text{ cm} = 2.0\text{ dm}$.
  * $c$ must be in $\text{g}\cdot\text{cm}^{-3}$ ($\text{g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$): $5\text{ g in } 100\text{ mL} \implies c = 0.05\text{ g}\cdot\text{mL}^{-1}$.

### 5. Incomplete Description of $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ Racemisation
* **Error:** Writing "The carbocation is planar so the product is completely racemic."
* **Correction:** Board answer schemes look for:
  1. Formation of a planar, $sp^2$-hybridized achiral carbocation.
  2. Attack of nucleophile from **both faces (front and back) with equal probability**.
  3. Mention of intimate ion-pair effects (if asked for real yields: racemisation with partial inversion).

## 1. Pedagogical Theory & Concepts

### 1.1 Dual Electronic Nature of Halogens on the Benzene Ring

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

Halogens attached directly to an $sp^2$-hybridized aromatic ring exhibit two competing electronic effects:

1. **Strong Inductive Effect ($-I$ Effect):**
   - Halogens ($\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$) possess higher electronegativities than $sp^2$-hybridized aromatic carbon ($\chi_{\text{Cl}} \approx 3.16$, $\chi_{\text{C}(sp^2)} \approx 2.75$).
   - The $-I$ effect pulls $\sigma$-electron density away from the aromatic nucleus, permanently decreasing electron density across all ring carbons.
   - **Consequence:** Deactivation of the ring toward electrophiles relative to benzene.

2. **Resonance Effect ($+R$ / $+M$ Effect):**
   - The unshared non-bonding electron pairs ($p$-orbital lone pairs) on the halogen atom undergo delocalization with the adjacent $\pi$-system of the benzene ring.
   - Overlap between carbon $2p$ and halogen $np$ orbitals donates $\pi$-electron density into the ring.

```
       :Cl:                   :Cl:+                  :Cl:+                  :Cl:+                  :Cl:
      .. /                   .. /                   .. /                   .. /                   .. /
    //    \\               //    \\               /      \\              //    \               //    \\
   |        |   <--->     |        |-  <--->     |        |   <--->     |-      |   <--->     |        |
    \\    //               \\    //               \\    //-              \\    //              \\    //
      \--/                   \--/                   \--/                   \--/                   \--/
       (I)                   (II)                   (III)                  (IV)                    (V)
```

- **Resonance Canonical Analysis:**
  - Structure **II** places a formal negative charge on the **ortho** position (C-2).
  - Structure **III** places a formal negative charge on the **para** position (C-4).
  - Structure **IV** places a formal negative charge on the other **ortho** position (C-6).
  - **Zero** resonance structures place a formal negative charge on the **meta** positions (C-3 and C-5).

#### Resolution of the Paradox (Orientation vs. Reactivity)
- **Net Reactivity (Controlled by $-I$):** The $-I$ effect of halogens is stronger than the $+R$ effect. Hence, there is a net withdrawal of electron density from the ring, making haloarenes **less reactive (deactivated)** toward electrophilic attack than unsubstituted benzene.
- **Orientation (Controlled by $+R$):** The $+R$ effect selectively increases the electron density at the *ortho* and *para* positions relative to the *meta* positions. Therefore, incoming electrophiles ($E^+$) preferentially attack at **ortho** and **para** sites.
- **Thermodynamic vs. Steric Outcome:** Between the two isomers, the **para-isomer is the major product** due to minimal steric hindrance between the incoming bulky electrophile and the existing halogen atom, combined with higher molecular symmetry allowing better crystal packing.

## 2. Chemical Reactions & Step-by-Step Mechanisms

### 2.1 Halogenation of Haloarenes

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

Electrophilic chlorination of chlorobenzene requires a Lewis acid catalyst ($\text{FeCl}_3$ or anhydrous $\text{Fe}$) to polarize the halogen molecule.

#### Generation of the Electrophile (Chloronium Ion, $\text{Cl}^+$)

$$
\text{Cl}-\text{Cl} + \text{FeCl}_3 \rightleftharpoons \left[ \text{Cl}^{\delta+}\cdots\text{Cl}\cdots\text{FeCl}_3^{\delta-} \right] \rightleftharpoons \text{Cl}^+ + [\text{FeCl}_4]^-
$$

#### Stoichiometric Equation

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Anhydrous }\text{FeCl}_3,\;\Delta} \underset{\text{1,2-Dichlorobenzene (Minor)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}_2} + \underset{\text{1,4-Dichlorobenzene (Major)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}_2} + \text{HCl}
$$

### 2.2 Nitration of Haloarenes

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

Nitration is carried out using nitrating mixture: concentrated nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$, $68\%\text{ w/w}$) and concentrated sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, $98\%\text{ w/w}$).

#### Mechanism of Electrophile Generation ($\text{NO}_2^+$)
Sulfuric acid acts as a Brønsted acid (proton donor) and nitric acid acts as a Brønsted base (proton acceptor):

$$
\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HSO}_4^-
$$

$$
\text{HNO}_3 + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{O}^+-\text{NO}_2
$$

$$
\text{H}_2\text{O}^+-\text{NO}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{slow}} \text{H}_2\text{O} + \underset{\text{Nitronium Ion (Electrophile)}}{\text{NO}_2^+}
$$

$$
\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{HSO}_4^-
$$

$$
\text{Overall: } \text{HNO}_3 + 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightleftharpoons \text{NO}_2^+ + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + 2\text{HSO}_4^-
$$

#### Stoichiometric Equation

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{HNO}_3\text{ (conc.)} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4,\; \Delta} \underset{\text{1-Chloro-2-nitrobenzene (Minor)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}(\text{NO}_2)} + \underset{\text{1-Chloro-4-nitrobenzene (Major)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}(\text{NO}_2)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

### 2.4 Friedel-Crafts Alkylation and Acylation

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

#### A. Friedel-Crafts Alkylation
Reaction with an alkyl halide in the presence of anhydrous aluminium chloride ($\text{AlCl}_3$):

1. **Electrophile Generation:**
   

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{AlCl}_3 \rightleftharpoons \left[\text{CH}_3^{\delta+}\cdots\text{Cl}\cdots\text{AlCl}_3^{\delta-}\right] \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3^+ + [\text{AlCl}_4]^-
$$

2. **Stoichiometric Equation:**
   

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\text{Anhydrous }\text{AlCl}_3} \underset{\text{1-Chloro-2-methylbenzene (Minor)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}(\text{CH}_3)} + \underset{\text{1-Chloro-4-methylbenzene (Major)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}(\text{CH}_3)} + \text{HCl}
$$

#### B. Friedel-Crafts Acylation
Reaction with an acyl halide (or acid anhydride) in the presence of anhydrous aluminium chloride:

1. **Electrophile Generation (Acylium Ion):**
   

$$
\text{CH}_3-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{Cl} + \text{AlCl}_3 \rightleftharpoons \left[\text{CH}_3-\overset{+}{\text{C}}=\text{O} \longleftrightarrow \text{CH}_3-\text{C}\equiv\overset{+}{\text{O}}\right] + [\text{AlCl}_4]^-
$$

2. **Stoichiometric Equation:**
   

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{CH}_3\text{COCl} \xrightarrow{\text{Anhydrous }\text{AlCl}_3} \underset{\text{2-Chloroacetophenone (Minor)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}(\text{COCH}_3)} + \underset{\text{4-Chloroacetophenone (Major)}}{\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\text{Cl}(\text{COCH}_3)} + \text{HCl}
$$

> [!NOTE]
> The product is named as a derivative of acetophenone because $-\text{COCH}_3$ takes principal functional group priority over the chloro substituent.

### 2.5 Arenium Ion (Wheland Intermediate) Stability Derivation

To prove why *ortho/para* attack is favored over *meta* attack:

#### Attack at Ortho-Position:
```
           Cl                      Cl                     Cl                     :Cl:+
         // \                    // \                   // \                   //  \
        |    | + E+  ----->     |    |-- E   <--->     |    |-- E   <--->     |     |-- E
         \\ /                  + \\ /                   \--/+                  \\  /
                                  H                       H                      H
                              (Carbocation)          (Carbocation)         (EXTRA STABLE:
                                                                          Octet complete on all atoms)
```

#### Attack at Para-Position:
```
           Cl                         :Cl:+                      Cl                       Cl
         // \                        //  \                     // \                     // \
        |    | + E+  ----->         |     |          <--->    |    |          <--->    +|   |
         \\ /                      + \\  /                     \\ /+                    \\ /
                                      |                          |                        |
                                    H-C-E                      H-C-E                    H-C-E
                                (EXTRA STABLE)
```

#### Attack at Meta-Position:
```
           Cl                    Cl                    Cl
         // \                  // \                  // \
        |    | + E+  ----->  +|    |-- E   <--->    |    |-- E   <--->   (No resonance contributor
         \\ /                  \\ /                  \\ /+                allows chlorine lone pair
                                  H                     H                 to share positive charge)
```

**Conclusion:** For *ortho* and *para* attacks, an especially stable resonance contributor forms where the positive charge is directly delocalized onto the halogen atom, giving **all atoms (including carbon and halogen) complete octets**. For *meta* attack, the positive charge never lands on the carbon bearing the halogen, preventing the halogen's lone pair from providing direct $+R$ stabilization.

### 3.1 Dichloromethane (Methylene Chloride, $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$)

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

- **Industrial Applications:**
  - Widely used as an industrial solvent and paint stripper/remover.
  - Used as a propellant in aerosols and process solvent in pharmaceutical manufacturing.
  - Solvent in food chemistry (decaffeination of coffee and tea, extraction of flavourings).
- **Human Toxicity and Physiological Impact:**
  - **Central Nervous System (CNS):** Exposure to lower levels impairs hearing and vision. High air levels cause dizziness, nausea, tingling, numbness in fingers and toes.
  - **Metabolic Pathway:** Inhaled $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$ is metabolized by hepatic cytochrome P450 to carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$), forming **carboxyhemoglobin ($\text{COHb}$)**, leading to impaired oxygen-carrying capacity.
  - **Direct Contact:** Direct skin contact causes intense burning and mild erythema; cornea damage occurs upon direct splash exposure.

***

### 3.2 Trichloromethane (Chloroform, $\text{CHCl}_3$)

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

- **Primary Uses:**
  - Precursor to fluorocarbon refrigerant **R-22 (chlorodifluoromethane)**:
    

$$
\text{CHCl}_3 + 2\text{HF} \xrightarrow{\text{SbCl}_5} \underset{\text{Freon-22}}{\text{CHClF}_2} + 2\text{HCl}
$$

  - Historically used as an inhalation anesthetic; replaced by safer alternatives like halothane ($\text{CF}_3\text{CHBrCl}$).
- **The Phosgene Degradation Trap:**
  Chloroform is oxidized slowly by air in the presence of light to form an extremely poisonous gas, **carbonyl chloride (phosgene, $\text{COCl}_2$)**:

  

$$
\boxed{2\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu\text{ (light)}} 2\text{COCl}_2\text{ (phosgene)} + 2\text{HCl}}
$$

- **NCERT Storage Mandate:**
  1. Stored in **closed, dark amber-colored bottles** to exclude light.
  2. Filled completely **up to the brim** to exclude air (oxygen).
  3. **Preservation Agent:** $\approx 1\%$ **ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$)** is added. Ethanol converts any trace phosgene formed into non-toxic **diethyl carbonate**:
     

$$
\text{COCl}_2 + 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \longrightarrow \underset{\text{Diethyl carbonate (non-toxic)}}{(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{O})_2\text{C}=\text{O}} + 2\text{HCl}
$$

***

### 3.3 Triiodomethane (Iodoform, $\text{CHI}_3$)

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

- **Physical Properties:** Bright yellow crystalline solid, melting point $119^\circ\text{C}$, characteristic pungent/medicinal odor.
- **Antiseptic Action Mechanism:**
  

$$
\boxed{\text{CHI}_3\text{ does NOT possess intrinsic antiseptic activity.}}
$$

  Its antiseptic properties are due entirely to the **slow liberation of free molecular iodine ($\text{I}_2$)** upon contact with skin/organic tissue:
  

$$
\text{CHI}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{tissue contact}} \text{I}_2\text{ (free iodine, the active bactericide)}
$$

- Due to its objectionable odor, it has been replaced by modern formulations containing povidone-iodine.

***

### 3.4 Tetrachloromethane (Carbon Tetrachloride, $\text{CCl}_4$)

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

- **Industrial Applications:**
  - Manufacture of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and aerosol propellants.
  - Solvent for oils, fats, resins, and iodine.
  - Used commercially as a fire extinguisher under the trade name **Pyrene** (its heavy, non-combustible vapors blanket the fire, cutting off oxygen).
- **Environmental & Toxicological Degradation:**
  - When released into the troposphere, $\text{CCl}_4$ does not readily degrade; it diffuses into the stratosphere, where UV photolysis generates chlorine radicals ($\text{Cl}^\bullet$) that catalyze **ozone layer depletion**:
    

$$
\text{CCl}_4 \xrightarrow{h\nu} \text{CCl}_3^\bullet + \text{Cl}^\bullet
$$

    

$$
\text{Cl}^\bullet + \text{O}_3 \longrightarrow \text{ClO}^\bullet + \text{O}_2
$$

    

$$
\text{ClO}^\bullet + \text{O}^\bullet \longrightarrow \text{Cl}^\bullet + \text{O}_2
$$

  - Causes liver cancer (hepatotoxic), nerve damage, and kidney failure in humans.

***

### 3.5 Freons (Chlorofluorocarbons, CFCs)

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

- **Definition:** The chlorofluorocarbon compounds of methane and ethane are collectively known as **freons**.
- **Key Example — Freon-12 ($\text{CCl}_2\text{F}_2$, Dichlorodifluoromethane):**
  Synthesized industrially via the **Swarts reaction**:
  

$$
\text{CCl}_4 + 2\text{HF} \xrightarrow{\text{SbF}_5 / \text{SbCl}_5} \text{CCl}_2\text{F}_2 + 2\text{HCl}
$$

- **Characteristics:** Extremely stable, unreactive, non-corrosive, non-toxic, easily liquefiable gas.
- **Ozone Depletion Mechanism in Stratosphere:**
  Freon-12 diffuses unchanged into the stratosphere, where high-energy UV-C photolyzes the $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond:
  

$$
\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu\text{ (UV)}} \text{CF}_2\text{Cl}^\bullet + \text{Cl}^\bullet
$$

  A single chlorine free radical ($\text{Cl}^\bullet$) can catalytically destroy over $100{,}000$ ozone molecules via chain propagation before termination.

### 3.6 $p,p^\prime$-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT)

$$
\text{Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar
$$

```
               Cl                                 Cl
                \                                 /
                 \---\                       /---/
                 | O |                      | O |
                 /---/                       \---\
                   \                           /
                    \                         /
                     \------- CH ------- C -- Cl
                               |         |
                                         Cl
```

#### Complete Synthesis
Synthesized by condensing chlorobenzene with **chloral (trichloroethanal)** in a $2:1$ molar ratio in the presence of concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ as a dehydrating agent:

$$
\text{CCl}_3-\text{CHO} + 2\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4} \underset{\text{DDT}}{\left(p\text{-Cl}-\text{C}_6\text{H}_4\right)_2\text{CH}-\text{CCl}_3} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

#### IUPAC Name

$$
\boxed{\text{2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)-1,1,1-trichloroethane}}
$$

#### Environmental Profile
- **Biomagnification:** DDT is highly lipophilic (fat-soluble) and practically insoluble in water. It is not metabolized or excreted efficiently by organisms; instead, it deposits and concentrates in adipose (fatty) tissues.
- **Ecological Impact:** In birds, high DDT concentrations inhibit the enzyme **calcium carbonic anhydrase**, disrupting calcium transport and causing eggshells to thin prematurely, leading to severe population collapses (e.g., bald eagles, peregrine falcons).
- **Regulatory Status:** Banned in the USA in 1972 and internationally under the Stockholm Convention, with limited exemptions for malaria vector control.

## 4. High-Yield Examples & Solved 5-Year Board PYQs (2020–2025)

### Example 1: The Reactivity-Orientation Paradox
**[CBSE 2024, 2 Marks]**
> **Question:** Although chlorine is an electron-withdrawing group via its inductive effect, it directs incoming electrophiles to the *ortho*- and *para*-positions in electrophilic aromatic substitution reactions. Explain why.

**Model Answer:**
1. **Inductive vs. Resonance Competition:** Chlorine exerts two opposing effects:
   - A strong $-I$ (inductive) effect through the $\sigma$-bond, withdrawing electron density from the aromatic ring and deactivating it.
   - A $+R$ (resonance) effect through the delocalization of its lone pair electrons into the ring $\pi$-system.
2. **Positional Electronic Stabilization:** The $+R$ effect increases electron density specifically at the *ortho* and *para* positions relative to the *meta* positions.
3. **Resonance Stabilization of Intermediate:** When an electrophile attacks at the *ortho* or *para* position, the resulting arenium ion intermediate has four resonance structures, including one particularly stable structure where every atom has a complete octet:
   

$$
\left( -\overset{+}{\text{C}}-\overset{..}{\text{Cl}}: \longleftrightarrow -\text{C}=\overset{+}{\text{Cl}}: \right)
$$

   Attack at the *meta* position does not yield this octet-complete contributor (only 3 structures).
4. **Conclusion:** The $-I$ effect controls **reactivity** (deactivating the ring), whereas the $+R$ effect controls **orientation** (*ortho/para*-directing).

### Example 2: Mechanism of Chloroform Inactivation
**[CBSE 2023, 2 Marks]**
> **Question:** 
> (a) Write the balanced chemical equation for the reaction that occurs when chloroform is exposed to air and sunlight.  
> (b) Why is $1\%$ ethanol added to commercial chloroform bottles?

**Model Answer:**
(a) Chloroform is oxidized by oxygen in the presence of sunlight to form toxic phosgene:

$$
2\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\text{COCl}_2\text{ (carbonyl chloride)} + 2\text{HCl}
$$

(b) Ethanol serves two purposes:
1. It acts as an inhibitor, retarding the oxidation of chloroform.
2. It converts any toxic phosgene formed into non-toxic diethyl carbonate:

$$
\text{COCl}_2 + 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \longrightarrow \underset{\text{Diethyl carbonate}}{(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{O})_2\text{C}=\text{O}} + 2\text{HCl}
$$

### Example 4: Physical Properties of Dichlorobenzene Isomers
**[CBSE 2021 Compartment / Sample Question Bank, 2 Marks]**
> **Question:** Out of *o*-dichlorobenzene, *m*-dichlorobenzene, and *p*-dichlorobenzene, which isomer has the highest melting point and why?

**Model Answer:**
- **Highest Melting Point:** **$p$-Dichlorobenzene** (Melting point: $p$-isomer $\approx 325\text{ K} > o$-isomer $\approx 256\text{ K} > m$-isomer $\approx 248\text{ K}$).
- **Reasoning:**
  1. The $p$-dichlorobenzene molecule possesses a center of inversion, making it **symmetrical**.
  2. Due to this symmetry, molecules of the *para*-isomer pack more closely and uniformly into the crystal lattice.
  3. Greater packing efficiency maximizes intermolecular lattice forces, requiring more thermal energy to break the lattice during melting.

### Example 5: Environmental and Antiseptic Action of Polyhalogens
**[CBSE 2020, 3 Marks]**
> **Question:** Give reasons:
> 1. Iodoform exhibits antiseptic properties, but pure $\text{CHI}_3$ itself is not the active species.
> 2. DDT is banned in several countries.
> 3. Dichloromethane reduces coordination in human operators.

**Model Answer:**
1. $\text{CHI}_3$ has no inherent antiseptic action; its antiseptic effect is due to **slow decomposition to release elemental iodine ($\text{I}_2$)**, which oxidizes bacterial proteins.
2. DDT is chemically stable and fat-soluble, causing it to undergo **biomagnification** across trophic chains. It thins eggshells in birds and disrupts reproductive physiology, leading to environmental bans.
3. Dichloromethane depresses the **central nervous system (CNS)** and is metabolized in the liver to **carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$)**, which binds with hemoglobin to form carboxyhemoglobin, impairing oxygen delivery to brain tissues.

### Q23. [CBSE 2024 Board Examination, 4 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]
**Passage:**  
Nucleophilic substitution reactions are classified into two broad kinetic categories: $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ and $\text{S}_\text{N}2$. The $\text{S}_\text{N}2$ mechanism proceeds through a single concerted bimolecular transition state, characterized by backside nucleophilic attack, inversion of configuration (Walden Inversion), and absence of rearrangements. In contrast, the $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ reaction is a two-step unimolecular process where the leaving group departs to generate a planar, $\text{sp}^2$-hybridized intermediate carbocation, which can be attacked by a nucleophile from either face with equal probability, leading to racemisation. The solvent's polarity, leaving group ability, steric congestion around the electrophilic carbon, and nucleophile strength dictate the operative mechanism.

Answer the following questions:  
**(a)** Why does optically active 2-bromobutane yield an optically inactive product mixture when treated with aqueous acetone? `[1 Mark]`  
**(b)** Arrange the isomeric bromobutanes in increasing order of reactivity towards $\text{S}_\text{N}2$ substitution. `[1 Mark]`  
**(c)** [Attempt either sub-part (i) or (ii)]:  
*(i)* Optically active compound (X) with molecular formula $\text{C}_7\text{H}_{15}\text{Br}$ reacts with aqueous alkali by $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ pathway to give an optically inactive alcohol. Draw the structure of (X) and account for the loss of optical activity. `[2 Marks]`  
**OR**  
*(ii)* Allyl chloride ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}$) undergoes hydrolysis much faster than $n$-propyl chloride ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}$) under $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ conditions. Justify using canonical structures. `[2 Marks]`

***

**Model Answer / Marking Scheme:**  
- **(a)** 2-Bromobutane undergoes solvolysis via an $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ mechanism. The intermediate formed is a planar, achiral sec-butyl carbocation $\text{CH}_3\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{HCH}_2\text{CH}_3$. Water molecules attack from either the front face or back face with equal probability, forming an equimolar enantiomeric mixture ($50\%\; (R)\text{-butan-2-ol}$ and $50\%\; (S)\text{-butan-2-ol}$). This racemic mixture is optically inactive due to external compensation. `[1 Mark]`  
- **(b)** $\text{S}_\text{N}2$ reactivity depends inversely on steric congestion around the $\alpha$-carbon:

$$
(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr} < (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CHCH}_2\text{Br} \approx \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH(Br)CH}_3 < \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Br}
$$

Strict Order:

$$
3^\circ < 2^\circ < 1^\circ \text{ (isobutyl)} < 1^\circ \text{ (n-butyl)}
$$

$$
\text{tert-butyl bromide} < \text{sec-butyl bromide} < \text{isobutyl bromide} < n\text{-butyl bromide}
$$

`[1 Mark]`  
- **(c) (i)**  
  Structure of (X): 3-Bromo-3-methylhexane or an asymmetric tertiary alkyl halide:
  

$$
\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}_2-\overset{\overset{\text{CH}_3}{\displaystyle|}}{\underset{\underset{\text{Br}}{\displaystyle|}}{\text{C}}}-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_3 \quad \text{or} \quad \text{CH}_3-\overset{\overset{\text{C}_2\text{H}_5}{\displaystyle|}}{\underset{\underset{\text{Br}}{\displaystyle|}}{\text{C}}}-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_3
$$

  *(Notice $\text{C}^*$ has 4 distinct groups: $-\text{CH}_3$, $-\text{C}_2\text{H}_5$, $-\text{C}_3\text{H}_7$, and $-\text{Br}$.)* `[1 Mark]`  
  *Reason for loss of optical activity:* The departure of $\text{Br}^-$ produces a planar, $\text{sp}^2$-hybridised carbocation intermediate with a plane of symmetry. Attack of $\text{OH}^-$ from either face yields an equimolar racemic mixture of enantiomers, destroying optical activity. `[1 Mark]`  
  **OR**  
  **(c) (ii)**  
  Ionization of allyl chloride gives an allylic carbocation that is resonance-stabilized:
  

$$
\big[\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2 \longleftrightarrow \overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2-\text{CH}=\text{CH}_2\big]
$$

  `[1 Mark]`  
  In contrast, ionization of $n$-propyl chloride produces an unstabilized primary carbocation ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2$), which has much higher activation energy. Hence, allyl chloride hydrolyzes significantly faster. `[1 Mark]`

  **$\text{S}_\text{N}2$ Mechanism Pathway:**

\text{HO}^- + \text{CH}_3-\text{CH(Cl)}-\text{C}_2\text{H}_5 \longrightarrow \left[ \text{HO}\cdots\overset{\delta-}{\text{C}}(\text{H})(\text{CH}_3)(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5)\cdots\overset{\delta-}{\text{Cl}} \right]^\ddagger

## 1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

### Mechanism Determination Flowchart (3-Second Rule)
To avoid confusion between $\text{S}_\text{N}1$, $\text{S}_\text{N}2$, $\text{E}1$, and $\text{E}2$:

$$
\text{Substrate} \longrightarrow \begin{cases}
1^\circ \text{ Alkyl Halide} & \xrightarrow{\text{Strong Nucleophile (e.g., } \text{OH}^-, \text{CN}^-)} \mathbf{S_N2} \text{ (Inversion)} \\
1^\circ \text{ Alkyl Halide} & \xrightarrow{\text{Bulky Base (e.g., } t\text{-BuO}^- \text{ or alc. KOH + }\Delta)} \mathbf{E2} \text{ (Saytzeff/Hofmann)} \\
2^\circ \text{ Alkyl Halide} & \xrightarrow{\text{Polar Aprotic (DMSO, Acetone) / Strong Nu}^-} \mathbf{S_N2} \\
2^\circ \text{ Alkyl Halide} & \xrightarrow{\text{Polar Protic (}\text{H}_2\text{O, EtOH)} \text{ / Weak Nu}} \mathbf{S_N1} \\
3^\circ \text{ Alkyl Halide} & \xrightarrow{\text{Nucleophile in Protic medium (}\text{H}_2\text{O, MeOH)}} \mathbf{S_N1} \text{ (Racemisation)} \\
3^\circ \text{ Alkyl Halide} & \xrightarrow{\text{Base + Heat / alc. KOH}} \mathbf{E2/E1} \text{ (Alkene, Saytzeff preferred)}
\end{cases}
$$

* **Steric vs Electronic Dominance:**
  * $\text{S}_\text{N}2$ rate: $\text{CH}_3\text{X} > 1^\circ > 2^\circ \gg 3^\circ$ (Driven entirely by steric hindrance).
  * $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ rate: $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \text{CH}_3\text{X}$ (Driven entirely by carbocation stability).
  * **Resonance override:** Allylic ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\text{CH}_2\text{X}$) and benzylic ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_2\text{X}$) substrates undergo **both** $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ (resonance-stabilised carbocation) and $\text{S}_\text{N}2$ ($1^\circ$ sterics) at exceptionally fast rates.

### Ambident Nucleophile Rule
* **$\text{KCN}$ vs $\text{AgCN}$:**
  * $\text{KCN}$ is predominantly **ionic** $\implies \text{C}$ is nucleophilic $\implies \mathbf{R-CN}$ (Alkyl cyanide/Nitrile).
  * $\text{AgCN}$ is predominantly **covalent** $\implies \text{lone pair on N}$ attacks $\implies \mathbf{R-NC}$ (Isocyanide/Carbylamine).
* **$\text{KNO}_2$ vs $\text{AgNO}_2$:**
  * $\text{KNO}_2$ is **ionic** ($\text{K}^+ \, ^-\text{O}-\text{N}=\text{O}$) $\implies \text{O}$ attacks $\implies \mathbf{R-ONO}$ (Alkyl nitrite).
  * $\text{AgNO}_2$ is **covalent** ($\text{Ag}-\text{O}-\text{N}=\text{O}$) $\implies \text{N}$ attacks $\implies \mathbf{R-NO}_2$ (Nitroalkane).

### Elimination Regiochemistry (Saytzeff vs Hofmann)
* **Saytzeff Rule:** In dehydrohalogenation, base abstracts $\beta\text{-H}$ from the carbon bearing fewer hydrogens to yield the **more substituted, thermodynamically more stable alkene** (major).
* **Hofmann Exception:** Use of sterically hindered base (e.g., potassium $t$-butoxide) or a poor leaving group ($-\text{F}$) yields the **less substituted alkene** as the major product.

## 2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

Examiners strictly adhere to the official CBSE Marking Scheme. Below are exact areas where deductions occur:

### 1. Missing Reagents and Conditions (0.5 to 1 Mark Loss)
* **Finkelstein Reaction:** Writing only $\text{NaI}$ without specifying **dry acetone** leads to a $1/2$ mark deduction. (Acetone drives equilibrium forward via precipitation of $\text{NaCl}/\text{NaBr}$).
* **Swarts Reaction:** Writing generic fluorinating conditions instead of specific heavy metal fluorides ($\text{AgF}$, $\text{Hg}_2\text{F}_2$, $\text{CoF}_3$, or $\text{SbF}_3$).
* **Free-radical halogenation:** Writing $\text{Cl}_2$ without $\mathbf{h\nu}$ / **sunlight** or **heat** loses the entire mark for the step.
* **Aromatic electrophilic substitution:** Omitting anhydrous Lewis acid catalysts like **anh. $\text{AlCl}_3$** or **anh. $\text{FeCl}_3$**.

### 2. Ambiguity in Explaining Low Reactivity of Haloarenes (Frequent 2-3 Mark Question)
Students often write one generic point. CBSE marking schemes mandate at least **three distinct points** with diagrams/reasons for full credit:
1. **Resonance Effect:** Lone pair of halogen conjugates with the ring, imparting **partial double bond character** to the $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond (requires drawing canonical structures).
2. **Hybridisation of Carbon:** In haloarenes, carbon is $\mathbf{sp^2}$ hybridised (greater $s$-character, shorter bond length of $169\text{ pm}$, held more tightly) vs $\mathbf{sp^3}$ carbon in haloalkanes ($177\text{ pm}$).
3. **Instability of Phenyl Cation:** Self-ionisation to form a phenyl cation is not stabilised by resonance.
4. **Electronic Repulsion:** Electron-rich nucleophile faces repulsion from the electron-rich $\pi$-electron cloud of the benzene ring.

### 3. Stereochemical Inaccuracies ($\text{S}_\text{N}1$ vs $\text{S}_\text{N}2$)
* Stating that $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ gives "100% racemic mixture".  
  * *Correction for Full Marks:* Mention that $\text{S}_\text{N}1$ proceeds with **racemisation accompanied by slight inversion** due to the leaving group shielding the front side immediately after ionisation.
* For $\text{S}_\text{N}2$, failing to write **"complete inversion of configuration"** (Walden inversion) or not showing the planar pentacoordinate transition state in mechanism questions.

### 4. Grignard Reagent Handling Pitfalls
* Forgetting to write **"dry ether"** over the arrow:
  

$$
\text{R}-\text{X} + \text{Mg} \xrightarrow{dry ether} \text{RMgX}
$$

* Writing water in the preparation step destroys the reagent:
  

$$
\text{RMgX} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{R}-\text{H} + \text{Mg(OH)X}
$$

  *(Examiner trap: Explaining why Grignard reagents must be prepared under anhydrous conditions).*

## 3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT In-Text Lines & High-Yield Truths)

1. **Dipole Moment Anomaly:**
   

$$
\mathbf{\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} > \text{CH}_3\text{F} > \text{CH}_3\text{Br} > \text{CH}_3\text{I}}
$$

   *Reason:* Even though fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine, the $\text{C}-\text{Cl}$ bond length is significantly longer than $\text{C}-\text{F}$. Since $\mu = q \times d$, the larger bond length ($d$) in $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$ overcompensates for the slight difference in charge separation ($q$).

2. **Melting Point of Dichlorobenzene Isomers:**
   

$$
\text{Melting Point: } \mathbf{\text{para} > \text{ortho} > \text{meta}}
$$

   *Reason:* The $p$-isomer is symmetrical and packs closely in the crystal lattice, leading to stronger intermolecular forces and a significantly higher melting point compared to $o$- and $m$-isomers.
   *Boiling Point:* Nearly identical across isomers ($o \approx p > m$).

3. **Boiling Point Trends of Alkyl Halides:**
   * For the same alkyl group: $\mathbf{\text{R}-\text{I} > \text{R}-\text{Br} > \text{R}-\text{Cl} > \text{R}-\text{F}}$ (Due to increasing molecular mass, size, and London dispersion forces).
   * For isomeric alkyl halides: $\mathbf{\text{Primary} > \text{Secondary} > \text{Tertiary}}$ (Branching decreases surface area $\implies$ reduces van der Waals forces $\implies$ lowers boiling point).

4. **Chloroform Storage & Phosgene Trap:**
   * Chloroform ($\text{CHCl}_3$) slowly oxidises in air and light to form the poisonous gas **phosgene** ($\text{COCl}_2$):
     

$$
2\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{light}} 2\text{COCl}_2 + 2\text{HCl}
$$

   * *Prevention:* Stored in dark, amber-coloured bottles filled to the brim. Addition of **1% ethanol** acts as a negative catalyst and converts any formed phosgene into harmless diethyl carbonate:
     

$$
\text{COCl}_2 + 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \longrightarrow (\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{O})_2\text{C}=\text{O} + 2\text{HCl}
$$

5. **Reactivity towards $\text{AgNO}_3$ Test:**
   * Haloarenes and vinyl halides do **not** give a precipitate of $\text{AgX}$ with alcoholic $\text{AgNO}_3$ at room temperature (partial double-bond character inhibits ionisation).
   * Allylic, benzylic, and $3^\circ$ alkyl halides form precipitates instantaneously.

### 2. Named Reactions Master Checklist

* **Finkelstein Reaction:**
  

$$
\text{R}-\text{X} + \text{NaI} \xrightarrow{dry acetone} \text{R}-\text{I} + \text{NaX} \downarrow \quad (\text{X} = \text{Cl, Br})
$$

* **Swarts Reaction:**
  

$$
\text{R}-\text{X} + \text{AgF} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{R}-\text{F} + \text{AgX} \downarrow \quad (\text{also using } \text{Hg}_2\text{F}_2, \text{CoF}_3, \text{SbF}_3)
$$

* **Darzens Halogenation (Best method for pure alkyl chlorides):**
  

$$
\text{R}-\text{OH} + \text{SOCl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{pyridine}} \text{R}-\text{Cl} + \text{SO}_2 \uparrow + \text{HCl} \uparrow
$$

  *(By-products $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{HCl}$ are gaseous, leaving behind pure alkyl halide).*

* **Sandmeyer Reaction:**
  

$$
\text{Ar}-\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cu}_2\text{Cl}_2 / \text{HCl}} \text{Ar}-\text{Cl} + \text{N}_2 \uparrow
$$

  

$$
\text{Ar}-\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cu}_2\text{Br}_2 / \text{HBr}} \text{Ar}-\text{Br} + \text{N}_2 \uparrow
$$

* **Gattermann Reaction:**
  

$$
\text{Ar}-\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- \xrightarrow{\text{Cu powder / HCl}} \text{Ar}-\text{Cl} + \text{N}_2 \uparrow + \text{CuCl}
$$

* **Wurtz Reaction:**
  

$$
2\text{R}-\text{X} + 2\text{Na} \xrightarrow{dry ether} \text{R}-\text{R} + 2\text{NaX}
$$

* **Fittig Reaction:**
  

$$
2\text{Ar}-\text{X} + 2\text{Na} \xrightarrow{dry ether} \text{Ar}-\text{Ar} \text{ (Diphenyl)} + 2\text{NaX}
$$

* **Wurtz-Fittig Reaction:**
  

$$
\text{Ar}-\text{X} + \text{R}-\text{X} + 2\text{Na} \xrightarrow{dry ether} \text{Ar}-\text{R} + 2\text{NaX}
$$

* **Dow's Process (Drastic conditions proving unreactivity of Chlorobenzene):**
  

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{NaOH} \xrightarrow[300\text{ atm}]{623\text{ K}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}^-\text{Na}^+ \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}
$$

  *(Rate accelerates sharply when electron-withdrawing groups like $-\text{NO}_2$ are present at $o$- and $p$-positions).*

* **Hunsdiecker Reaction:**
  

$$
\text{R}-\text{COOAg} + \text{Br}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{CCl}_4, \, \Delta} \text{R}-\text{Br} + \text{CO}_2 \uparrow + \text{AgBr} \downarrow
$$
